# Related Work

## Medication Work in Family Care Networks

Adherence technology has treated medication as one person's memory problem, but the discipline's own empirical record resists that characterisation. Reminder applications optimise for the notification, and not for the daily routine that notification is supposed to enter. Older adults consequently meet interfaces calibrated to another individual's eyesight and another person's schedule. For older adults managing multimorbidity the difficulty lies in reconciling contradictory clinical advice, and so recollection of a schedule is not the binding constraint [107, 35, 27]. And the strongest result in this literature argues against its own dominant design. Reminders sustained repetition, and yet they hindered the cue learning that produces automaticity. And event-based cues raised that automaticity instead [108]. We read reminding, therefore, as at once the field's standard remedy and, on its own measurement, an obstacle to the habit that remedy exists to establish. Because every one of these adherence technologies addresses a single individual, and so each leaves the surrounding household outside the design.

Medication management is produced in practice rather than described from outside it, and so compliance is the wrong object to design for [9, 61]. That household has subsequently entered the identical literature as an empirical finding, yet it has not entered it as a design assumption. Chronic disease management is characterised as a set of interrelated collective family routines, and within those routines adolescents and their parents produce self-management jointly long before any formal transition. Even sleep emerges as household coordination and not as private behaviour, and self-care is collaborative in its ordinary operation. And where habit formation is the wrong objective, the published alternative is a conversational check-in that compensates as one person's capability changes, and that check-in never decides whom else to inform [92, 120, 102, 74; cite: Nunes and Fitzpatrick 2015 on self-care technologies and collaboration]. So the diagnosis relocated to the family, yet the prescription never followed it there.

Collective device use in South Asian households is an arrangement families actively operate, and not a deficiency measured against individual ownership. Sambasivan et al. identify three configurations that this intermediation takes, because one person supplies another's intent in proximate enabling, interprets the device's output in proximate translation, or performs both operations in surrogate usage [98]. And Ghosh extends that account of intermediation, and reports low-literate users who relinquish custody of an information resource deliberately, precisely so that the intermediation continues [33]. In Bangladesh, telephone sharing is expected and not merely tolerated, and so the resulting privacy strain runs inside relations of family obligation and not against strangers [3, 4, 2, 1]. And designs that begin from shared use fit these households better than designs beginning from one account per individual. And the relational closeness that makes intermediation workable is the same closeness that generates the privacy strain.

Where formal eldercare provision does not exist, the care network is not a preference but the entirety of the available care system. Population ageing is global, and functional ability rather than disease is the frame the World Health Organization proposes for it. Bangladesh is ageing rapidly with almost no residential or formal home-care infrastructure, and so formal services misfit the family arrangements that Bangladeshi carers already operate. Across the subcontinent, caregiver burden and difficulty adopting health technology recur as structural conditions and not as individual failings, and interventions addressed to the individual leave those conditions untouched [12, 5, 77, 90, 53, 37]. Yet the same reviews that document reciprocal filial obligation as the organising structure also report that obligation thinning under migration and changing household composition [51]. And so the arrangement we build upon is the arrangement already under strain.

What this literature cannot settle is what happens when the artifact stops waiting. We note that care theory has argued for two decades that choice-centred framings misread the tinkering care actually involves, whether the alternative is located in practice or in political obligation [80, 112]. And either way, the artifact in every study above is operated, and an operated artifact never has to decide whose share of the work it takes over. That is precisely the decision a household agent makes, and it reaches that determination inside a division of labour that already exists and already carries moral significance. We therefore begin by asking how Bangladeshi care networks distributed, claimed, and morally accounted for medication work before any agent arrived. An agent joining a functioning arrangement can only be interpreted against the arrangement it joins.

## Caregiver-Facing Systems and Their Limits

Confronted with distributed care, HCI has made that care legible, and it has rendered that care legible to the relatives surrounding the older adult. The digital family portrait visualised a parent's daily activity for extended family, and that visualisation sustained its value across a year-long deployment. From the same period the term care network enters the field as a design target that is the network and not the elder [82, 94, 22]. Subsequent research characterised caregiving as continual role-switching, and a substantial proportion of that caregiving labour remains invisible. The same literature reconceived caregiver support through community health equity, and it carried the coordination problem outward from households into clinical teams [47, 36, 81, 119]. That coordination logic, however, works against itself at scale. As caregivers enlarge the informal care network, remaining informed becomes progressively harder [118].

Rendering care visible is an intervention in the care, and not a neutral report upon it. Visibility is produced by indicators and by audiences, and so representing invisible work reconfigures the work itself. And telemonitoring research finds patients acquiring new invisible labour, since they become participants in their own diagnosis [106, 85, 8]. And privacy in these settings is cooperatively produced and not individually held, so caregivers and older adults perform it as articulation work and do not configure it as a preference [23, 87]. Yamashita et al. document what such an intervention costs to operate. Sharing caregiving journals incorporated care recipients into the record, and those journals initiated conversations about difficult subjects between relatives. Yet the authors attribute that reception partly to caregivers censoring what they wrote, since a thoughtless entry would have been received differently [115]. And somebody has to administer whatever becomes visible, and throughout these caregiver-facing platforms that somebody is a person, not the software.

The individual a care system reports upon is the individual it is least likely to have consulted. Onboarding discourse positioned care recipients as collaborators while placing them in a more dependent position, and that identical discourse simultaneously pressured caregivers into absorbing additional responsibility. Older adults perform relational work toward telehealth companionship systems, and yet those systems were never built to receive that work [24, 65]. And among couples managing cognitive decline, a stated commitment to shared decision-making gave way to caregivers acting unilaterally, since no safeguarding option existed between inaction and deciding on another person's behalf [76]. In an early-warning dashboard, the students the system profiled were largely unaware of its existence, and its stakeholders disagreed about whether those students warranted notification [111]. Where care is planned jointly the care recipient's agency is present but structurally under-represented. And the field's own reviews trace that under-representation to sampling rather than to accident [56, 41].

And the same instrument can also strengthen the hand of the person it monitors, which is why the surveillance reading cannot simply be assumed. Adults self-tracking atrial fibrillation externalised bodily awareness to what they called a digitised heart, and they deployed that record to negotiate within shared decision-making rather than to acquiesce to it [54]. Clinical research reaches a compatible position from another direction, because it treats a third party in a health decision as unremarkable. The TRIO framework maps caregiver involvement and influence across the successive stages of a treatment decision, and it treats that involvement as something clinicians accommodate and not something they circumvent. The wider triadic literature has measured companions' roles and their consequences for the clinical consultation for decades [63; cite: systematic review of triadic medical consultations; meta-analytic review of family presence in routine visits]. We read the two literatures against each other, and one act carries opposite defaults. And a relative who participates in a treatment decision is a resource in one discipline, whereas the other interprets that same act as an encroachment upon independence.

So this division of labour is what the literature leaves unexamined. Every platform reviewed above settles what gets rendered and leaves who gets told to a person, and that division holds while the system only reports. That division collapses the moment the system itself selects the recipient, because responsibility for the choice becomes attributable to the system and contestable by the surrounding household. Prior work consequently offers no account of the older adult's standing at the moment of disclosure, and no methodology for recovering that standing. A study that consults one side of an arrangement reports only what that particular side perceived. Yet where both accounts of a single arrangement have been collected directly, they diverge in patterned ways [cite: gerontological study measuring disagreement between older adults and their adult children over in-home sensors]. And neither discipline settles what such involvement costs the older adult's standing, and neither has constructed a system that holds the disclosure decision itself. We therefore interviewed the older adult and the family caregiver separately about the same two weeks in the same household, so that the standing question receives two independent answers rather than one. Dashboards report, whereas an agent must decide whom to tell and when.

## Autonomous Assistants and Whom They Serve

Assistants that act, and not merely report, already occupy homes and clinics, and each of them is addressed to one person. A conversational voice assistant mediates communication between older adults and healthcare providers. And a conversational check-in supports medication management for older adults living with mild cognitive impairment. In a three-week deployment, voice assistants opened computing accessibility to older adults whom mobile and desktop interfaces had excluded [117, 74, 89]. Throughout these deployed systems the beneficiary and the addressee are presumed identical. That presumption is a design position and not an oversight, and its consequences materialise at the moment it fails.

Alignment research holds the identical assumption explicitly, and it has begun to articulate the limit of that assumption. Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning formalises alignment as a game between an agent and a single human. And Gabriel then separates what such an agent might be aligned to, because instructions, intentions, revealed preferences, interests, and values are materially different targets [38, 32]. And once the principal becomes plural, misalignment arises along interacting axes, so the question becomes aligned enough, for whom, and at what cost. And preferences misrepresent values as soon as normative roles are involved. And pluralistic alignment now identifies an alignment design space with distinct targets, and a technical response in composition across models [62, 121, 105, 29]. So the conceptual literature has named the plural principal, although the machinery beneath it still resolves to one human. We position this work against that gap, because aggregating across a plural principal treats a family as a distribution to be summed and not as a relation to be negotiated.

And how far an agent should defer remains unsettled inside this same literature. Milli et al. establish analytically that with an imperfectly rational human, an agent inferring underlying preferences outperforms an agent following literal instructions, and so obedience trades against the value the agent delivers. And revocability follows the identical logic, because it must be engineered against an agent's own incentives and not granted as a courtesy [79, 39]. But contestability arrives at nearly the opposite instruction. And that instruction holds that responsiveness to human intervention belongs across the lifecycle and not appended to its end, and developers supervising agents in practice improvise heuristics instead of following procedure [6, 25]. So a veto retained by an older adult is pulled by both arguments simultaneously. Yet neither argument was formulated for a veto held by one person over a disclosure concerning them to somebody else.

Initiative diminishes what a person must do, and it multiplies what that person must interpret. Mixed-initiative interfaces coupled automation to direct manipulation, and the agent's confidence governed when it acted, while generative proactivity now outperforms user-initiated conversation on sustained engagement [45, 70]. Yet when an agent acts unprompted, people speculate about why it was designed to act, and those speculations determine the explanations and controls they subsequently request. Disclosing a plan before executing it shifts both trust and team performance [52, 42]. And trust in an automated system should in any case be calibrated to demonstrated capability, and both over-reliance and under-reliance count as failures of that calibration [67]. Patients asked how much decision power an AI should hold answer differently by person and by context, and their answers move over time [55]. So the two results pull apart. Initiative is valuable to the person because they no longer have to originate the interaction, and costly because that person must subsequently reconstruct why it originated.

Households have never been single-user environments, and computing's answer has been to subdivide the account and not the allegiance. And multi-user homes operate interfaces constructed for a single account holder where several occupants hold standing, and so the technical response has been finer-grained access control with automated negotiation between users' policies [cite: CHI study of interactions in multi-user smart homes; multi-user access control for shared smart homes]. Interaction research, however, points the other way, because domestic voice interfaces are used as multi-party conversational resources and not as one-to-one assistants, and conversational breakdowns within families are social as much as technical [cite: ethnography of voice interfaces in everyday life; study of communication breakdowns between families and a voice assistant]. But individualising the account and accommodating the collective practice are not equivalent remedies. Such architectures arbitrate permissions between occupants, and none maintains an allegiance a household can inspect, relocate, or retract.

Where use is already intermediated, the person operating a system and the person the system concerns are routinely different people. Sambasivan et al. and Ghosh describe that separation and name its configurations, and who may legitimately operate a household device is determined by household authority and not by individual preference [98, 33, 110]. And systems that decide rather than inform have been fielded in precisely such intermediated settings. Rural clinicians in China reported an AI clinical decision-support system fundamentally misaligned with their local context and workflow, and they doubted its transparency. Yet all of those clinicians still endorsed an assistant that worked alongside them. And so the clinicians' objection concerned whose work the system accommodated, and not automation itself. Explainability research in the Global South remains thin against the deployments it would have to serve, and community innovations there fail for structural and not design reasons. Global South stakeholders hold under-recognised roles in the governance of these systems, and design frameworks translate badly without historical and structural analysis [114, 84, 96, 88, 49, 26].

So the four literatures we have reviewed stop at the same limitation. Chronic-illness research established that medication work is collective in these households, and the literature continued building for one individual. And caregiver-facing systems made care legible, but they left the disclosure decision to a human. Alignment named the plural principal but holds no situated case of one, and multi-user computing arbitrates access and not allegiance. Each contributes a portion of the vocabulary, and none contributes the situated case. That missing case is not a curiosity. An agent escalating on its own opens a responsibility gap that is really four problems, of culpability, moral accountability, public accountability, and active responsibility [99]. And none of those four can be assigned without knowing whom the agent was working for. To our knowledge, no empirical account exists of a household assigning, contesting, and revoking an autonomous agent's allegiance in daily life. And we locate this study at that stopping point. We deployed an agent that announced in Bangla whom it was currently serving, and it asked the older adult before involving a family member. We ran it in Bangladeshi households where an older adult and a family caregiver each gave their own account of the same two weeks.

## Contestable Design and the Cost of Visibility

So whether relinquishing control to family costs an older adult their standing, or expresses it, remains unsettled. Caregiver-facing systems tend to settle that question on the older adult's behalf, because onboarding language recasts dependence as independence, and because no option exists between inaction and deciding for somebody [24, 76]. Disability and ageing scholarship supplies the opposing answer to that question about standing, and it supplies that answer from experience. Access is collaboratively produced and not individually achieved. And autonomy is socially constituted, so relinquishing control can itself constitute an exercise of autonomy. People who depend upon assistance describe independence as fluid and continually renegotiated, and they achieve it through social dependency and not against it [13, 30, 72, 101, 78]. And adoption in South Asia turns on agency and social position rather than on the usability of the artifact. Refusal belongs on the same relational account, because non-use is a reasoned position and not a failure of comprehension, and distrust is a bid for accountability [86, 58, 11, 48, 57]. Yet the autonomy-preservation tradition that the critical turn documents predicts the opposite of all of it, and conflating age with impairment is the mechanism that produces the prediction [66, 59, 64].

And every instrument that would render an agent's allegiance contestable also renders it visible, and visible assistance is what this population pays for. And contestability requires that a decision be legible before anybody can dispute it, and seamful design extends that requirement, because it asks that the seam be exhibited and not smoothed over [6, 7, 28]. That stigma evidence, however, runs in the opposite direction. And older adults reject visible assistance that signals decline, and they deliberately select assistive devices that preserve face, since visible assistance jeopardises social standing. Among cochlear implant users, perceived visibility is associated with self-stigma and social interaction anxiety, and deficit-framed domestic technology reifies the dependency it names [17, 69, 20, 73]. Coghlan et al. already propose dignity, autonomy, and style of company as the dimensions older adults weigh when reasoning about a domestic agent [21]. We take that account as the one any further set of dimensions must be argued against and not placed beside. So a mechanism must be visible to be contestable, yet it is penalised for being visible.

We find that the consent instruments which would govern such a mechanism exist in every domain except care. And individual one-time consent breaks down once data and decisions are distributed across related people, and so distributed models have been proposed in its place [71]. A feminist reading supplies the vocabulary of grants that are freely given, reversible, informed, specific, and ongoing, and games demonstrate consent operating as an interaction mechanism and not as a dialogue box. Household energy data elicits usefulness and surveillance readings from the same people. Privacy configurations on shared sensing devices have been scaffolded as explicit negotiation, and one-click consent misfits older adults whose boundaries continually shift [109, 83, 104, 122, 50]. And feminist HCI warrants treating advocacy and pluralism as design qualities rather than as paternalism, while care ethics has begun to reach design practice as a working instrument [10, 43]. Seymour et al. add the warning that matters most for a spoken announcement. They argue that voice-forward consent is more usable yet less meaningful, since requesting consent in passing undermines the comprehension consent depends upon [100]. But a ceremony repeated often enough to qualify as ongoing is a ceremony that people stop attending to.

We situate scores and streaks here and not within a motivational account. Game elements are defined inconsistently across the discipline, and nudging mechanisms carry thinner evidence than their circulation suggests, so any project employing either owes its reader a specific mechanism rather than a categorical label [40, 18]. And shared family metrics trigger interaction between relatives, and a broken streak carries relational significance and genuine loss for the people maintaining it [68, 97, 46, 19, 91]. Self-determination theory explains why such a loss stings, through competence and relatedness rather than through reinforcement, and it has been applied to conversational agents specifically. That theory's own reviewers report, however, that it is frequently cited without doing analytical work [95, 116, 14, 113]. So we read a symbolic streak as a relational trigger and not as the behavioural lever the persuasive tradition proposed [31].

And what this leaves us is a design problem with every ingredient present and no instance assembled. Contestability presupposes legibility, and the stigma literature prices that legibility in social standing. And consent research supplies revocable and ongoing grants, and it derives them from energy, gaming, and social networking rather than from healthcare. And interdependence research argues that delegation may be an exercise of agency, the autonomy-preservation tradition predicts that it is a loss, and no empirical investigation we located adjudicates between them for an agent that changes sides. What none of this literature contains is a household in which those requirements converge simultaneously, where an agent's allegiance is announced, granted, refused, and reclaimed in front of the family it concerns. That configuration is what we report, and the justification for reporting it is that each of these literatures has already specified what such a configuration would test.
