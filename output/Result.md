# 4 Results

Across the two phases, medication care developed through repeated adjustments among people, routines, tools, and records. Phase 1 first located a narrow but consequential care gap: when routines moved, older adults could lose familiar cues while caregivers lacked evidence about what had happened. Following that gap outward showed how differentiated household roles, paper artifacts, alarms, and shared devices usually coordinated a dose, and why they sometimes failed to complete a handoff. These tensions became expectations for a bounded agent and informed what entered the deployment, although Phase 2 did not supply an effectiveness verdict. Instead, participants described where reminders, records, service announcements, and permission requests fitted care, where they introduced uncertainty, and what they expected to change next.

We organize this empirical arc in five connected subsections. The first two follow the four Phase 1 themes from differentiated medication work to bounded expectations for technology. The next two trace the five merged Phase 2 themes across routine support, control over family involvement, uncertain exceptions, progress traces, and community care. The final subsection brings these movements together as a descriptive care framework. Throughout, we distinguish deployed features from elicited or hypothetical mechanisms, and participant-reported changes from adherence, workload, or clinical outcomes that the study did not measure.

## 4.1 Care Became Fragile When Routines Moved

Medication care became most fragile when an ordinary routine changed. Travel removed familiar places and meal cues, work competed with dose time, and visitors or family programs interrupted an otherwise repeated sequence. Medicines could be left behind, supplies could run out, or changed packaging could unsettle visual recognition. P01 condensed this first Phase 1 theme, *Routine transitions expose weak coordination and uncertain verification*: “At home, everything is fixed. I know where the medicine is, I know the time. When travelling, everything changes” (P01, Phase 1). The gap appeared when time, place, medicine, and attention no longer met, not as a stable absence of competence.

When those cues receded, caregivers could become responsible for noticing without having evidence of what had happened. Several relatives might each expect another person to remember, everybody could become busy, or distance could prevent a check. C03 described the vigilance this uncertainty produced: “If nobody checks then I worry. So it is pressure” (C03, Phase 1). Older adults lived with the other side of that pressure, as P05 said: “Sometimes I get irritated also because she asks so much. But later I think okay she is helping” (P05, Phase 1). These were not paired household accounts, but together they located a care gap between the caregiver's need for reassurance and the older adult's experience of repeated questioning.

That care gap exposed the differentiated work normally holding a dose together. The Phase 1 theme *Medication work is distributed through differentiated household roles* separated taking, recognizing, interpreting, supplying, reminding, checking, and authorizing. Older adults often retained the medication action through familiar packages, fixed locations, meals, and repeated timing, even when another person held the medicine name or prescription. P08 stated, “Taking is easy. Knowing which medicine is little difficult” (P08, Phase 1). Written information could move to a relative without moving the whole medication task.

Authority was distributed just as specifically: relatives kept prescriptions, bought medicine, recalled clinical instructions, or helped interpret a change, and pharmacists read difficult handwriting and clarified unfamiliar labels. Doctors retained authority over treatment changes, which P06 summarized as, “No no. Change means doctor has to say” (P06, Phase 1). A spouse could remind and a son could read a prescription, but neither role became interchangeable with clinical authorization; collective care remained differentiated care.

The care gap also involved tools and devices because paper prescriptions, medicine strips, handwritten notes, fixed storage places, meals, and ordinary alarms carried parts of the routine. Some older adults used personal smartphones, and others relied on household smartphones shared with or operated by relatives; Phase 1 accounts also included button phones used mainly for calls. Across these devices, small text, multi-step interaction, and difficult documents often moved configuration or interpretation to somebody with greater device or literacy experience, so participation followed the task rather than device ownership.

In these Bangladesh accounts, the setting-specific tension lay between individual device operation and medication work enacted through shared devices, proxy use, collective decisions, and checking as care. P10 explained that “we don't live in isolation” and concluded, “Technology should complement that” (P10, Phase 1). Yet this arrangement did not describe every household in Bangladesh: several older adults managed through memory, prescriptions, packages, or notes and declined more help. P17 asserted that “my memory is very sharp” (P17, Phase 1). Interdependence and refusal both belonged within the care network.

Phase 1 therefore began with neither absent care nor settled care because working arrangements became uncertain when routine, availability, tools, or devices shifted. That fragility moves the story to a narrower question: why could existing tools signal a dose yet fail to complete the handoff between an older adult and a caregiver?

## 4.2 Existing Tools Signaled Time but Could Not Complete the Handoff

The uncertain handoff exposed why another alarm was insufficient. The third Phase 1 theme, *Existing aids fit tasks but miss household handoffs*, showed that notes and prescriptions organized complex schedules, fixed placement supported recognition, and an ordinary alarm already met some participants' needs. Yet an alarm could ring without naming the medicine, and a pillbox still required somebody to fill it, while small controls and multi-step interaction could transfer operation to a relative or lead an older adult to stop. Existing tools signaled, stored, or displayed one part of medication work, but they could not determine who should notice an unresolved dose, what that person should know, or whether family involvement was welcome.

That incomplete handoff led into the fourth theme, *Families expect technology to support bounded care network roles*, which converted those frustrations into conditions for an agent. Within this theme, participants evaluated prompts that named the medicine and timing, used short Bangla speech or recognizable visuals, and required few actions. Their expectations included a second opportunity before family involvement, visible rather than hidden checking, and access limited to the relative responsible for the exception. The same expectations allowed trusted kin to configure a schedule, people to verify it against the prescription, and clinicians to authorize medication changes. These were evaluations of proposed features, not Phase 1 experiences of an agent.

Table 1 follows each Phase 1 arrangement or frustration into a formative expectation, the response that entered the deployment, and the evidentiary boundary carried into Phase 2. The agent was therefore positioned as a bounded adjustment within an existing care network, not as a substitute for that network.

| Phase 1 arrangement or frustration | Formative expectation | Deployed response | Boundary carried into Phase 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Travel, work, visitors, or changed meals weakened familiar cues. | A semantic Bangla prompt identifies the medicine and time with few actions. | Scheduled reminders carried medicine name and timing. | Medicine images and selectable prompt modes were not deployed. |
| Small text, difficult documents, shared devices, and multi-step setup moved interaction to relatives. | Trusted kin can configure as the older adult remains able to hear, inspect, respond, and refuse. | Researchers configured schedules with the older adult or the relative operating the household device; onboarding included a spoken service announcement. | Prescription capture remained a concept, and proxy setup could still create hidden dependence. |
| Distance and simultaneous busyness made family checking uncertain and repetitive. | Give another opportunity, make the handoff visible, and involve family selectively. | After an unconfirmed dose, the agent requested permission before notifying family; the participant could decline. | Retry schedules, selected-recipient configuration, patterned silence, and risk-graded refusal were not deployed. |
| Routine care work remained quiet and difficult for family members to notice. | Phase 1 supplied no direct expectation about gamification. | A logbook, daily heat map, and symbolic streak represented recorded continuity. | Only Phase 2 could establish how participants interpreted these features; shared family scores were not deployed. |
| Stock depletion and difficult prescriptions interrupted action beyond timing. | Route supply and interpretation to accountable people. | Stock sensing and prescription capture were not implemented. | These remained future expectations and continued to require human verification. |

These boundaries specify what the agent could and could not do: it followed configured schedules, issued reminders, updated records after confirmation, and requested permission to involve family after an unconfirmed dose. Those configured actions did not include reading prescriptions, sensing stock, inferring the meaning of silence, or deciding medication changes. Phase 2 consequently examined a bounded phone-based agent situated among existing tools, not an autonomous clinical system.

The deployment placed the agent inside the previously uncertain handoff. The question therefore shifted from what families wanted in the abstract to what the agent could carry, what people still had to carry, and who directed the transition between them.

## 4.3 The Agent Carried Time, but Families Still Carried Care

The first merged Phase 2 theme, *The agent supports routines while people retain care*, returned to the transitions that had exposed the Phase 1 handoff. Older adults described the reminder as most useful when travel, fieldwork, gatherings, or altered schedules reduced access to household cues. P06 contrasted home and mobility: “At home my wife can tell me. In the field there is nobody asking” (P06, Phase 2). The agent carried a configured time into the field; it did not carry the wife's situated knowledge, the medicine, or the decision to take it. At home it joined meals and family reminders, placing timing beside existing supports rather than above them.

Some older adults also described anticipating the reminder around a familiar time, as P11 reflected: “Maybe it has reinforced the timing” (P11, Phase 2). Because this interpretation followed a leading prompt about memory and retraining, it remains an elicited account of felt timing rather than evidence of cognitive improvement; the reported change was narrower. A recurring reminder could become another cue inside an established routine, while memory, notes, prescriptions, and family reminders remained available as fallbacks.

This routine support depended on how interaction was divided across devices. A relative could enter a schedule or operate a shared phone as the older adult listened, recognized the medicine, confirmed, questioned, and took it. P05 described that sequence: “My daughter set it up for me. After that, the reminder part was easy” (P05, Phase 2). Another older adult distinguished help with configuration from somebody placing medicine in her hand. Proxy setup could preserve direct medication action when the older adult could inspect the prompt, reject an error, and reach a fallback, but it became a new dependency when every change required an unavailable relative.

Use also made device expectations more specific as participants associated short Bangla speech, large text, medicine names or pictures, and one clear confirmation action with direct response. The reminder and spoken announcement supplied speech and timing, but medicine images and selectable voice, vibration, or visual modes remained requests. Audible prompts could attract attention during work but expose medication information in company. Simplicity therefore meant neither one universal interface nor unlimited customization, but a small set of legible choices that did not create another operation task for the care network.

Once the agent could carry time, the second merged theme, *Older adults control when the agent involves family*, concerned who should receive that prompt and what happened next. Older adults expected the first opportunity to respond in self-managed routines, while a caregiver could be the appropriate first recipient when they administered medicine. C05 reported, “Mostly it reminded me, because I am the one managing their medicines” (C05, Phase 2). This countercase showed that age or device ownership did not determine direction. The first prompt followed the person already responsible for the next medication action.

Direction became more understandable when the agent announced whom it was serving, as P10 explained: “When it said it is working for me, I understood it better” (P10, Phase 2). That announced direction changed after a dose remained unconfirmed, when the deployed permission request marked a possible move from a personal reminder to family involvement. Participants described agreeing to that handoff in some instances and stopping or declining it in others. Those handoff accounts made the announcement and request perceptible, and refusal kept the shift revisable, although one account of feeling exposed around others showed that ceremony still carried a social cost.

Caregivers described a corresponding redistribution of attention. Regular dose times did not remain continuously active in memory, ordinary prompts reached the person taking or giving medicine, and some routine questions receded. Caregivers still interpreted context, checked prescriptions, managed supply, consulted clinicians, and corrected errors; their accounts describe less clock watching and more selective attention, not an objective reduction in workload or responsibility.

Participants thus described specific changes in coordination: a portable cue during disruption, direct response after delegated setup, visible direction, and caregiver attention focused more narrowly on exceptions. The study did not measure whether these changes improved adherence, health, or workload, and no log established their frequency. Each reported benefit also depended on a record that could not determine what had happened to the medicine.

The agent had carried timing and redirected attention, but its reminder, confirmation, and alert record now became a new object that the care network had to interpret.

## 4.4 The Agent Made Care Visible, but Not Certain

The new object of interpretation exposed the third merged Phase 2 theme, *Caregivers act on uncertain exceptions, not automatic judgments*. Medication action and device action could diverge when an older adult took a dose without confirming it, confirmed without establishing ingestion, or remained silent during work, travel, conversation, delay, or an unheard prompt. P05 described this lived record gap: “I had actually taken it, I just forgot the phone part” (P05, Phase 2). The agent made nonconfirmation visible, but the record could misrepresent the event that family members were being asked to address.

This mismatch distributed frustration in both directions: an older adult could be treated as having missed medicine already taken, while a false or unexplained alert could spread worry among caregivers and weaken attention to later alerts. Participants consequently treated nonconfirmation as a reason to ask rather than a verdict, with P14 stating that “the family should check instead of assuming” (P14, Phase 2). The agent could narrow routine checking while recreating vigilance around ambiguous exceptions.

That ambiguity produced a more specific expectation for the next iteration: distinguish a dose confirmed as taken, a dose reported as not taken, and a dose not yet confirmed. Participants wanted the agent to identify the medicine and reason for attention, allow time and correction, repeat before escalation, and contact one chosen relative only while the event remained unresolved. These conditions were elicited or hypothetical because the deployed agent did not interpret patterned silence, configure risk-graded refusal, or implement the full retry and selected-recipient sequence. The lived record gap established a need to preserve uncertainty; it did not establish that these proposed mechanisms would resolve it.

Verification provided the practical boundary around that uncertainty as older adults and caregivers compared prompts with prescriptions, medicine strips, notes, familiar routines, relatives, pharmacists, and doctors. P15 summarized this verification order: “The prescription is the authority for me” (P15, Phase 2). Repeated agreement could reduce checking for one stable schedule, but a new medicine, conflicting prompt, hidden change, or maintenance burden reopened verification. Participants could refuse suspect instructions, correct information, return to earlier tools, or stop using an agent that created more work than it removed, so reliance stayed local to a verified routine.

The fourth merged theme, *Streaks show care work, while medicine remains primary*, followed the same record problem into gamification. The symbolic streak made recorded continuity visible to older adults and relatives, while caregivers could see scheduling, prompting, confirming, and checking represented in one trace. Participants sometimes welcomed modest family recognition, yet the streak captured only actions recorded through the agent and could neither prove ingestion nor include every form of care. This visibility was relational but incomplete.

That incomplete visibility became consequential when a streak broke and P10 recalled, “I was a little upset because I had kept it for many days” (P10, Phase 2). His son's response, “okay, it happened, take the next one properly,” redirected attention from the lost record to continuing care (P10, Phase 2). This family response contained the metric's disappointment, while participants rejected the same feature when it became competition, blame, public sharing, constant checking, or another measure of family performance. Medicine remained primary; the streak's limited value depended on staying optional, quiet, recoverable, and visible only within chosen relationships.

The fifth theme, *Friends and community support care, while doctors guide medicine*, widened the question of who could belong to those relationships. Older adults described reminding spouses and peers, receiving reminders in return, and turning everyday health talk into questions. P06 said, “I also ask her about her medicine. So it is both ways” (P06, Phase 2). This reciprocity positioned older adults as contributors rather than only recipients of care, complicating a family-only account of collectivism.

Community involvement had its own boundary because peer conversation could cue remembering or make unfamiliar technology approachable while also adding conflicting information. P07 explained, “I listen, but I don't want the phone adding more confusion” (P07, Phase 2). Participants continued to place medicine decisions with doctors and pharmacists, and social conversation did not authorize automatic sharing of records or streaks. Most Phase 2 community mechanisms were elicited rather than experienced through the agent, which contained no community feature.

Across uncertainty, verification, gamification, and community, each representation made some care work visible while leaving other work unrecorded or newly exposed. More sensing or sharing did not resolve this tension by itself. The remaining task was to organize how families located the agent, tested its representation, moved its allegiance, and responded when that position no longer fitted.

## 4.5 A Care Framework for Moving the Agent's Allegiance

Across the two phases, the empirical arc moved from one distribution of work to another, not from human care to automated care, and then followed the checks and revisions that the new distribution required. We organize that movement as a care framework in which the agent occupies three revisable roles. As a **tool**, it carries a human-configured routine through reminders and records; as a **coach**, it makes represented continuity available for modest recognition. As an **advocate**, it proposes involving another person around an unresolved event, making these roles positions within the care network rather than levels of increasing autonomy.

Four dimensions describe how participants made each position accountable. The **direction** dimension identifies who receives the next prompt or task, and **visibility** identifies what another person can see and why the agent has brought it forward. **Revocability** identifies how people refuse, correct, withdraw, or return to earlier practices, and **ceremony** identifies how a role change becomes perceptible through the spoken service announcement and permission request. Together, the announcement and request-and-grant sequence formed the deployed portion of the **Affiliation Ledger**; patterned silence, risk-graded refusal, and other proposed mechanisms were not deployed.

Table 2 presents the framework as the adjustment cycle connecting both phases, beginning with existing human and material work rather than with a feature. Each later stage asks what the agent represents, who may act on that representation, and what remains unresolved after the handoff.

| Adjustment stage | Care-network question | Agent role and allegiance dimension | Empirical basis | Residual expectation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Locate existing work | Who takes, recognizes, configures, supplies, checks, interprets, and authorizes? | Tool; direction | Phase 1 differentiated roles, paper records, packages, alarms, personal and shared devices | A role map that does not equate the device owner, medicine taker, and caregiver. |
| Identify disruption | Which cue, person, artifact, supply, or record has stopped aligning? | Tool; visibility | Routine transitions, device barriers, document difficulty, and stock gaps | An unresolved coordination event rather than a memory verdict. |
| Propose a bounded handoff | Who receives attention next, what becomes visible, and why? | Advocate; direction, visibility, ceremony | Spoken allegiance and permissioned family notification | An explanation, time to respond, and one chosen responsible person. |
| Verify and contest | What evidence supports the reminder, record, or alert? | Tool or advocate; revocability | Checking against prescriptions, packages, routines, relatives, pharmacists, and doctors | Correction, clinical authority, fallback, and withdrawal remain available. |
| Recognize and respond | Does a progress trace acknowledge care or turn it into performance? | Coach; visibility, revocability | Streak recognition, incomplete records, disappointment, family response, and pressure | Progress remains optional, recoverable, and separate from evidence of ingestion. |

This cycle also locates the future AI-assisted agent described through participants' expectations. Rather than entering the household as the sole source of memory, medication truth, or authority, it remained positioned around bounded tasks: semantically clear reminders, routed supply or interpretation, exposed uncertainty, visible handoffs, and human verification. Prescription capture, stock sensing, selected escalation, and interpretation of silence belong to this future-facing edge because the deployment did not test them.

Continuous adjustment therefore describes more than incremental feature addition. A reminder that carried timing could reduce routine attention while creating a confirmation burden, and a family alert could focus care while spreading unnecessary worry. A streak could acknowledge quiet work while turning continuity into performance, just as a community feature could support reciprocity while widening confusion or disclosure. Each next expectation emerged from the tension produced by the preceding adjustment.

Families did not steadily move the agent from tool to coach to advocate, nor did they treat greater agent authority as improvement. They moved its allegiance according to the task, evidence, relationship, and social cost present at that moment because no reminder, record, or score settled who should act next. This movement closes the empirical arc: fragile coordination produced an expectation for bounded assistance; bounded assistance redistributed timing and attention; that redistribution exposed uncertainty; and uncertainty prompted another adjustment in the agent's role. Discussion returns this empirical movement to prior work and develops its implications for future agent design.
