# Phase 1, Caregiver: themes

Four themes from the eight Phase 1 caregiver transcripts (C01 to C08), read as one story in two pairs. Theme 1 is the family system and its failure; theme 3 is what caregivers want in its place. Theme 2 is the misfit of today's tools; theme 4 is what they want from AI instead. Each theme has a definition and ends with the consequence that leads onward. Each sub-theme has a paragraph on what it means. Codes and quotes are in `master-codebook.md`.

## 1. A medication system that lives in one caregiver's memory and breaks when they are away

**Definition.** An adult child or grandchild carries the household's medication work without anyone having decided it: they remind whoever is home, buy and read, hold the names and the paper, and filter outside advice, while the parent holds the act of taking and the look of the packet. Nothing is written down, so the system works only while the caregiver is present. At work, on a trip, or asleep, the parent's time is carried away by housework and guests, a phone yes may be false, and when everyone is busy at once a missed dose can end in hospital. The caregiver pays in standing worry and repeated calls.

### A caregiving role that arrived without a decision and cannot be handed back

Nobody chose who would manage the medicines. The role grew from need and settled on whoever was competent and present, and reminding stays shared by whoever is at home. Because it was never decided, it is never reviewed, and the caregiver has no one to pass it to when they are away.

### Knowledge split between the caregiver who knows the names and the parent who knows the packet, with the caregiver filtering outside advice

The caregiver holds the medicine names, the prescription, the buying, and the doctor's words; the parent holds the act of taking and recognises medicine by packet, colour, and place. The caregiver also screens the advice of relatives and neighbours, which feels tested when many agree, and keeps the doctor as the only authority for change. The two halves fit while both people are in the same room and separate the moment they are not.

### Dose time lost to housework, guests, and travel rather than to forgetting

The parent understands the dose but kitchen work, visitors, and talk carry the time away, and a trip or an occasion removes the cues of the home routine. The lapse is one of attention, not of understanding, so a louder instruction to the parent would not reach it.

### No record and no way to know from a distance, and a phone yes that may be false

Daily tracking rests on habit and a fixed shelf, with the paper prescription as fallback; nothing is written, so a lapse cannot be seen after the fact. Away from home the caregiver either trusts blindly or calls, and the call returns a yes that is sometimes wrong. Doses are also missed while the caregiver works or sleeps, when nobody is supervising at all.

### A shared check that fails when everyone is busy at once, sometimes ending in hospital

The family's mutual reminding fails on the day nobody is free. Two households describe a missed dose at a wedding or during a working morning that ended in illness or hospital. After that the caregiver no longer trusts memory alone; the wish for a reminder becomes a felt need.

### The caregiver's standing worry and repeated calling as the hidden cost

Not knowing sits in the caregiver's mind through the working day as pressure and as the question, did she take it or not. The cost of the current arrangement is paid by the caregiver in attention and calls, not only by the parent in missed doses.

**Consequence.** The households reached for tools to close the gap left by the caregiver's absence. The tools they had failed in specific ways, which is the next theme.

## 2. Tools that add work or carry no meaning, and prescriptions nobody can read

**Definition.** Every aid these families have met fails in a specific way. The pill box created a chore and was abandoned. Phone alarms ring without saying which medicine, or were never set. No medicine app was tried because the parent handles calls, not menus, and some use button phones. Handwriting only the pharmacist can read, brand names that differ, and a harmful medicine found only through illness leave the family without the information it needs most.

### A pill box that became one more chore and was abandoned

The box had to be filled every time and was forgotten, so the medicine went back to the strips. An aid that adds a task for the caregiver competes with the work it was meant to remove and loses. It also does nothing for a parent who is out of the house.

### Alarms that ring without naming the medicine, or were never set

A phone alarm does not say which medicine, so it is ignored or leaves the parent guessing; repeated sounds get tuned out; most families never set one for medicine at all. One household finds alarms plus the paper enough, which marks where the problem ends.

### Apps never tried because the parent manages calls, not menus

No family has used a medicine app. Caregivers never thought of one and doubt the parent would manage it: parents make calls and use Facebook but many buttons defeat them, and some keep button phones. The barrier is the interface, not the smartphone.

### Handwriting only the pharmacist can read, brand names that differ, and harm noticed too late

Handwritten prescriptions defeat parents and often the caregiver, so the pharmacist reads them. Brand names change from shop to shop. A medicine that was harming a parent was discovered only when they fell ill. The information the family most needs is the least accessible, and nothing warns them.

**Consequence.** Each failure names a want. The alarm's empty ring and the caregiver's blindness call for an agent that speaks to the parent and reports to the caregiver on exception, the next theme. The unreadable paper and the unnoticed harm call for AI that reads and warns, the last theme.

## 3. An agent that speaks the medicine's name to the parent and tells the caregiver only when a dose is really missed

**Definition.** Caregivers want a reminder that talks to the parent in plain words, naming the medicine and whether it is before or after food, with one button and nothing to read or type, reaching the parent outside the home and without internet. For themselves they want to be told once, after the parent has had a repeat and some time, only when a dose is really missed, through a call that reaches them at work. The checking must be visible to the parent, and the relief it brings is not, in their words, less care.

### A spoken name and timing with one button, nothing to read or type

A reminder that only rings repeats the alarm's failure. It must say which medicine, when, and before or after food, and say it aloud, because parents will not look at text but will hear a voice from the next room. Big text, a picture of the tablet to match the strip, and a single button to confirm are all the parent should face; anything more, or anything that feels like a big medical thing, ends use.

### Reminders that follow the parent outside, offline, on trips, and before the strip runs out

The reminder matters most where family is not: outside, travelling, at occasions. It must work without internet, say pack the medicine before a trip, and warn before the strip is finished. These are the moments the family check cannot cover.

### Escalation that waits, repeats, then reaches one caregiver through a channel that interrupts

First the parent, then one more reminder after a while, then the caregiver, and only when there is a real miss. The delay matters because the system may not know the dose was taken; ten notifications a day would be ignored. For a caregiver at housework or in an office a call cuts through where a notification does not.

### Checking done in the open that lightens the caregiver without replacing the family

Any checking must be visible to the older adult; caregivers refuse a system that watches their parents behind their backs. Being told only about misses would end the calling and the standing worry, and caregivers insist this is not less care: the system helps the parent when they cannot be there, and the family stays the one that checks and decides.

**Consequence.** Telling the caregiver about a miss is only worth trusting if the agent holds the right medicine and timing. Who enters them, keeps them current, and checks them is the last theme.

## 4. AI that reads the prescription, remembers it, and warns, while the doctor still decides

**Definition.** Caregivers want AI to do the reading, remembering, and cross-checking the family cannot do reliably: read a prescription from a photo, hold the current medicines, mark old ones as old, and warn when something does not match. They draw a firm line at recommendation or decision, which stay with the doctor, and they expect to set the system up themselves and verify what it reads.

### Reading the prescription from a photo, trusted only after the caregiver checks it

Photographing a prescription and being told the medicine, dose, and timing would save pharmacy trips and settle doubts. Every caregiver adds that it must be correct and that they will compare it with the pharmacist or the old medicine, because a wrong reading is a new problem. Generic names would make it consistent.

### Holding the current prescription and warning when something changed or does not match

The system should keep the current medicines, mark a changed medicine clearly as old, and warn when something entered is not on the prescription. This is the check nobody performed when a harmful medicine went unnoticed, and it is the vigilance a busy family cannot sustain alone.

### Information the family can check against advice, set up by the caregiver, decided by the doctor

The system may show what the doctor prescribed and what it is for, and let the caregiver check a neighbour's suggestion against it; it must not suggest medicines or act like a doctor. The caregiver enters medicines and times once and changes them when the doctor does, and daily entry is refused. The caregiver becomes the administrator, which places the agent's knowledge, and its loyalty, with the person who enters and checks it.

**Consequence.** This closes the story. The agent's knowledge, and its loyalty, sit with the caregiver who enters and checks it; its voice goes to the parent; its authority stops at the doctor. Phase 2 tests whether a deployed agent kept to these terms.

## Variation and counter-cases

C06 finds phone alarms plus the paper prescription sufficient and wants nothing more; this bounds theme 2 and is kept as a code. C05 uses no aid, misses doses when housework piles up, and wants a call for herself rather than an app for the parents-in-law; C07 wants a paid tool that reminds him. In these homes the caregiver, not the older adult, is the reminder's user, which theme 3's escalation sub-theme carries. C08 alone raises brand versus generic names. The four longer interviews (C01 to C04) carry most codes; the four short ones confirm themes 1 and 3 and add the call, the button phone, and generic names.
