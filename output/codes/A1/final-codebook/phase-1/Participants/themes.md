# Phase 1, Participants (older adults): themes

Four themes from the sixteen Phase 1 older-adult transcripts (P01 to P17, no P14), read as one story. Theme 1 is the collective practice: medicine as family work. Theme 2 is where it breaks and why no tool they have catches it. Theme 3 is what they want the phone to do. Theme 4 is where they place the agent among the people who already remind, check, buy, and decide. Each theme has a definition and ends with the consequence that leads onward. Each sub-theme has a paragraph on what it means. Codes and quotes are in `master-codebook.md`.

## 1. Medication as Distributed Family Work

**Definition.** Older adults take their own medicine and know it by strip, colour, place, and meal, not by name. The names, the paper, the buying, and the doctor's words sit with a son, daughter, or spouse, and they call this sensible rather than a loss. Reminding belongs to whoever is present, a family question settles a doubt, and being checked is received as care with one limit: not to be watched all the time. Friends' talk is heard; only the doctor changes a medicine. A few manage entirely alone.

### Retained Autonomy in Taking, Delegated Knowledge to Kin

Swallowing the tablet is the older adult's own act and nobody hands it to them. The long names, the prescription, the pharmacy trip, and what the doctor said are handed to a child or spouse, and this is described as sensible and easing, not as dependence.

### Recognition by Strip, Place, and Meal Rather Than Name

Recognition replaces reading. Medicines are known by colour and foil, kept in one fixed spot, and tied to tea, breakfast, or dinner. This is the older adult's own system and it works every ordinary day.

### Shared Reminding and the Family Question as Verification

Reminding is not one person's job: a spouse at home, a child who calls, a daughter-in-law who notices, a friend who takes his own tablet. Because older adults are often unsure whether they took a dose, a family question is what restores it, and the rule is to ask rather than take two.

### Checking as Care, Surveillance as Its Limit

A son's or daughter's question is received as safety and help, with an admitted edge of irritation when it is constant. The line is drawn at surveillance: tell me if I miss, do not watch me every dose. Checking is welcome because of who does it and how often.

### Peer Experience as Advice, the Doctor as Authority

Tea shops, walks, and gatherings are full of medicine talk, and agreement among many people makes a medicine feel trustworthy. Older adults listen, take the name to a child or the doctor, and never change on their own.

### Counter-Case: Independent Self-Managers

Three men and one woman manage many medicines from memory, the written prescription, or their own notes, miss doses only when stock runs out, and say they need no tool. They bound the family system and the reminder's reach.

**Consequence.** This arrangement works by presence and habit. When the routine breaks or nobody is near, the dose is lost and, as the next theme shows, no tool or paper the older adult can use catches it.

## 2. Routine Breakdown and the Inaccessibility of Existing Aids

**Definition.** Doses slip when guests, work, or travel change the day, and the miss is caught only if someone is near; when the family is busy or away nobody asks. The tools offered so far do not reach them: a phone alarm set by a child rings without saying why and is abandoned, an app is too many steps, a button phone has two keys they know. Prescriptions are small print and handwriting the pharmacy reads for them; a new packet is not recognised; a strip runs out and they cannot go to buy. The frustration is dependence at exactly the moments nobody is there.

### Time Slipping Outside the Home Routine

Nobody describes forgetting as a failing mind. At home, with the strip in front of them and the family around, the routine carries itself; away from it, in talk, travel, or work without fixed hours, the time goes. On trips the medicine itself is left behind.

### Absent or Occupied Family and Unwanted Dependence

The family check is the repair, and it fails when the family is not there or is occupied at the same moment. Children work, a husband is out, a house full of guests is busy; the older adult remembers late, or not at all. They also do not want to depend on family for every small thing.

### Meaningless Alarms and Unusable Apps

A phone alarm set by a child rings without naming the medicine and is switched off or ignored; the one woman who uses an alarm daily hears it, defers, and forgets. Smartphones serve calls, WhatsApp, and videos; an app with many steps leaves them pressing the wrong thing, and button-phone users know only the green and red keys. Nobody has set up anything for medicine because nobody knew how.

### Unreadable Prescriptions and Unrecognised Packets

Even literate participants cannot read prescriptions without glasses and the handwriting defeats most; the paper goes to the pharmacy, which reads it and hands over the medicine. A different brand changes the packet and they no longer know if it is theirs; a changed prescription leaves old and new strips side by side. The document that defines the regimen is one they cannot consult.

### Supply Failure and Dependence on Others to Buy

Nobody counts tablets; a strip finishes and the market is far. The older adult waits for a child or a neighbour going to market, and doses are missed not from forgetting but from supply.

**Consequence.** Each of these barriers names what a phone would have to do instead: speak, show, ring again, reach them outside, read the paper, and watch the stock. The next theme carries those wants.

## 3. Design Expectations for an Accessible Medication Reminder

**Definition.** The technology older adults describe is concrete. It speaks the medicine time in plain Bangla, simply and not loudly in company, because they will not read a screen. It shows a picture of the tablet, marks morning and night with sun and moon, and says which medicine is new. It rings again if they do not respond, reminds them to carry the medicine before going out, and works in the field or at a relative's house. It reads the handwritten paper and tells the buyer before the strip finishes, if it is correct. The older adult presses one big button and nothing more; the child sets it up.

### Spoken Bangla Reminders with Privacy in Company

Voice is preferred almost unanimously: they do not look at the phone, cannot read small print, and glasses are not always at hand. The voice must be simple, respectful, in Bangla, and private when others are around.

### Visual Recognition Cues and Marked Medication Changes

Because they know medicine by sight, a photo of the strip or tablet lets them match it; simple pictures mark the time of day. When the doctor changes something the phone should say plainly which is new and which has stopped, so the old strip is not taken by mistake.

### Repeated, Pre-Departure, and Location-Independent Reminders

One sound, then another after some time because they may be outside or busy, but not every minute. A nudge before leaving home to carry the medicine. And the reminder must work in the field, at a relative's house, and on the road, which is exactly where the routine fails.

### Prescription Reading and Stock Alerts Conditional on Accuracy

A phone that reads the handwriting and says which medicine, how much, and when would settle doubts, and a message to the buyer when the strip is finishing would prevent the days without medicine. Both are wanted only if correct: medicine is not something to guess.

### Single-Button Interaction with Kin-Managed Setup

The older adult's whole interaction is to hear and to press one large button. Setup, names, times, and changes belong to the son or daughter who knows phones. Typing, passwords, small buttons, and many screens are refused outright.

**Consequence.** A phone that does all this still has to find its place among the people who already remind, check, buy, and decide. The last theme is where the older adults put it.

## 4. Negotiating the Agent's Place in the Care Network

**Definition.** Older adults place the phone inside the arrangement they already have, not above it. It reminds where nobody is, in the field or at a relative's house, while at home the wife knows what the phone cannot, so they want both. It may tell one child when a dose is really missed, after time is given, but never watch every dose, and a forgotten press must not be counted as a missed dose. It is trusted because the son put it in and because people they know use it. It gives small information and defers to the doctor; it never decides, and family is family.

### The Agent Where Family Is Absent, Family Where It Is Present

The phone's place is where family is absent. At home the spouse knows whether they have eaten and is preferred, but she is sometimes busy, so the phone and the family together are better than either alone.

### Exception-Only Escalation to One Family Member

Family being told is welcome because family already asks, but only for a real miss, after a repeat and some time, and not a message for every dose. This restates the household's own rule against being watched.

### The Record Subordinate to the Act

Older adults foresee taking the tablet and forgetting the phone. A missed press must not be read as a missed dose and must not alarm the child; if in doubt the child should ask.

### Trust Borrowed from Kin and Peers

Trust is borrowed from people. If the son entered the medicines it was checked; an unknown app is not trusted. If the walking group or fellow farmers use it and say it works, they will ask a child to install it.

### Information Without Decision, Family Without Replacement

They want to know what a medicine is for, in a line, not an explanation every time. The phone may help with small things and tell them when to ask the doctor; it must not suggest or change medicine, and it never replaces the family.

**Consequence.** This closes the older adults' account and fixes the prototype's terms: voice to the older adult, a picture and a button, configuration and stock alerts to the child, escalation only on a real miss with time allowed, the record below the act, information without decision, and the family kept in. Phase 2 tests whether the deployed agent kept to them.

## Variation and counter-cases

P11, P15, P17 (men, 65 to 80) and P16 (a woman with written notes) manage many medicines alone and want no tool; they are the counter-case sub-theme in theme 1 and bound themes 3 and 4. P12 is the only older adult who uses a phone alarm daily; her account of hearing it and deferring is the strongest evidence in theme 2. P08 and P09 cannot read and use button phones; their wants (a loud ring, Bangla voice, sun and moon) set the floor for theme 3. P02 alone asks that the reminder be quiet in company. The longer interviews (P01 to P08) carry most codes; the shorter ones (P09 to P17) confirm the routine slips, the preference for voice, and the request that family set it up.

## What the prototype takes from these themes

From theme 3: a spoken Bangla reminder naming the medicine and its timing; a tablet picture with sun and moon; a repeat after a delay; a pre-departure carry reminder; operation away from home; prescription reading and a stock alert directed to the buyer, both gated on correctness; one large confirmation button; configuration by a family member. From theme 4: a family notification only after a real miss and a wait; no inference of a miss from a missed press; a spoken statement of whom the agent serves, since trust runs through the person who set it; information without recommendation or change; and a design that keeps family reminding in place rather than replacing it.
