Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-2/Caregiver/C06.md`. Caregiver, 22, woman, granddaughter managing her grandmother's five or six daily medicines; already uses phone alarms. The header carries a real personal name, which does not enter this slot (CLAUDE.md 3.4). Household pairing unfiled; no pairing asserted. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned; long variant guide with many design probes. No consent-exclusion record is filed. Evidence-grade rule as in the Phase 2 P01 memo.

**Household configuration note.** As with C05, the caregiver is the confirming user: she gives the medicines and might have 'given the medicine but forgotten to confirm it.' The agent's first principal is her; the family's checking is of her.

## What this account is about

A granddaughter for whom the agent 'felt like an extension of what I was already doing' with alarms. It is for 'both': her grandmother gets the medicine on time and she need not remember every timing. Being checked is welcome: 'Since I am responsible for her medicines, I actually like having that check. It makes me more confident that I have not missed something.' Family asking is care: 'I would rather someone ask than find out later that a medicine was missed'; 'It is just making sure she is okay.' Silence: remind again, 'I might have given the medicine but forgotten to confirm it'; a false alarm to the family means 'everyone would start worrying unnecessarily.' The prescription is the reference; 'The system is a reminder, not the final authority.' Streak: 'satisfying'; 'With five or six medicines across the day, seeing that regularity is nice'; family said 'good, you are keeping it regular'; a break would feel 'a little bad' but 'The score is not the medicine.' Probation: checked names, timing, ease when busy. Memory: 'the phone is also keeping track. So it reduces the mental load'; 'It did not teach me the routine. It just made me more organized about it.' Escalation: 'It could go to me first. If I don't act on it, then another family member can know.' Closing: 'it should not replace people looking after each other.'

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'I actually like having that check.' Does the caregiver welcome oversight of herself as confidence, inverting the older adults' ambivalence?
2. 'I would rather someone ask than find out later.' Is the family's checking of the carer valued for what it prevents, a missed dose discovered late?
3. 'It could go to me first.' Is the caregiver here the first recipient of every signal, with the family as her backstop rather than the older adult's?
4. 'It did not teach me the routine. It just made me more organized.' Is this the caregiver's version of restored-not-replaced?
