# P08, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P08.md`. Older adult, 67 (an age she gives on her son's authority), woman, homemaker, lives with her son, his wife, and a grandson. Two to four medicines, for pressure, pain, and one at night. Basic phone for calls only. Very little schooling. The transcript labels her turns 'Woman' and opens and closes with greetings; she addresses the interviewer as 'baba'. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned, without apostrophes in places ('dont'); not repaired. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

This is the account in which the partition between getting the medicine and taking it is drawn most sharply, and by the participant herself, three times: 'Taking is easy. Knowing which medicine is little difficult'; 'For bringing and checking yes. For taking no'; 'Taking is not difficult. Getting medicine is difficult sometimes.' The son brings the medicine, knows what the doctor gave, takes her to the doctor and tells her again at home, breaks the half tablet she cannot see to break, and talks to the doctor about side effects she reports to him. She does not know the names, cannot read the small letters, and identifies the medicine by packet. When a packet changed, she refused it: 'I said this is not mine', until the son said it was the same medicine. She asks rather than guesses: 'Why will I guess. Better ask.'

The household has a second checker: the daughter-in-law, who asks whether she has taken it, who caught her about to take the night tablet in the morning, and whom she asks whether she was seen taking a dose when she cannot remember. The daughter-in-law is a witness to the act in a way the son, who is out, cannot be.

Two things I want to protect. First, the stock failures: medicine has run out before anyone bought more, and the son's response was 'you should have told before', which she reports without resentment and follows with 'Sometimes he forgets also. He has work.' The failure is shared and so is the forgetting. Second, her answer about technology: 'Maybe for my son. For me I dont know. I am used to this way. My son brings and tells me. I take.' She locates the technology's user in the son, not in herself, which is a precise reading of where the work is.

She asks for help without shame and says why: 'No why. They are my children.'

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'Whatever they bring, that I take.' Is this a statement of trust, of position, or of the limit of what she is asked to know?
2. 'I said this is not mine.' A refusal of a changed packet is a competence act. How does it sit with an account that otherwise defers on everything about identity?
3. The daughter-in-law is the witness to the act; the son is the holder of knowledge. Is the division by gender and presence, and what does it make of the daughter-in-law's position?
4. 'You should have told before.' Is the stock failure attributed to her, and does she accept the attribution?
5. 'Maybe for my son.' If the technology's user is the son, what would it mean to remind her rather than him?
6. 'No why. They are my children.' Is asking one's children for medicine a right, and how does that reading differ from P05's irritation?
7. 'Old age you know.' She explains her own uncertainty by age, twice. How does an analysis that refuses the deficit frame hold that?
8. She offers the interviewer a seat at the end. What does the hospitality frame do to the account: is the interviewer a guest, and does that shape what she says about her family?

## Absences

The grandson is mentioned as a possible errand-runner and never otherwise. Nothing about the son's wife beyond her checking. No husband is mentioned at all. Nothing about cost, though the son pays. No episode of a serious consequence. The interviewer never asks how the son came to hold the role, whether the daughter-in-law and son coordinate, or whether the participant has ever disagreed with the son.

## Interactional conditions

Questions are short and many are closed; several are leading ('So your son knows which medicine you need', 'So you prefer your family to help you', 'So the main problem is not taking the medicine'). Confirmations are `[elicited]` and substance is taken where she adds. All technology talk from L243 is `[hypothetical]`, and she rejects the app premise outright at L249 before conditionally accepting a reminder. The interviewer's greeting and the participant's address ('baba', 'apa') mark a kin-like register that I have noted and not analysed further.
