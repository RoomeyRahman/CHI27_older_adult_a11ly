# C01, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Caregiver/C01.md`. Caregiver, woman, an adult daughter in paid work, living with her parents and an elderly aunt; she looks after the medicines of all three. Her mother takes three or four medicines for diabetes and pressure; her father two. The transcript carries the caregiver's real name and her employer at L5 (CLAUDE.md Section 3.4); neither enters this slot, and L5 is never extracted. The household pairing to an older-adult participant is not filed (blocker 2), and I have not inferred one. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned, unpunctuated; not repaired. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

A working daughter describes a check that works when she is home and fails as knowledge when she is not. At home she tells them, gives the medicine or says 'take this one now', and they sometimes know themselves: 'They are not completely dependent on me.' Away, she tells them before leaving and calls; and she names the limit exactly: 'If I cannot then I just have to trust that she will take it. There is no proper way to know.' The false yes appears from the caregiver's side: 'sometimes they say yes I took it but then later I find out they forgot. So I have to ask again sometimes.' The mother's forgetting is accounted for as time lost to occupation, not misunderstanding: 'She knows she has to take it but the time goes away.'

Aids have become tasks. A pillbox was tried and abandoned because 'You have to remember to put the medicine in the box'; there is no fixed system, no alarm, no app, because 'we never thought about it'. The pharmacist reads handwriting; the doctor decided when her father reacted to a medicine; 'Medicine is not something where you can guess' (the same phrase P04 used), and 'Doctor is still doctor.'

The passages that matter most for the study are about the role itself. It 'mostly just happened. I started helping because they needed it, and then it became my routine... We don't really sit and decide these things formally.' When the parents say they can manage, 'I just let them do it themselves if they are confident. I don't want to control everything. But if I know they may forget, then I still remind them.' And for a system: she would configure it, they should be told when she changes something, it should tell her after a delay and only for a real miss, and it must not check secretly: 'I don't think it should secretly check. They should know. It is for helping them.' Her closing on calling is the account's moral hinge: 'if I don't have to call it doesn't mean I don't care. It means the system is helping them when I am not there.'

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'There is no proper way to know.' Is the caregiver's problem knowledge rather than reminding, and does the wish for a notification answer that rather than the parents' forgetting?
2. 'Sometimes they say yes I took it but then later I find out they forgot.' The same false yes three older adults volunteered, seen from the other side: what does she do with it, and what does asking again cost the relationship?
3. 'It mostly just happened.' Is the absence of a decision about the role the norm this household runs on, and does that make ceremony absent rather than refused?
4. 'I don't want to control everything.' What distinguishes reminding from controlling for her, and where does the line fall when 'I know they may forget'?
5. 'It should not secretly check. They should know.' Is visibility to the parents a condition of her own comfort with checking, and why?
6. 'If I don't have to call it doesn't mean I don't care.' Is she defending herself against a reading of the system as replacing her, and to whom?
7. She would trust an app's prescription reading only if reliable, and the doctor over the app. Where does the pharmacist sit between the two for her?
8. The aunt is named at the start and never again. Whose medicine is she managing when she says 'their'?

## Absences

The interviewer never asks how her parents came to accept her role, whether they have ever refused a reminder, or how she and the 'other family members' divide the work.
