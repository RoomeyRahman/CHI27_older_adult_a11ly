# P03, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P03.md`. Older adult, 66, woman, lives with her husband; a daughter who lives close comes every day, a son sometimes. Three or four medicines, morning and night. Own smartphone, given by the daughter. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned at source; the English is unpunctuated in places and I have not repaired it. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

This is the first account in the phase in which the medication work is plainly held by someone else, and the participant says so without discomfort: 'I just take what they give', 'I just follow'. The daughter knows the names, keeps most of the strips and arranges them by time, holds the prescription and photographs it, buys the medicine, tracks appointments, accompanies her to the doctor and remembers what was said, relays changes, and 'has written everything'. The husband is the reminder at hand: he asks, sometimes hands her the tablet in the kitchen, and the two spouses remind each other because he has his own medicines. The participant's own competence is recognition by sight, strip and colour and packet, and she defends it precisely: put three strips in front of her and she can pick hers, but she cannot say the name.

The moral account of this arrangement is prudence, not incapacity. 'Why I will take chance. They know more.' Deference to the daughter is presented as the sensible thing to do with something that matters, and the daughter's role is legitimated by knowledge, not by the participant's deficit. Relatives and other women supply talk about medicines; the daughter checks it with the doctor.

The passages I keep returning to are the two about checking. The daughter 'gets angry' when she forgets to carry medicine to someone's house and 'tells me same thing many times'; and at the close, 'I feel better when somebody checks.' She wants a daughter notification because her own report may be wrong: 'Sometimes I say yes yes I took it and then later I realise maybe I didn't.' That is a participant asking for oversight of herself, on the grounds that she is an unreliable witness to her own dose, and receiving scolding as part of the same care. I did not expect either to be volunteered.

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'I just take what they give' is said twice in different words. Is this compliance, trust, or a claim that taking is the only part that is hers?
2. What does the daughter's anger do in the account? It is reported without complaint. Is scolding legible here as care because of who is doing it?
3. She asks that the app tell the daughter when she has not taken, on the grounds that she herself may misreport. Is this delegating oversight, or is it protecting the daughter from a false reassurance?
4. Why does she distinguish so carefully between the husband who reminds and the daughter who knows? Is proximity the husband's licence and knowledge the daughter's?
5. The abandoned alarm: 'Phone keeps making sound and I don't know why.' Is the failure the alarm's unintelligibility, or that it was set without being explained?
6. 'If I forget to press then she can ask me.' A missed confirmation should be resolved by a human question, not by the record. What does that assume about the record's standing?
7. Group advice is discounted with a pharmacological argument ('One medicine may be good for one person and not for another'). Where did that reasoning come from, and is it the daughter's voice?
8. 'If nobody is there then I have to manage but I feel better when somebody checks.' Is managing alone a capacity she holds in reserve, or a condition she dislikes?

## Absences

The son is mentioned three times and never described doing anything except being a further backup. Nothing about cost or who pays. No side effects, no wrong dose. The participant never expresses a wish to know the names or to hold the prescription herself. The interviewer never asks how the daughter came to hold the role, whether the participant has ever disagreed with her, or what the husband thinks of the daughter's arrangement.

## Interactional conditions

Questions are short and mostly open; several are closed summaries ('So you prefer to ask your family before doing anything', 'So your daughter manages the medicines', 'So you depend on your family more than the group'), and answers to those are tagged `[elicited]` on form. All app talk from L97 onward is `[hypothetical]`. The daughter-notification question at L105 is leading; the reason she gives (her own misreport) is not in the question and is treated as hers.
