# P05, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P05.md`. Older adult, 68, woman, retired school teacher, lives with her husband and a working daughter. One regular tablet, taken for years. Button phone by choice. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned, unpunctuated in places; not repaired. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

A retired teacher with one tablet gives an account in which her own competence is explicit and defended by profession: she can read, she checks before taking, she has internalized the prescription ('In the beginning I had to look at the paper. Now no need'). What she hands to her family is the tracking, and she names the partition herself: 'For the medicine itself no. I know my medicine. But for keeping the papers, buying it, doctor appointment, those things yes.' The daughter keeps the medical papers in one file, checks the strip before it runs out, relays changes, and is 'very particular' about not changing anything without the doctor. The husband asks whether she has taken it. Nobody hands her the tablet: 'They don't give me the tablet usually. Just remind me.'

The account's most useful passages are about being checked. Asked whether the reminders help, she volunteers the same mechanism as P03 and P04: 'Sometimes I say yes I have taken, then I realise no no I haven't.' Later she describes the daughter asking 'again and again' whether she has kept her medicine before going out, admits 'Sometimes I get irritated also because she asks so much', and immediately reframes: 'But later I think okay she is helping.' And she supplies a generational reading of the checking: 'Children are like that now. They worry more. They keep checking.' Oversight is described as the children's worry, received with irritation and then gratitude, in one participant, in her own words.

The closing is the sharpest single line on technology in the phase so far: 'Technology can help maybe but for people like me it should be very simple. Otherwise family is easier.' The family is the simpler technology.

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. Her competence claims are grounded in having been a teacher. Does the profession license a different relation to the daughter's role than P03's, where reading is not available?
2. 'Taking medicine is the main thing. Pressing button should be secondary.' What standing does she give the record, and who bears the consequence if the two disagree?
3. She is irritated and then thinks 'she is helping'. Is the reframing a settled evaluation or a repair performed for the interviewer?
4. 'Children are like that now.' Is the checking read as a property of a generation rather than of her condition, and what does that do for her dignity?
5. 'Two people hear better than one.' Is accompaniment to the doctor framed as redundancy rather than as her own deficit?
6. She would accept two reminders, husband and app, and predicts his 'I already told you'. What does the husband lose when an app duplicates him?
7. 'Otherwise family is easier.' Is the family being compared to technology as a competing means, and on what dimension does it win: simplicity, or knowing her?
8. She keeps a strip in her bag 'now'. Is this a change made under the daughter's repeated instruction, and does she own it or attribute it?

## Absences

The husband's own medication, if any, is never mentioned; nor whether he has his own health needs. No cost. No episode of a missed dose is specified beyond timing slips. The interviewer never asks how the daughter came to hold the file, whether the husband and daughter divide the work between them by agreement, or whether the participant has ever refused the daughter's instruction.
