# P09, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P09.md`. Older adult, about 68, woman, rural household in Bogra district, lives with her elder son and daughter-in-law. Three doses a day across morning, noon, and night, for aches, pressure, and stomach. Basic button phone. The transcript is short (under 1,100 words), structured by the protocol's phase headings, and carries an interviewer's observation note at the top and bracketed stage directions in her turns. Translated from Bangla, more heavily edited than the other transcripts (full sentences, some interviewer paraphrase risk). No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

A short account, but the arrangement is stated with unusual completeness in one turn: 'the whole thing stems from them', and they 'bring the medicine, explain the timing, and keep track of stock.' The elder son buys from the town pharmacy and pays; when he brings a new box he opens it and explains it by pointing, in her medium, 'Ma, this blue strip is for your stomach, take it at noon with the round white one. The red foil strip is for your pressure, take it at night.' The daughter-in-law checks the bowl every few days and tells the son what to bring, and asks 'Amma, did you take your noon pill?' when she has forgotten. The participant's part is to keep it in her head: 'Once they tell me two or three times, it becomes a habit.'

Her lapses are seasonal: paddy drying, guests, big meals. Her recovery rule differs from every other account so far: she takes it when she remembers 'or I skip it until the next scheduled time', with no consultation mentioned. Her phone competence is two buttons, green and red. What she would accept from technology is a loud alarm and nothing more: 'Beyond a simple alarm, I cannot operate complex things.'

The closing positions her: 'what suggestions can an uneducated village woman give? As long as my children bring the proper medicines on time and remind me when I forget, Allah keeps me going fine.' The children and providence are the system; she declines the design role the question offers.

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'The whole thing stems from them.' Is this a description, or a disclaimer of responsibility that also disclaims standing?
2. The son explains by colour and by pairing ('with the round white one'). Is the household already running a labelled, in-medium instruction system that a design would only reproduce?
3. She skips to the next dose without asking anyone. Is the no-double rule absent here, or is skipping the rural form of it?
4. 'Amma, did you take your noon pill?' The daughter-in-law addresses her as mother. What does the kin address do to the check?
5. 'What suggestions can an uneducated village woman give?' Is the self-description a refusal of the interview's premise, or a statement of the position from which she speaks about design?
6. The observation note records a folded prescription slip in the bowl. She never mentions it. Who is it for?

## Absences

No husband is mentioned. No other children. Nothing about cost beyond that the son pays. No specific episode of a missed dose. The transcript is edited into full sentences and the interviewer's phrasing may have shaped hers; the contractions and stage directions suggest a cleaned rendering. The interviewer never asks how the son came to be the main one, whether she has ever refused an instruction, or what the daughter-in-law's checking is like to receive.

## Interactional conditions

Six questions in all, several compound. 'So you rely mostly on what your family members bring and explain to you?' supplies the summary she confirms; her addition (two or three tellings become habit) is hers. The alarm question at L64 is `[hypothetical]`; her answer is the only design content. The observation note at L6 is the interviewer's and is coded as context, never quoted as hers.
