## What this account is about

The youngest caregiver in the phase describes herself as the one who gives the medicine to 'those who are elderly or sick in our home and can't take their medicines themselves'. She remembers the regimen herself, by habit, with no aids, and the lapses are hers: 'Sometimes I simply forget, and other times I just get really busy with household work and miss the time', hardest in the mornings. What she wants is a call to herself: 'An incoming call is definitely the most effective for me because it grabs my attention right away while I'm working.' The peace of mind she names is that she 'won't accidentally miss their medication times again'.

In this household, on this account, the older adults do not appear as actors at all; the caregiver is the memory and the hand, and the reminder is for her.

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'Can't take their medicines themselves.' Is this the only account in the phase in which the older adults are described as unable to take, and what does that do to the partition every other account draws?
2. The lapse is hers. Does the caregiver's busyness become the household's forgetting when she is the sole memory?
3. A call, not a sound. Why does a call grab attention where an alarm does not, and is the call a person-shaped reminder?
4. 'Caring for elderly parents is a daily responsibility.' Whose parents: she says father-in-law and mother-in-law. Is the in-law relation collapsed into 'parents' by her or by the translation?
