# P07, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P07.md`. Older adult, 71, woman, widowed some years, lives with her son, his wife, and their child; a daughter lives nearby and calls almost daily. Three or four medicines. Smartphone given by the son, used for calls and messages. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned, unpunctuated in places; not repaired. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

A widow describes a household in which her son has taken the place her husband once held over 'these things': 'after my husband died he looks after these things. He knows my medicines. He knows the doctor.' The succession is stated once and organizes everything. The son holds the names and the prescription, and he took custody of the paper after she lost one: 'Then son said you don't keep these papers give it to me. Now he keeps.' He sets the morning doses out, separates a new medicine and writes morning and night on paper when the regimen changes, buys, takes her to the doctor, and tells her again at home. The daughter calls, 'Ma did you take your medicine', worries more when a medicine is new, arranges the tablets when she visits, and prepares them when the participant stays with her: 'I don't have to think.'

The participant's own part is recognition and taking, and a rule she states three times in different words: ask, do not guess. 'Why take chance. He tells me this is yours.' Even with glasses she asks the son, and she explains why: 'Because he knows.'

What this account gives that the earlier ones did not is the reception of oversight in its most affirmative form. Asked directly whether a son's notification would feel like being watched, she answers: 'No no. He already asks. It is okay. He is my son. I feel safe when he checks.' And at the close: 'I can take the medicine myself but somebody should keep an eye.' The checking is safety, and the eye is wanted. Alongside this sits a plain self-report of decline, 'Before I remembered everything. Now sometimes I forget small things', which I have kept as her account rather than smoothing it into the framing.

The other passage to protect is L111: if the daughter and the son disagreed about a medicine, 'they will talk between themselves. I won't decide. I will say you people decide and tell me. Why I will take tension.' The decision is not hers to make and she says so with relief.

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'After my husband died he looks after these things.' Is medication work a household office that passes by succession, and what did the husband hold before?
2. 'You people decide and tell me. Why I will take tension.' Is this abdication, or an account of where decision properly sits in a household with adult children?
3. 'I feel safe when he checks.' What distinguishes this from P04's 'don't make it like somebody is watching me': the person, the frequency, or her position as a widow in her son's house?
4. The son took the papers after she lost one. Was the handover negotiated, imposed, or accepted, and how does she narrate it?
5. 'I don't trust myself with these new things.' Is distrust of herself with technology the same disposition as asking rather than guessing with medicine?
6. She prefers strips to the box the son offers. Is that a small refusal inside a large delegation, and does it show where her competence lives?
7. 'Phone reminder can do first. If I don't take then child can call.' She orders the agent before the children. What does that ordering protect: the children's time, or her from being asked?
8. 'Before I remembered everything.' How should an analysis that refuses the deficit frame hold a participant's own deficit account?

## Absences

The daughter-in-law and grandchild are in the house and never appear in the medication work. Nothing about cost. Nothing about the husband's own illness or death beyond its date. She never describes disagreeing with the son. The interviewer never asks how the son's role was settled after the death, whether the daughter and son coordinate, or what the son thinks of holding it.

## Interactional conditions

Closed and leading questions are frequent ('Do you think you depend on your son', 'Would that make you feel like someone is watching you', 'So app first family after that'). The watching question supplied the frame she rejects; her rejection and its grounds are hers. All app material from L145 is `[hypothetical]`. The group block (L85 to L111) is probed as in other accounts; her L111 answer about the children deciding between themselves exceeds the question and is retained on that basis.
