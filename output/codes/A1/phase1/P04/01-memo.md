# P04, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P04.md`. Older adult, 69, man, lives with his wife and a grandson; a son and his family live nearby and come almost daily. Four types of medicine across morning, afternoon, and night. Android phone given by the son. A morning tea-shop group. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned, unpunctuated in places; not repaired. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

This is the most fully articulated account of distributed medication work so far, and the participant articulates it himself at the close: 'Friends tell you something. Wife tells you something. Son checks the doctor things. We manage like that. Medicine is not one person's work only.' The knowledge is divided by kind. The son knows the long names, keeps the prescription, remembers what the doctor said, and goes along when a medicine is being changed. The wife knows which tablet is morning and which is night, and whether he has eaten. He knows the strip by shape and colour and refuses the need to know more: 'Why to remember all. If you show me the packet I know.' The tea-shop group supplies a fourth kind of knowledge, experience: 'They are taking it also. They are not just saying from book.'

Two things stand out. First, the uncertainty about having taken a dose is his own and recurrent: 'Sometimes I think I already took and then I have to check', and the wife's question is answered 'yes yes' and then found wrong. He treats his own report as unreliable and the wife's question as the check on it, which is the same mechanism P03 volunteered. Second, the escalation rule is stated with a limit on both sides: the son should know if he misses, 'But don't make it like somebody is watching me all the time. Just if I miss many times then tell him.' This is the first explicit refusal of continuous watching in the phase, and it sits beside an acceptance of being told about.

The account also makes trust relational in two directions: he would trust an app more 'if son puts everything' because 'I know he checked it', and he would be more interested if his group used it, 'Same like medicine.' Trust travels along the same lines as advice.

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'Why to remember all' refuses a standard the interviewer implied. What kind of competence is he claiming instead, and is it the same one P03 claims with the strips?
2. The wife 'knows if I have eaten or not. Phone doesn't know.' What does the phone lack: information, or the standing to ask?
3. 'Don't make it like somebody is watching me all the time.' Who is the somebody: the app, the son, or the son through the app?
4. Peer consensus produces 'some trust' but 'final decision doctor'. Is the group a source of candidates for the doctor, and does the son sit between the two?
5. Indirect reminding: 'They say I have to take mine and then I remember mine.' Is this a reminder no one has to give, and therefore one that costs no face?
6. He would trust the app because the son checked it. Is the son's configuration a warranty, and what happens to the app's standing when the son is absent?
7. The wife tells him to carry the box and he says yes and forgets again. Is 'yes yes' a courtesy that closes the exchange rather than a commitment?
8. Both alarms set by the son were abandoned as unintelligible sound. Is the failure that the son configured without explaining, as with P03?

## Absences

The grandson is named in the household and never mentioned again. Nothing about the daughter-in-law. Nothing about cost or who pays. No side effects or wrong doses. The interviewer never asks how the son came to hold the prescription, whether the wife and son ever disagree, or whether he has ever resisted the son's involvement. The interviewer's group questions are many and leading, and I have discounted the consensus material accordingly.
