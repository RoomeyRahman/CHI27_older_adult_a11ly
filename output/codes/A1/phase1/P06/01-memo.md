# P06, Phase 1: familiarization memo

Source: `Supplementary/Interviews/phase-1/Participants/P06.md`. Older adult, 67, man, farmer, rural joint household of six: himself, his wife, a son, the son's wife, two grandchildren. He and his wife both take diabetes medicine. No mobile phone of his own; the son's phone is the household's. Translated from Bangla, lightly cleaned, unpunctuated in places; not repaired. No consent-exclusion record is filed.

## What this account is about

A farmer who cannot read the prescription and does not own a phone gives the clearest account so far of medication work as a household and village practice. Two things structure it. First, the wife is on the same kind of medicine, so reminding is reciprocal between two patients: 'Same I also tell her. We both remind each other sometimes', with strips kept apart so that neither takes the other's. Second, the son is the reader. He reads the prescription because he is 'more educated', buys when there is a new prescription, accompanies for changes, writes or photographs what the doctor says, and tells it again at home. The pharmacy is the other reader, and its authority is long acquaintance: 'We have been going there many years. They know us. If I show strip they understand.'

What this account adds is time. Farming does not follow the clock: 'Water problem comes, labour comes late... You think I will come in one hour and then three hours go. Then medicine timing can move.' The lapse is the field's, not his. And it adds a fully reasoned account of peer trust: farmers sit together after work, 'we work same kind of work', 'He is not trying to sell me anything', and adoption of a tool would follow the same logic as a new seed: 'If one farmer uses some new seed and it works then others try. Same thing.' The doctor still decides, and he says so more times than the interviewer asks.

The agent's place is stated precisely: 'At home wife already tells me. Outside there is no one. So phone can tell there.' The agent is for the field. Control belongs to the son or wife: 'I can only take when it tells me.' And the son is the model for how it should speak: 'Like son telling me.'

## Analytic noticings, as questions

1. 'Nobody sits and tells me every day' is said before the wife's reminding is described. Is he pre-empting a reading of himself as supervised?
2. Reciprocal reminding between two patients: does sharing the condition remove the asymmetry that makes checking read as oversight?
3. 'He is not trying to sell me anything.' Is peer trust defined against a commercial source, and where does that put a phone that 'itself tells me'?
4. The new-seed analogy is his. Does it make adoption a collective decision of the group rather than of the household?
5. 'Outside there is no one.' Is the agent's role the wife's absence, and does that make it a proxy for her rather than for the son?
6. 'I can only take when it tells me.' Is this a description of the arrangement he wants, or of a position he is already in with the son?
7. 'I am trying to remember better now.' What is he responding to: his wife, his son, the interview?
8. 'You ask many questions today.' What does the length of the interview do to the last third of the answers?

## Absences

The son's wife and the grandchildren are never mentioned in the medication work, though six people share the house. The wife's own view is reported by him only. Nothing about cost, though a farmer's income and two regimens make it likely to matter. No episode of a serious consequence. The interviewer never asks how the son came to be the reader, whether the son's wife does any of the work, or whether anyone has ever checked without him knowing.

## Interactional conditions

The interview is long and the interviewer probes the group block heavily and leads often ('So the group influences you but doctor makes the final decision', 'So seeing others use it matters', 'So family and community both have influence'). Confirmations are `[elicited]` and the substance is taken where he adds. All app material from L161 is `[hypothetical]` and premised on a phone he does not have, which the interviewer has to supply ('If your son gave you a smartphone'). The closing remark about many questions marks fatigue; the last third is coded but weighted accordingly.
