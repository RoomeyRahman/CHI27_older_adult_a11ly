# Reflexive thematic analysis reconciliation: Phase 1 care networks

## 1. Analytic approach

This report answers RQ1 through a source level audit of Phase 1. The corpus contains 24 filed transcripts, 16 from older adults and 8 from caregivers. P14 has no Phase 1 transcript. The analysis uses a critical realist form of reflexive thematic analysis: accounts provide situated interpretations of medication work, not direct observation of every dose or household interaction. Coding was weighted inductively, then tested against RQ1 and the project's affiliation practices among humans.

The user asked Codex to evaluate the completed A1 analysis and repair its codebooks, themes, and story. This makes A2 a reconciliation analysis, not a clean independent analysis. I reviewed every Phase 1 transcript in full, inspected the A1 final artifacts, retested extracts at source, and reconstructed the master synthesis. A1 retains its per transcript audit trail. A2 does not invent a second five pass trail that was not produced. Its trace runs from the review decision log, through the code synthesis and theme construction, to the 40 code master matrix.

All interviews were conducted in Bangla and are filed in English translation. The analysis preserves the filed wording and attributes extracts by participant identifier, phase, and line. Caregiver transcripts remain identifiable at source, so no personal name, employer, or location specific detail enters A2. The absent household pairing map prevents claims that an older adult and caregiver described the same household episode.

The corrected trajectory is 40 master codes, 12 subthemes, and 4 themes. Thirty three codes are relevant to older adult accounts and 27 to caregiver accounts, with overlap where both roles contribute. Seven codes are single instance observations. Twelve design facing codes are classified as elicited in the audit trail because the guide introduced the relevant feature or scenario.

## 2. Reflexivity and positionality

The full reflexive statement is in `phase1/master/05-reflexivity.md`. I entered the audit primed by the project's interest in care, checking, and allegiance. That orientation risked finding one coherent family system where the corpus instead contains independent managers, negotiated help, occasional irritation, and uneven access to devices. I therefore treated individual autonomy as a serious rival rather than a position to dismiss.

The corpus also complicated a burden centered caregiver story. C01 described a role that accumulated without decision, but other transcripts distributed work among older adults, several kin, pharmacists, doctors, papers, packages, and routines. I used articulation work [106] as the primary interpretive frame because it keeps those different contributions visible. The logic of care [80] helps with delegated setup and checking, but does not explain every self manager or the firm clinical authority given to prescriptions and doctors. It remains supporting rather than the analytic spine.

Most feature judgments followed prompts. I treated them as evaluations of offered designs and gave greatest weight to expectations that addressed an independently described tension. This preserves a distinction between present practice, design expectation, and later use.

## 3. Overview of the thematic structure

The four themes form a sequence:

1. Medication work is distributed through differentiated household roles.
2. Routine transitions expose weak coordination and uncertain verification.
3. Existing aids fit tasks but miss household handoffs.
4. Families expect technology to support bounded care network roles.

Together they make one argument. Medication routines work when people, artifacts, places, and times align. Breakdowns arise when that alignment changes, not simply when an older adult fails to remember. Current aids sometimes support a task well, but often do not carry information or accountability across the resulting handoff. Participants therefore evaluated technology as legitimate when it helps coordinate differentiated roles, keeps the older adult involved, limits escalation, exposes checking, and leaves clinical decisions with people.

The collectivist angle lies in that differentiated relational work rather than in a claim that all Bangladeshi families behave alike. P10 directly connected family stock work, community encouragement, and technology that should complement existing relations. Independent managers and privacy boundaries remain part of the pattern. Relational interdependence is therefore an empirical arrangement with limits, not a cultural explanation imposed on every participant.

## 4. Medication work is distributed through differentiated household roles

Medication work is divided by kind rather than transferred wholesale. Older adults often retain the act of taking through repeated action and familiar appearance. P08 makes this division explicit: "Taking is easy. Knowing which medicine is little difficult" (P08, Phase 1, L101). The contrast protects competence. Taking remains an achievement even when another person holds the biomedical name or prescription.

Recognition is one form of knowledge within that division. P03 could identify familiar medicines by repeated sight but said, "the name I cannot say" (P03, Phase 1, L71). Kin often hold a different form. P04 explained, "My son. He keeps the prescription also. He knows what doctor said" (P04, Phase 1, L51). Pharmacists interpret difficult handwriting, while doctors retain authority over changes. P06 condensed the boundary: "Change means doctor has to say" (P06, Phase 1, L103). Work is distributed, but authority is unequal.

Reminding also moves with presence. P02 assigned it to "Whoever is around" (P02, Phase 1, L377), and several accounts describe spouses or peers cueing a dose while taking their own. C01's caregiving role "just happened" and became routine (C01, Phase 1, L357), but this is one account and remains labeled as such. It cannot support a claim that caregiver roles generally arrive without negotiation.

Independent managers define the theme's boundary. P11, P15, P16, and P17 described keeping track through memory, prescriptions, or notes and rejected a present need for help. P17 stated, "my memory is very sharp" (P17, Phase 1, L27). This is both a report of practice and a defense of competence. A care network account must therefore represent the right to distribute work and the right to retain it.

**So what.** Articulation work [106] explains why medication management cannot be reduced to dose taking. The care network coordinates different forms of knowledge and authority. Interdependence [13, 30] helps explain how delegated information work can sustain rather than diminish participation. The design implication is role specific support, not automatic transfer of control to a caregiver.

**Evidence status:** RQ1 and contribution C1. The general pattern clears the two source rule across both participant groups. Caregiving role accretion remains a single instance.

## 5. Routine transitions expose weak coordination and uncertain verification

Stable routines depend on aligned cues. P01 described home as fixed and travel as the point where "everything changes" (P01, Phase 1, L95). Work, visitors, altered meals, and leaving home similarly displace the cue or medicine that ordinarily carries the action. P08 described a distinct supply problem: "We forget to buy before it finishes. Then there is no medicine" (P08, Phase 1, L149). A louder reminder cannot supply an absent dose.

Shared responsibility also fails when availability shifts. P13 described a moment when "Everybody was busy. Nobody remembered either" (P13, Phase 1, L53). The account locates the lapse in a distribution of attention rather than one person's stable deficit. Changed packaging exposes a parallel dependency: visual expertise works until a familiar strip changes.

Distance produces uncertainty rather than proof. C01 explained, "I just have to trust that she will take it" (C01, Phase 1, L109). That uncertainty creates pressure for caregivers, but it does not establish that the medicine was missed. Nor does a confirmation button establish that it was taken. The evidence supports an uncertain handoff between action and record.

Checking becomes morally ambiguous at that handoff. C03 described worry when nobody checks. P05 described irritation at repeated questions, then interpreted the same questions as help. These accounts prevent a fixed reading of checking as either surveillance or care. Its meaning depends on the relationship, frequency, visibility, and consequence.

Two caregivers associated missed doses with later illness, including a hospital visit. These narratives explain the perceived stakes of checking. They do not provide clinical evidence that the missed doses caused the symptoms.

**So what.** The design problem is transition management. Systems need to represent changed place, interrupted time, missing stock, altered packaging, unavailable kin, and uncertainty about action. Treating all of these as forgetting would erase the coordination work and invite inappropriate escalation.

**Evidence status:** RQ1 and contribution C1, with a design basis for C3. Transition problems clear the two source rule. Phone confirmation uncertainty and irritation from repeated checking each include a labeled single instance.

## 6. Existing aids fit tasks but miss household handoffs

Existing aids have variable fit. C01 described abandoning a pillbox because the family had to refill it and sometimes forgot. The artifact shifted rather than removed remembering work. Generic alarms created a different problem. C04 said, "It just rings. It does not say what to take" (C04, Phase 1, L93). The sound signaled time while leaving medicine identification to the user or another person.

Countercases prevent a universal failure narrative. P12 found a daily alarm helpful, even though taking could be deferred after it rang. P16 used notes prepared with a spouse. C06 reported that a basic phone alarm and paper prescription already met current needs. Notes, prescriptions, labels, and leaflets can support dense regimens. The question is which task an aid supports and what new work it creates.

Device and document practices further shape fit. P06 described stopping after an unintended interaction: "Then I leave it" (P06, Phase 1, L167). This response is patterned participation, not a failure to adopt. Button phones remain useful for calls while limiting proposed app interaction. Small print and handwriting can shift reading to kin or pharmacists, while stable paper records can support independent management.

**So what.** Technology should join an existing ecology rather than replace it. A design may preserve alarms, prescriptions, packaging, and family setup while repairing the handoff between them. Evaluating an aid only by whether an older adult operates it alone would miss intermediated use [98, 33, 1] and the competence expressed through choosing who configures what.

**Evidence status:** RQ1 and contribution C1, with formative support for C3. Pillbox abandonment and the sufficiency claim from C06 remain single instances. Multiple countercases support the broader claim of variable fit.

## 7. Families expect technology to support bounded care network roles

This theme reports elicited expectations. The interview guide proposed voice, pictures, prescription reading, reminders, family notifications, and confirmation. The data therefore show how participants evaluated offered possibilities, not what they spontaneously requested or later used.

Participants often favored cues that kept the older adult directly involved. P02 valued voice because "I can just hear it" (P02, Phase 1, L273). Medicine names, pictures, large controls, and simple timing linked a digital prompt to familiar practice. Yet P02 alone raised privacy in company, showing that voice first design needs a quieter alternative.

Escalation was evaluated as a sequence, not a default broadcast. Participants often preferred the older adult to receive a reminder and another chance before one caregiver learned about a meaningful exception. P01 accepted family involvement "if I really don't take it," but "not for every small thing" (P01, Phase 1, L211). C01 rejected secret checking. Direction and visibility therefore matter before any agent enters the household.

Participants also separated system evidence from medication action. P05 said, "Taking medicine is the main thing. Pressing button should be secondary" (P05, Phase 1, L211). Nonconfirmation should remain an uncertain state rather than an accusation of nonadherence. This boundary matters for both dignity and the legitimacy of caregiver alerts.

Finally, participants placed setup and clinical decisions in different hands. Trusted kin could enter a schedule, but the technology should not recommend medication changes. P04 tied trust to a son's verification of entered information. The system's legitimacy therefore rests on accountable human configuration and continuing clinical authority, not independent judgment.

**So what.** These expectations define an accountability boundary around technology. The system may remind, identify, record, and route an exception. It may not infer ingestion from a button, conceal family access, or assume prescribing authority. The opportunity is to make delegated configuration inspectable and revocable while retaining direct older adult participation.

**Evidence status:** RQ1 with formative support for RQ3 and contribution C3. Every code in this theme is elicited. Privacy around spoken reminders is a single instance.

## 8. Relations between themes and design opportunity

The themes move from arrangement to breakdown to intervention. Differentiated roles make everyday medication work possible. Transitions expose where their handoffs fail. Existing aids show that solving one task can leave coordination untouched or create another task. Elicited expectations then specify which technological role might repair the handoff without taking over clinical or household authority.

This relation yields a Phase 1 framework with three analytic layers. First, identify who takes, recognizes, interprets, buys, reminds, verifies, configures, and authorizes. Second, identify what changes when time, place, supply, regimen, or availability shifts. Third, identify who may see, change, escalate, and decide, and how that authority becomes known. The framework is grounded in Bangladesh. The portable contribution is an analytic contrast between a system designed for one principal and a care network that distributes work and authority, not a claim about every Global South setting.

The fuller narrative and evidence to design trace appear in `analysis/plans/result/story_phase_1.md`. Phase 1 establishes the human affiliation baseline. It does not show negotiation with the agent, which requires Phase 2.

The current tool and future feature themes should be read together. Generic alarms motivate semantic voice and visual prompts. Difficult documents motivate human verified interpretation. Distance and checking pressure motivate delayed, visible exception escalation. Existing notes, prescriptions, and ordinary alarms establish a baseline that future technology must exceed rather than replace. Stock notices and prescription reading remain elicited expectations, and prescription capture was not implemented in the later deployment.

## 9. Gamification boundary

Phase 1 contains no direct evaluation of streaks, points, scores, badges, rewards, or other game-like features. It cannot support a finding that older adults liked or disliked gamification. Phase 1 instead establishes constraints for the later analysis: taking medicine is more important than recording it, repeated prompting can create pressure, family involvement should remain selective, and technology should complement relational care. Older adults' lived interpretations of the deployed streak belong to Phase 2.

## 10. Limits

This analysis covers one country, one language in translation, and interview accounts rather than observed dosing or system logs. It does not claim saturation. The missing household pairing map prevents paired episode analysis. Caregiver files remain undeidentified at source. The guide introduced most technology features, so design preferences remain elicited. P14 has no Phase 1 transcript. The method file and corpus conflict on caregiver phase participation, and the proposal still describes the completed deployment as future work.
