# Phase 1 caregiver themes

The caregiver account is organized as four connected themes. The sequence distinguishes the care arrangement, its breakdowns, the limits of current tools, and expectations for future technology. These are role-specific views of the combined Phase 1 analysis, not a separate code hierarchy.

## 1. Caregiving coordinates a differentiated care network

Caregivers do more than deliver doses. They interpret prescriptions, obtain medicine, preserve clinical instructions, remind when present, and check when uncertain. Older adults may still recognize and take their own medicine, while pharmacists interpret handwriting and doctors authorize changes. Care is therefore distributed by task and authority rather than transferred wholesale to one caregiver.

### Responsibility follows presence and may settle without a formal decision

Whoever is available may remind or notice a low supply. One caregiver described this responsibility gradually becoming routine, but that account remains a single instance rather than a general origin story for caregiver roles.

### Knowledge and authority sit with different people

Caregivers may hold medicine names, papers, and purchasing knowledge. Pharmacists make difficult prescriptions actionable, while participants reserve medication changes for doctors. Collective decision making is thus structured, not interchangeable.

### Supporting another person does not erase that person's participation

Caregiver coordination can leave the act of taking with the older adult. This distinction prevents family involvement from being read automatically as dependence.

## 2. Distance and transition turn coordination into uncertain verification

The arrangement becomes fragile when work, travel, visitors, or changed routines separate the people and cues that normally align. Caregivers then face an evidentiary problem: they may know that nobody checked, but they do not necessarily know that a dose was missed.

### Shared reminding can fail collectively

When everyone is occupied, responsibility does not move cleanly to another person. This is a coordination lapse rather than proof that one family member failed to care.

### Distance converts observation into trust and repeated contact

Caregivers away from home may call or rely on verbal confirmation. One account questioned whether a verbal yes always settled the matter, so this uncertainty is retained as a boundary case.

### Checking carries care and pressure at the same time

Caregivers described worry when they could not check. Older adult evidence shows that repeated questions can also irritate, which prevents monitoring from being classified in advance as either benign care or surveillance.

## 3. Current tools solve isolated tasks but do not coordinate handoffs

Existing tools have uneven fit. A pillbox can create refilling work, a generic alarm can signal time without naming the medicine, and a phone interface can exceed the interaction a household has assigned to an older adult. Paper prescriptions, notes, labels, and ordinary alarms also remain useful in some households.

### An aid can redistribute the work it promises to remove

The pillbox abandonment came from one household, but it reveals an important design test: a tool fails that household when maintaining it becomes another unassigned remembering task.

### Signals without medicine meaning leave interpretation to the family

A generic alarm may ring without specifying what should be taken or whether it relates to food. The sound reaches the person, but the care network still supplies the missing meaning.

### Low technology practices remain legitimate baselines

One caregiver found phone alarms and the paper prescription sufficient. That countercase rules out a universal claim that families need an app and establishes replacement cost as part of technology fit.

## 4. Future technology is expected to support selective coordination under human authority

Caregivers did not ask technology to judge adherence or replace family decisions. Their elicited expectations connect each proposed feature to a specific weakness in the current arrangement: meaningful reminders for the older adult, delayed exception alerts for one caregiver, and human verification of medication information.

### Reminders should state the medicine action, not merely attract attention

Caregivers expected speech, medicine names, timing, and simple visual matching to make prompts actionable. These expectations were elicited by interview probes, so they are design judgments rather than reports of use.

### Escalation should wait, remain selective, and be visible

Participants preferred another reminder and time to respond before caregiver involvement. They rejected constant notification and secret checking. A missing confirmation still cannot prove a missed dose.

### Kin may configure, but clinicians retain decisional authority

Trusted kin may enter schedules and verify information against the prescription. Technology may inform or warn, but participants did not grant it authority to change medicines. This arrangement positions a future agent inside an existing relational order.
