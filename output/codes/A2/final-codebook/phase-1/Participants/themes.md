# Phase 1 older adult themes

The older adult account is organized as four connected themes. The sequence distinguishes participation in collective medication work, breakdowns during transition, the fit of current tools, and expectations for future technology. These are role-specific views of the combined Phase 1 analysis, not a separate code hierarchy.

## 1. Older adults preserve agency inside collective medication work

Older adults often retain the act of taking by recognizing medicines through strips, places, meals, and repeated action. Kin may hold prescriptions, purchase supplies, or repeat clinical instructions, while pharmacists interpret difficult handwriting and doctors authorize change. This is differentiated participation rather than a simple division between an independent user and a dependent recipient.

### Taking and knowing are different forms of competence

An older adult may take the correct familiar medicine without recalling its biomedical name. Kin-held knowledge can support that action without replacing it.

### Reminding circulates through relationships and presence

Spouses, children, and peers can cue remembering because they are present within an ordinary routine. This relational distribution gives the care network practical capacity that an individual-only account would miss.

### Independence remains an active boundary

Several older adults managed with memory, notes, or prescriptions and rejected a current need for medication technology. Their refusal is patterned participation and must remain available in any care network design.

## 2. Routine transitions expose coordination problems, not a general memory deficit

Home routines often work because medicine, meals, places, and people remain aligned. Travel, visitors, variable work, delayed meals, packaging changes, and depleted stock disrupt that alignment. The problem is therefore broader than remembering a clock time.

### Changed time and place displace familiar cues

A person may remember the schedule but leave the medicine behind, or notice an alarm and defer action while occupied. These episodes require different responses from a louder reminder.

### Family availability shapes whether a lapse is noticed

When everyone is busy or a caregiver is away, shared reminding can fail. Distance produces uncertainty rather than direct evidence about what the older adult did.

### Supply and packaging create non-reminder failures

A finished strip prevents a dose even when the schedule is remembered. A changed package can also unsettle recognition, showing that access and interpretation belong inside medication support.

## 3. Current tools support fragments of practice and create new handoffs

Older adults encounter an ecology of alarms, paper prescriptions, labels, notes, familiar locations, calls, and shared phones. Each artifact can help with one task, but its value depends on who configures it, who can interpret it, and what happens after the signal.

### Generic alarms attract attention without specifying action

An alarm may help, but it may not identify the medicine and can be deferred while the person is busy. Reminder efficacy therefore depends on semantic content and follow-through.

### Device and document access shape direct participation

Multi-step apps, button phones, small print, and difficult handwriting change who can act without help. Stopping at an unfamiliar screen is an interaction boundary, not evidence of age-based incapacity.

### Existing practices may already be sufficient

Notes, prescriptions, fixed locations, and family questions continue to work for some older adults. Future technology must justify itself against these situated practices rather than assuming an unmet need from age alone.

## 4. Future technology is acceptable when it extends, rather than replaces, the care network

Older adults' elicited expectations favored technology that keeps them directly involved while making family support more selective. Voice, medicine names, pictures, and simple controls connect prompts to familiar action. Family setup and clinician authority bound what the system may do.

### Voice and visual cues should connect a prompt to a familiar medicine

Spoken Bangla can reduce sustained screen attention, while pictures and medicine names can support matching. One privacy countercase shows that voice should have a quieter alternative when other people are present.

### Escalation should communicate an exception, not continuous compliance

Participants often preferred a repeat and time to respond before one family member was told. They also separated taking medicine from pressing a confirmation button, so a digital record remains subordinate to the action it represents.

### Trust remains attached to accountable people

Older adults may trust information more when kin configure it and check it against a prescription. The doctor retains authority over medication change, and family remains relationally present even when technology handles routine prompts.

## Analytic boundary: Phase 1 did not evaluate gamification

No Phase 1 interview directly asked older adults to assess streaks, points, scores, badges, rewards, or game-like medication features. Phase 1 therefore cannot support a theme claiming that older adults liked or disliked gamification. It supplies constraints for later analysis: digital confirmation is secondary to taking medicine, repeated prompting can create pressure, selective family involvement matters, and technology should complement relational care. Whether a streak becomes encouragement, pressure, competition, or a relational occasion is a Phase 2 question.
