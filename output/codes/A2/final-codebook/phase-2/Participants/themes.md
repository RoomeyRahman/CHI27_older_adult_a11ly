# Phase 2 older adult themes

The revised analysis contains five themes. They explain when older adults accept family involvement through the agent, why they keep taking medicine, how friends and community matter, how care tasks are divided, and what the prototype supported or failed to support. Each theme also states what can go wrong. Positive comments about the prototype are not treated as proof that medication taking improved.

## 1. Older adults accept family involvement when they stay in control

**Central organizing concept:** Older adults accept the agent involving family when it explains what is happening, gives them time to respond, lets them refuse, and keeps people in charge of medicine decisions.

**Constituent subthemes:** Older adults respond before family is involved; People check before trusting the agent.

Older adults understood family involvement through a step-by-step process. The agent said whom it was helping, reminded the older adult first, asked before contacting family, allowed refusal, waited or reminded again, and then contacted one chosen relative. This sequence showed older adults when and why family became involved.

### Older adults respond before family is involved

Putting the older adult first did not mean excluding family. It meant that ordinary reminders began with the person taking the medicine and that family involvement needed a clear reason. Waiting, reminding again, asking permission, and contacting one chosen person kept the response matched to the situation. An older adult managing medicine alone could keep ordinary reminders private. Another could choose family involvement as part of an existing care relationship.

A fixed sequence does not automatically create meaningful consent. A spoken announcement initially felt strange to some participants, and repeated requests could become irritating. Allowing the agent to override a refusal in higher-risk situations was discussed in interviews but not deployed. The data therefore shows what participants said an acceptable process should look like, not what the prototype achieved in use.

### People check before trusting the agent

Older adults compared reminders with prescriptions, packages, established routines, relatives, pharmacists, and doctors. They refused duplicate or contradictory prompts and checked the setup again when medication changed.

These practices show how older adults can take back control. They may ignore a doubtful prompt, ask a trusted person, return to a known routine, or stop relying on the agent. Family involvement is acceptable when the agent helps coordinate an approved medicine routine without deciding what that routine should be.

## 2. Routines and prescriptions matter more than scores and streaks

**Central organizing concept:** Older adults mainly take medicine because of established routines and prescriptions. Streaks add some interest but can also cause disappointment and pressure.

**Constituent subthemes:** Routines and prescriptions come before scores; A streak can add small interest; Broken streaks can create pressure.

Older adults described years of medication practice, following a prescription, knowing that medicine had to be taken, and treating timely medicine as a priority. These reasons existed before the prototype. A score or streak could make regular practice more visible, but it did not create the main reason for taking medicine.

### Routines and prescriptions come before scores

Long-term routines and doctors' instructions made medication taking necessary. Some participants did not understand why one tablet needed a score, found scores difficult to understand, or said they would follow the prescription with or without points. They were not resisting medication. They were saying that game feedback should not redefine a necessary health practice.

### A streak can add small interest

A run of completed days made regular practice visible. For some older adults, seeing that run added interest or a small desire not to lose it. Family members could also notice the effort. The streak displayed what the older adult had done. It did not create the main reason for taking medicine.

The study cannot establish that gamification works differently for older adults than for a regular or younger user because it contains no comparison group. It can show why common gamification assumptions fit unevenly here. Participants brought longstanding routines, prescription based responsibility, limited interest in numerical scores, and different levels of score comprehension. These conditions reduce the analytic value of treating points as a universal behavioral incentive.

### Broken streaks can create pressure

Accumulation also created vulnerability. Losing a long streak produced disappointment or irritation, even when the dose mattered more than the number. Family members sometimes redirected attention to the next dose, but this repair came from the relationship rather than the game mechanic. Constant monitoring, competition, exaggerated celebration, or performance judgment could turn a secondary display into pressure.

The legitimate role of gamification is therefore narrow. A progress trace may represent continuity and enable low pressure recognition. It cannot measure ingestion, replace established motivation, or become a condition for family approval.

## 3. Friends and community shape care, but doctors decide medicine

**Central organizing concept:** Older adults remind others and learn from friends and community groups, but doctors still decide which medicine they should take.

**Constituent subthemes:** People remind and learn from each other; Community advice leads to questions, not decisions.

The earlier analysis treated collective care mainly as family involvement. The transcripts show a wider network with different roles. Older adults reminded spouses, asked about other people's medicine, heard medicine experiences at gatherings and community groups, and judged unfamiliar technology partly through people they knew. They received care, but they also reminded, informed, and cared for others.

### People remind and learn from each other

Mutual reminders made medication care bidirectional within close relationships. Beyond the household, hearing another person mention a tablet could cue attention to one's own schedule. Medicine experiences also circulated through everyday conversation. These exchanges did not resemble a formal health service, but they formed part of the social environment through which health routines were remembered and interpreted.

The data does not show that every older adult wanted broad community involvement. Community questions appeared only in some interviews, often after a direct question from the interviewer. This theme shows a form of care that a family-only view had hidden. It does not claim that all participants wanted it.

### Community advice leads to questions, not decisions

Use by known peers could make a new app seem more trustworthy. Other people's medicine experiences could also lead an older adult to ask a family member or doctor. Community advice opened questions, but it did not decide treatment. Participants separated listening to others from changing medicine and kept doctors responsible for medicine decisions.

The same participants also identified weaknesses. Conflicting stories could create confusion, and several rejected an agent that recommended medicine based on other people's use. They also separated conversational health sharing from automatic disclosure of scores or medication records. A collectivist care network therefore needs chosen relationships and differentiated rights, not indiscriminate data sharing.

## 4. Care tasks are divided while older adults stay involved

**Central organizing concept:** Family members can handle setup and supplies while older adults remain involved in taking, confirming, and checking medicine.

**Constituent subthemes:** Family setup can preserve older adult control; Features send tasks to responsible people.

Accessible design did not require the older adult to do every task alone, and it did not give the whole task to a caregiver. Instead, it divided the work. A relative could set the schedule, another person could buy medicine, and a doctor or pharmacist could check unclear instructions. The older adult could still recognize the reminder, take the medicine, confirm the action, and question an error.

### Family setup can preserve older adult control

A relative with more device experience could type information and set the schedule. The older adult could still take medicine, recognize the prompt, confirm a dose, question an error, and ask for help. Receiving setup help therefore did not mean that the older adult was not using the system.

The weakness is hidden dependence. If every change requires an unavailable relative, or if configuration changes are invisible, assisted setup can remove rather than preserve control. The agent should announce material changes and keep the older adult able to inspect, refuse, and fall back to familiar records.

### Features send tasks to responsible people

Proposed low supply alerts routed purchasing to the person who could obtain medicine. Proposed prescription capture routed difficult handwriting toward a digital representation, but participants still required family, pharmacist, or doctor verification.

Stock sensing and prescription capture were not deployed. These accounts support a design allocation, not evidence that either feature worked. The acceptable pattern is that a feature routes a bounded task to an accountable person while keeping its uncertainty visible.

## 5. The agent helps when it supports routines and shows uncertainty

**Central organizing concept:** The agent supports rather than replaces older adults when it reinforces familiar routines, stays easy to use, and treats missing confirmation as uncertainty.

**Constituent subthemes:** Reminders can strengthen an existing routine; Simple prompts support direct use; A missing response does not prove a missed dose.

The reminder was the clearest reported strength of the prototype. Participants valued it when travel, work, visitors, or a changed daily schedule weakened familiar cues. They described it as extra support rather than a replacement for memory, family, notes, or prescriptions. Some also said repeated reminders made the dose time easier to anticipate. These interview accounts evaluate perceived fit, not adherence or system performance, because no app logs or health outcomes are filed.

### Reminders can strengthen an existing routine

Phase 1 correctly showed that support is most useful when routines change. Phase 2 confirmed this pattern during travel, fieldwork, busy periods, and social visits. The prototype added a portable cue where meals, places, and family reminders were less available.

Phase 2 also corrected an earlier assumption. The Phase 1 analysis gave more weight to refusal and the risk that aids could threaten competence. That reading was too narrow. Participants instead often described the reminder as a second layer that reinforced timing while they continued to know their medicines and use familiar routines. Because the interview directly offered memory replacement or retraining as choices, this correction concerns how participants positioned the reminder, not an observed cognitive change. A stronger implementation should reinforce a routine while preserving notes, prescriptions, family support, and the ability to continue without the agent.

### Simple prompts support direct use

The deployed reminder and spoken Bangla service announcement made the system understandable without requiring the older adult to manage every setup task. Participants valued clear timing and asked for short Bangla speech, large text, medicine names or pictures, and very few controls. This supports a direct design direction: show the medicine and time, provide one clear confirmation action, and let the older adult choose sound, voice, vibration, or visual prompts.

Not every requested feature was tested. Medicine images were not deployed, and the data includes a privacy concern about audible prompts in public. Repeated sounds and too much information could also become irritating. A stronger prototype therefore needs selectable prompts and simple language rather than one fixed accessibility mode.

### A missing response does not prove a missed dose

The main implementation weakness was the gap between taking medicine and confirming it on the phone. No response could mean work, conversation, travel, an unheard reminder, a delay, or a dose already taken. A family alert could therefore create worry even when no dose was missed. Phase 1 had already identified this risk, but the prototype did not fully solve it.

The next implementation should record three different states: confirmed as taken, not yet confirmed, and reported as not taken. Before contacting family, the agent should state what is unknown, give the older adult time to respond, allow correction, and send the uncertainty to one chosen caregiver rather than report a missed dose as fact. Medicine changes should reopen human checking against the prescription. Patterned interpretation of silence was not deployed, so this design direction remains grounded in participant reasoning rather than tested automation.

## Care-network design lessons from Bangladesh

The data supports a framework grounded in Bangladeshi households and community relations. It does not justify a framework for the Global South as a homogeneous region. The transferable contribution is a set of care-network questions that can be tested in other interdependent and intermediated settings.

| Design layer | Empirical mechanism | Design requirement | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network membership | Medication work involves older adults, relatives, reciprocal peers, community groups, clinicians, and pharmacists | Let older adults identify which people hold reminder, supply, configuration, consultation, or emergency roles | Treating family as the only collective or giving every relation equal access |
| Agent allegiance | Service moves through announcement, first response, permission, retry, delay, and selected escalation | Make every shift visible, contestable, and revocable for the affected older adult | Equating nonconfirmation with nonadherence or weakening consent silently |
| Community participation | Peer talk cues remembering, questions, and technology trust | Support optional social learning while separating experience sharing from medical advice | Converting social proof into prescriptions or automatic community disclosure |
| Accessible participation | Voice, images, simple controls, proxy setup, and routed information divide interaction work | Assign each task to the person able and accountable to perform it while preserving older adult inspection and refusal | Measuring participation by solitary configuration or making relatives permanent gatekeepers |
| Medication motivation | Habit, prescription, necessity, and relational responsibility precede the score | Keep progress displays optional, comprehensible, private, and secondary to the medication event | Using streaks as adherence evidence, competition, or family performance monitoring |
| Prototype fit | Portable reminders support existing routines, especially when familiar cues are disrupted | Keep reminders simple and selectable while preserving notes, prescriptions, family support, and fallback routines | Treating support as memory replacement or assuming positive comments prove better adherence |
| Accountability | People compare records, interpret context, and restore fallback routines | Expose uncertainty and preserve human verification, clinical authority, and recovery paths | Presenting agent output as medication truth |

This framework generalizes through the contrast between individual configuration and relationally distributed care, not through a claim that all Global South settings share the same household structure. Its roles, access boundaries, modalities, and community relations require local validation.

## Adversarial boundary check

The first theme could still overstate negotiation because several escalation mechanisms were described rather than deployed. The claim is limited to the sequence participants treated as acceptable. The second theme could understate genuine enjoyment of a streak. Secondary interest is retained, but it does not displace habit and prescription as the stated basis of medication taking. The third theme could romanticize community. Peer talk also confused participants, and digital sharing beyond family was frequently refused. The fourth theme could celebrate family setup while hiding dependence. The fifth theme could mistake favorable interview accounts for measured success. It therefore evaluates perceived fit and reported problems, not adherence, usability performance, or health outcomes.
