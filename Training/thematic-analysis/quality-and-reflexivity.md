## 1. The theme-development log

Every structural change to the thematic map gets one row. This is the audit trail; it is what
makes a single-analyst reflexive analysis defensible.

## 2. Quality checklist

Run this before finalizing. Answer each one explicitly in your own head; report the ones the
user needs to see.

**Coding**
- [ ] Every transcript coded in full, not just promising passages
- [ ] Early transcripts recoded after the coding vocabulary developed
- [ ] Codes carry a latent layer, not only semantic labels
- [ ] Disconfirming and contradictory material coded rather than skipped

**Themes**
- [ ] Each theme has one central organizing concept statable in a sentence
- [ ] No theme is a topic bucket or a question from the interview guide in disguise
- [ ] Theme boundaries are distinct; extracts do not sit equally well under two themes
- [ ] Each theme is supported across the dataset in proportion to the weight placed on it
- [ ] No theme rests disproportionately on a single articulate participant
- [ ] Themes together tell a coherent story that answers the research question
- [ ] Themes were split/merged/discarded on stated grounds, not just accumulated

**Interpretation**
- [ ] Frequency is never used as a proxy for importance
- [ ] Every theme answers "so what?"
- [ ] Analytic claims are distinguished from participant claims
- [ ] Alternative readings were considered and the choice among them is stated
- [ ] Limitations and boundary conditions are honest and specific

**Reporting**
- [ ] Extracts are analyzed, not merely displayed
- [ ] Quotes are verbatim, attributed, and anonymized
- [ ] Codebook and theme-development log are available as appendices
- [ ] No fabricated citations

## 3. Reflexivity statement structure

Write 250–500 words covering:

1. **Positionality** — disciplinary background, relationship to the domain, relationship to
   participants (insider/outsider, power asymmetries), and what in that background makes certain
   readings easy and others hard to reach.
2. **Assumptions brought in** — the theoretical commitments and expectations held at the start,
   named plainly.
3. **Where the data resisted** — at least one concrete instance where the analysis went somewhere
   the analyst did not expect, or where a favoured reading had to be abandoned. A reflexivity
   statement with no such instance is decorative.
4. **Decisions and their alternatives** — the two or three consequential analytic choices, the
   roads not taken, and why.
5. **Effects on the account** — what this analysis is therefore well-placed to see, and what it
   is likely to have missed.

Write it in first person and in the past tense. Keep it specific; generic statements about
"acknowledging subjectivity" add nothing.

Where the analysis is produced with AI assistance, say so plainly and describe the division of
labour, since that is now a material part of the audit trail.

## 4. Standing reflexive prompts

Ask these periodically during coding and theme development, and record answers that change
something:

- Whose account am I finding most persuasive, and why that one?
- Which participant am I quietly arguing with?
- What would a researcher from a different tradition see here that I am not seeing?
- Am I coding for what interests me or for what answers the research question?
- If this theme is wrong, what in the data would show it? Have I looked?
- Am I reaching for a familiar HCI construct because it fits, or because it is nearby?

## 5. Common failure modes

- **Topic summaries as themes.** Diagnosis: theme names are nouns; subthemes reproduce the
  interview guide; the "definition" is a list of what participants said about the topic. Fix:
  ask what the extracts share *in meaning*, and rename around that.
- **The bucket theme.** Everything that did not fit elsewhere. Fix: dissolve it and redistribute,
  or admit it is unanalyzed residue and say so.
- **Frequency creep.** "Most participants…", "the majority felt…". Fix: replace with an
  interpretive claim, or state the count and explicitly disclaim importance.
- **Quote dumping.** Fix: apply the claim → extract → interpretation → implication rhythm.
- **Theme count inflation.** Nine themes usually means codes were renamed rather than clustered.
- **Analysis of the interview guide.** If your themes map one-to-one onto the questions asked,
  you have summarized the instrument, not the data.
- **Positivist creep.** Talk of validation, saturation, reliability, and bias-elimination
  imported into a reflexive framework where those concepts do different work.
- **Over-claiming from thin data.** One transcript cannot evidence patterning across a dataset.

## 6. If asked about validity, saturation, or IRR

- **Inter-rater reliability**: not appropriate here. Coding consensus measures the extent to
  which two people can be trained to apply a fixed frame; reflexive TA treats interpretation as
  situated and generative, so agreement is neither achievable nor desirable as a quality
  criterion. Offer instead: audit trail, analytic memos, reflexive journaling, critical-friend
  challenge, member reflection, and transparency about alternative readings.
- **Saturation**: developed within grounded theory around theoretical sampling, and it presumes
  that meaning can be exhausted. Reflexive TA prefers *information power* — sample adequacy
  judged against study aim, specificity of the sample, quality of dialogue, and analytic
  strategy. Say what makes this dataset adequate for this question.
- **Validity**: reframe as trustworthiness and quality of interpretation. The claim is not that
  another analyst would produce the same themes, but that these themes are well-evidenced,
  coherent, transparently derived, and useful.

State these positions matter-of-factly rather than defensively; if the user's supervisor,
reviewer, or client expects a codebook or coding-reliability approach, explain the difference
and help them do that variant properly instead.
