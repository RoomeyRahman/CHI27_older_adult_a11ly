# A Writing Style, Calibrated to CHI

This is the same set of habits as before, tuned upward to the register a CHI paper needs. The mechanics do not change; what changes is how much weight each one is asked to carry. Academic prose at this level has to be denser than general writing, more precise about what it claims, and more explicit about where its claims came from, all without becoming the kind of writing people skim.

The difficulty is that density and engagement pull against each other. Most authors resolve the tension by sacrificing one. A paper that reads easily but claims loosely gets rejected on rigour; a paper that claims carefully but reads like a specification gets rejected because three reviewers never found the contribution. The habits below exist to hold both.

Read this before drafting, and check against the reference table afterward. Checking mid-draft produces sentences written to satisfy a metric, which is worse than either failure mode above.

## Rhythm

Write a long sentence that carries the qualification, then a short one that lands the point. The long sentence holds the conditions, the scope, and the caveats; the short one tells the reader what to take from it. This pairing does more for readability than any other single habit, and it matters more in academic prose than anywhere else, because academic sentences are long by necessity and a reader needs somewhere to rest.

Published CHI prose averages around nineteen words per sentence. That is the target, but the spread matters more than the mean: aim for a standard deviation between six and nine. Ten sentences of nineteen words each will read as machine output regardless of how good the words are. Per ten sentences, expect one or two under twelve words, six or seven in the middle, and two that run past twenty-five.

The short sentence is rarer in academic writing than in general writing, which is exactly why it works. Spend them on the claims you most want a reviewer to remember. "The message text was never modified." "One gap per paragraph." A flat six-word sentence after a thirty-word qualification reads as confidence.

Length past thirty-five words is almost always two sentences that have not been separated. The exception is a genuine list, where the parallel structure carries the reader through. Everything else should be split.

Long words are fine and long clauses are not. Density belongs in the vocabulary, where a precise term saves a clause, rather than in the syntax, where subordination just makes the reader hold more in working memory. This is also a fairness point. CHI reviewers read internationally, and a sentence with three embedded clauses costs a non-native reader far more than it costs you to split it.

## Holding the thread

Link sentences by pointing back at the previous idea rather than repeating the noun that carried it. "This suggests", "because of that", "the same pattern held for", "which is why". Repeating a noun across three consecutive sentences reads as a stall; referring to the idea keeps the argument moving.

In practice roughly half your adjacent sentence pairs will share no content word, and that is correct. Measured on the source paper, that proportion was 46 %, with the connective work done almost entirely by demonstratives and by abstract nouns standing in for whole propositions.

Order information given-before-new. Open each sentence with something the previous sentence established and close it with what you are adding. This is the single most reliable way to make dense material feel like it flows, and it is almost invisible when done well. When a paragraph feels choppy despite short sentences, the cause is usually new information arriving in subject position.

Fix three to five anchor terms for the whole paper. Define each once, use exactly that word every time you mean that thing, and vary everything around them. Synonym variation is a virtue in general prose and a defect in academic prose, because a reader cannot tell whether your second term names the same construct or a different one. Consistency where precision matters, variation everywhere else.

Watch nominalization. Turning verbs into nouns is how academic writing gets abstract without getting precise: "the utilisation of colour for the facilitation of expression" says less than "participants used colour to express tone" and takes longer to say it. Keep the actor and the action in the sentence. Nominalize only when the process itself is the thing you are discussing.

## Defining by contrast

Frame claims against what they are not. This is the signature move of the style and, at CHI, it is also how you position yourself in the literature. The gap in your related work, the framing sentence in your introduction, and the contribution claim in your abstract are all the same construction: rather than X, Y.

The construction works because readers understand new things by their boundaries, and stating the boundary explicitly saves them the inference. It works twice as hard in a paper, because the thing you are contrasting against is usually prior work, so the frame does positioning and definition in one sentence. "Rather than treating emotional expression as something to be inferred, we ask how it can remain something people shape." That sentence defines a construct and stakes a claim against a literature at the same time.

Six to eight per thousand words, spread across sections rather than clustered in the introduction. Use one for the thesis, one for each construct that matters, one for the gap in each related-work subsection, and one at the close. If they all appear in the first page, the paper reads as defensive.

Keep them honest. A contrast frame set against a position nobody holds is a straw man, and reviewers who work in the area you are contrasting against will notice immediately. Name the alternative accurately enough that its advocates would recognise it.

## Paragraphs

A paragraph makes a claim, supports it, and says what the support established.

Open with the claim, and make the claim arguable. "Participants used colour differently at work" is a claim. "This section describes how participants used colour in different settings" is an announcement, and an announcement wastes the position in the paragraph a reader attends to most. Background follows the claim; it does not precede it.

Then two to four sentences of support, which in academic prose means a mechanism, a citation, a number, a quotation, or a worked case. Restatement in different words is not support, and a reviewer reads it as padding. Every paragraph in a CHI paper should be answerable to the question "what is the evidence here", and if the answer is "the previous sentence said it more vaguely", cut one of them.

Close some paragraphs with a sentence naming what has been established. "Together, these themes show", "the upshot is", "what this leaves is". Use it in perhaps half your substantive paragraphs. Used in all of them, the pattern becomes visible, and a visible pattern reads as a template.

Paragraph length should vary the way sentence length does. Three to six sentences is the working range, with the occasional two-sentence paragraph used for emphasis, in the same way and for the same reason as the short sentence.

## Describing what already exists

Any passage covering prior work, competing approaches, or the obvious solution follows one shape. Say what the established line of work achieved. Give a sentence or two on how it plays out. Then the turn, with "however" or "yet" or nothing at all if the contrast is already clear. Say what it does not handle, why that matters here, and what you do about it.

One gap per paragraph. Stacking three complaints against the same body of work reads as an axe being ground rather than an argument being made, and it invites a reviewer who works in that area to defend it.

Be generous before you are critical. A gap identified after a fair account of what prior work accomplished reads as scholarship; the same gap identified after a dismissive summary reads as a failure to have read carefully.

## Who acts

Put the thing that actually acts in the subject position. Participants decide, adjust, abandon, and work around. Systems suggest, render, return, and fail. Write "participants could disable highlighting" rather than "the system allowed participants to disable highlighting", because the second quietly makes your software the protagonist of your own study.

This is not a stylistic preference at CHI. Agency in the grammar shapes what a reader believes was in the participants' control, which is frequently the substance of the finding.

Use first person for research acts you performed: we built, we recruited, we analysed, we argue. Around six mentions per thousand words is enough. Passive voice is not banned and is sometimes correct, particularly when the actor is irrelevant or obvious, but a method section written entirely in the passive hides exactly the decisions transparency requires you to expose. "Participants were recruited" leaves out who recruited them and how.

## Calibrating certainty

Be flat about procedure and observation. Hedge inference. Keeping these distinguishable is the core discipline of academic prose, because a reader who cannot tell what you watched from what you concluded cannot evaluate either.

There is a ladder of verbs, and each rung commits you to a different standard of evidence. _Shows_ and _demonstrates_ need a controlled comparison. _Indicates_ and _points toward_ need a strong, consistent pattern. _Suggests_ and _is consistent with_ need an observed regularity. _Appears_ and _may_ mark genuine uncertainty. Pick the rung your evidence reaches and stay there; over-hedging a result you actually measured is as much a miscalibration as over-claiming one you did not.

One hedge per claim. "This may possibly suggest that colour might support expression" hedges four times and says nothing. Reviewers read stacked hedging as either evasion or as an author who does not know what their data supports.

Reserve causal language for controlled designs. Deployment studies, interviews, and observational work yield association and description, and reviewers are unforgiving about this specific slip because it is the easiest overclaim to make accidentally.

Quantify qualitative patterns coarsely and consistently. Choose one vocabulary and hold it across the whole paper: most, roughly half, several, a few, one participant. Do not switch to percentages on a sample of twenty-four, and do not retreat into "many", which hides whether you counted.

State exact figures once, where they belong, and leave them. Repeating your sample size in three sections signals that you do not trust the reader to have read the first mention.

Say plainly what the work does not cover. A short passage naming the untested conditions, the populations you did not reach, and the claims you are not making does more for credibility than any amount of careful phrasing elsewhere.

## Citations as prose

Citations are part of the sentence, not decoration attached to it. Attach them to specific claims about the literature, position them at clause end rather than mid-clause, and make sure a bracket carrying four references points at something those four papers actually share.

Around twelve to thirteen citation brackets per thousand words is typical for CHI, most carrying two to four references. The number matters less than the integration. A long reference list does not demonstrate engagement, and a paragraph that summarises one paper per sentence demonstrates the opposite of it. Organise by idea, cite several papers per claim, and let the citation do argumentative work.

Never cite your own findings. Citations support claims about what is already known; the moment you attach one to your own result, a reader loses track of what is new.

## Being interesting without hype

Engagement in academic writing comes from stakes and tension, not from adjectives. A paper is interesting when the reader can see what would be lost if the question went unanswered, and when the findings contain something the reader would not have predicted.

So surface the tension rather than smoothing it over. A finding that complicates your own framing is more engaging than one that confirms it, and reporting the participant who abandoned your system, or the context where the design failed, buys more credibility than three more supporting quotations. The strongest sentence in many CHI papers is the one that starts "but this held only when".

Let participants' language do the work in qualitative sections. Their words carry voice that your prose cannot, which is why quotations should be integrated and left unglossed. Paraphrasing a quotation immediately after it appears tells the reader you did not trust it.

The intensifiers do the opposite of what they promise. "Highly novel", "significantly improves", "a powerful framework": each one asks the reader to accept an evaluation instead of showing them the thing being evaluated. Replace the adjective with the evidence and the sentence gets both shorter and more persuasive.

## Tuning by section

The habits hold throughout. What shifts is the mix.

In the introduction and discussion, contrast frames run densest and sentences run longest, because both sections argue. In the method, the prose flattens: short declaratives, exact figures, minimal hedging, agency fully explicit. Findings sit in between, with participants in subject position, quotations carrying the evidence, and coarse quantifiers doing the generalising. Related work leans hardest on the gap structure and on citation integration. Limitations should be the flattest prose in the paper, because hedged limitations read as excuses.

## What to avoid

Hype carries no information and costs trust: leverage, robust, novel, seamless, state-of-the-art, cutting-edge, comprehensive, powerful, crucial, pivotal, delve, landscape, realm, underscore, unlock, harness.

Stacked connectors announce a logical relationship the sentence should be demonstrating: moreover, furthermore, additionally, it is important to note that. Cut all of them. If the connection is real, the content carries it.

Also out: intensifiers that measure nothing, openers about the ever-evolving landscape of anything, rhetorical questions used as section transitions, and em-dashes for parenthetical asides. A comma, a semicolon, or a new sentence does the same work without the theatrical pause.

End on the last real point or on its consequence. A closing paragraph that restates the paper tells the reader their time was wasted.

## Before you send it

- No banned word survives anywhere.
- Six to eight contrast frames per thousand words, spread rather than bunched, and each one names an alternative its advocates would recognise.
- No three consecutive sentences share a length; at least one short flat sentence in every long paragraph.
- Sentences link by reference to the previous idea, and each opens with given information before new.
- Three to five anchor terms are used consistently; no synonym stands in for a defined construct.
- Every paragraph opens with an arguable claim, not an announcement.
- Every paragraph survives the question "what is the evidence here".
- Synthesis closers appear in about half the paragraphs, not all.
- Participants and researchers, not systems, occupy subject position in findings.
- Observation is flat, inference is hedged once, and the two are distinguishable.
- Every verb sits on the rung of the evidence ladder its evidence reaches.
- Causal language appears only where a controlled comparison supports it.
- Coarse quantifiers are consistent; no percentages on small samples.
- Citations attach to claims about the literature, never to your own findings.
- At least one finding complicates the framing rather than confirming it.
- What the work does not cover is stated plainly somewhere.
- The ending is a consequence, not a summary.
