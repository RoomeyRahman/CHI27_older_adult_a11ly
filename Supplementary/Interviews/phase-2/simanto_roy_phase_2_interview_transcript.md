# Phase 2 Interview Transcript

**Participant:** Simanto Roy  
**Age:** 21  
**Occupation:** Software worker

## Phase 2: Post-Deployment Evaluation Interview

### Section 1: Affiliation Ledger & Negotiating Allegiance

**Interviewer:** Now that you have used the medicine assistant for some time, how was it when the assistant clearly said that it was working for you and your parents together?

**Respondent:** At first it was a little different. Because normally I think of an app as something I use myself. Here it was also connected with my parents. But actually it made sense because the medicines are not only my responsibility. We all check for each other.

**Interviewer:** Did you feel it was being transparent about who it was helping?

**Respondent:** Yes, mostly. I liked that it was clear that my parents could get the reminder and I could also know if something was missed. It did not feel like it was secretly doing something. I knew what information was being shared.

**Interviewer:** Did that ever feel intrusive?

**Respondent:** Sometimes I thought about it. Like if my parents take a medicine, I don't need to know every single thing. I only need to know if they have taken it or if there is some problem. If I get every small update then it becomes too much.

**Interviewer:** So what kind of sharing felt acceptable?

**Respondent:** Basic things. Medicine time, whether they confirmed it, and maybe if they missed it. That is enough. I don't need a notification every time they open the app or something.

**Interviewer:** Can you remember a time when the assistant changed from something you used privately to something that became part of the family routine?

**Respondent:** Yes. Earlier I was thinking I will set it up for them and then leave it. But after using it, my mother started listening for the reminder and my father also knew that if he does not confirm then I may get a notification. So it became more of a family thing.

**Interviewer:** Did that make your parents more dependent on you?

**Respondent:** I don't think so. Actually maybe a little less, because the app reminds them directly. I still help when something is unclear, but I don't have to remember every medicine time myself.

**Interviewer:** Did you feel the assistant should act independently or should it depend on family trust?

**Respondent:** Both. It should work on its own for normal reminders. But if there is a medicine change or something important, then family and doctor should be involved. It should not decide everything by itself.

---

### Section 2: Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** When the assistant suggested something you were not comfortable with, did you feel you could stop it?

**Respondent:** Yes. I think there should always be a way to stop or ignore something. Especially with medicine. A reminder is one thing, but if it starts saying take a different medicine, I would not follow that without checking.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever use that kind of control?

**Respondent:** Not for a normal reminder. But there were times when something did not match what we knew from the prescription. Then I would check it instead of just accepting it.

**Interviewer:** How did you react when the assistant became more urgent about a missed medicine?

**Respondent:** I was actually okay with it because of what happened before with my father. If it is one reminder, fine. If he misses it, then I should know. So a stronger alert after a missed dose makes sense.

**Interviewer:** Would repeated alerts become irritating?

**Respondent:** Yes, if it keeps coming again and again. Then people will start ignoring it. It should probably remind once or twice and then tell the family member if it is still not confirmed.

**Interviewer:** What about silence? Suppose your father does not respond to the assistant.

**Respondent:** I would not assume that he has taken it. Earlier we might just think he forgot to answer. Now I would treat no response as something to check. Maybe he is busy, maybe he did not hear it, maybe something else happened.

**Interviewer:** Has that changed the way your family handles medicine?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before, somebody might ask later. Now if there is no confirmation, we know earlier that we should check.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the assistant to interrupt the family for every missed confirmation?

**Respondent:** No. Only when it matters. If it is a normal situation and they just did not press the button, it should not create panic. But if a medicine is important and it stays unconfirmed, then family should know.

**Interviewer:** Does the assistant feel like part of the normal routine now, or does it feel like a warning system?

**Respondent:** Both. Most of the time it is just routine. It gives the reminder and then nothing else happens. The warning part is there when something goes wrong.

---

### Section 3: Care Collectivism & Checking as Love

**Interviewer:** Before using the assistant, how did your family check whether your parents had taken their medicines?

**Respondent:** Mostly by asking. My mother asks my father sometimes. I ask them sometimes. Whoever is at home notices. There was no fixed person.

**Interviewer:** Has that changed after using the assistant?

**Respondent:** Yes. We ask less for the normal doses because the reminder is there. But if there is a missed confirmation, then we check. So the checking is more specific now.

**Interviewer:** Do you miss the old manual checking?

**Respondent:** Not really. I still ask sometimes because that is normal in a family. But I don't miss having to remember that I should ask at a particular time. The app handles that part.

**Interviewer:** Does checking still feel like an expression of care?

**Respondent:** Yes. Definitely. Even if the app reminds them, I still feel responsible because they are my parents. If I get a missed reminder and ask them, that is still care. The app is just helping us do it.

**Interviewer:** What happens when a prescription changes?

**Respondent:** Then we don't just trust the old information. We check the new prescription. If something is unclear, I ask the pharmacy or doctor. The assistant can help keep the information, but it should not replace that discussion.

**Interviewer:** Suppose the assistant says one thing but your mother says she was told something different by the doctor. What would you do?

**Respondent:** I would believe the doctor after checking the prescription. I would also update the assistant. The assistant can be wrong if the prescription changed.

**Interviewer:** Do medicine decisions become more of a family discussion now?

**Respondent:** A little, yes. Before also we discussed because we live together. Now if the assistant shows something different, we talk about it instead of just ignoring it. We check what the latest instruction is.

**Interviewer:** So does the assistant reduce family care or support it?

**Respondent:** Support it. I don't think it can replace the family. It reduces the memory part. The caring part is still from the family.

---

### Section 4: Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** The assistant also has things like points or streaks. What did you think when you saw your parents getting a good score or keeping a streak?

**Respondent:** At first I thought it was a little funny. But then it became something we noticed. If my father had a good streak, we would say okay, you are doing well. It gives a small feeling that he is keeping up with it.

**Interviewer:** Did your family praise or encourage each other because of it?

**Respondent:** Yes, sometimes. Not like a big celebration. Just normal things, like good, you didn't miss it this week. My mother also sometimes notices it.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever feel worried when a streak broke?

**Respondent:** A little, but more because a broken streak could mean a missed medicine. I would first check whether he actually missed the medicine or just forgot to mark it.

**Interviewer:** So the score itself is not the main thing?

**Respondent:** No. The medicine is the main thing. Score is just extra. If people become more worried about keeping the streak than taking the medicine correctly, then it is not useful.

**Interviewer:** Would you prefer the score to be private or shared with the family?

**Respondent:** For my parents I think shared is okay because we already discuss their medicines. But it should not be public or shared outside the family. It is personal health information.

**Interviewer:** Would you want to see your own score too, even though you do not regularly take medicine?

**Respondent:** Not really. For me the useful part is seeing whether my parents are okay. I don't need points for myself.

---

### Section 5: Probationary Trust & Retraining

**Interviewer:** When you first started using the assistant, did you trust it immediately?

**Respondent:** No, not fully. I would check it myself. Since I work in software, I usually want to understand how something is working before depending on it.

**Interviewer:** What did you do during the early period to test it?

**Respondent:** I compared the reminders with the prescription and the timings we already knew. I also checked whether the medicine names were correct. I did not just leave everything to the app from the first day.

**Interviewer:** And after using it for some time?

**Respondent:** Then I trusted the normal reminder more. If it gives the usual reminder at the right time, I don't need to check every time. But for a new medicine I still check.

**Interviewer:** Did the assistant replace your memory of your parents' medicine schedule?

**Respondent:** Not completely. I still know roughly what they take. But I don't try to remember every exact time now. Earlier I might think, did my father take his morning medicine. Now I can check the app if I am unsure.

**Interviewer:** Did that make you less responsible about their medicines?

**Respondent:** No. I think it reduced the pressure a little. Before, if I was busy with work, I would still think I should check. Now I know the assistant will remind them and tell me if there is a problem.

**Interviewer:** Did it change your own sense of time around their medicines?

**Respondent:** Yes, in a small way. I don't keep watching the clock for their medicine. I can focus on my work. If something needs my attention, then I get the notification.

**Interviewer:** So would you say the assistant trained your routine, or replaced your memory?

**Respondent:** More like it trained the routine. It made the timing more fixed. I still know the routine, but I don't have to actively remember every dose.

---

### Section 6: Closing & Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** Finally, who do you think the assistant ultimately works for?

**Respondent:** For both. It works for my parents because they get the reminders. It also works for me because I get to know if something is missed. So it is not only for one person.

**Interviewer:** If you had to choose between serving you or serving your parents, who should come first?

**Respondent:** My parents for the medicine itself. But for the family notification, it should consider both. I don't want information about them coming to me unnecessarily, but I do want to know when there is a real problem.

**Interviewer:** When is it acceptable for a private medicine tool to become a family advocate?

**Respondent:** When the person using it agrees that the family can be involved. In our case it is okay because we already help each other. But it should not automatically involve everyone for every small thing.

**Interviewer:** What would make you stop trusting the assistant?

**Respondent:** If it gives wrong medicine information, sends too many unnecessary alerts, or makes changes without us knowing. Also if I have to keep entering everything manually every day. That would become another job.

**Interviewer:** What is the most useful part of the assistant for your family?

**Respondent:** The shared reminder. My parents get the voice reminder and I only get something when they don't confirm. That is probably the most useful for me.

**Interviewer:** And the prescription reading feature?

**Respondent:** That is also very useful. Sometimes the handwriting is difficult. If I can take a photo and check the medicine name, dose and time, that saves time. But I would still compare it with the prescription or doctor if there is any doubt.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about the assistant, what would it be?

**Respondent:** Keep it simple. Don't add too many things. Voice reminder, missed-dose notification, prescription information, and easy contact for doctor or pharmacy. That is enough for us.

**Interviewer:** So overall, has the assistant changed how your family manages medicines?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before it was mostly memory and whoever happened to be around. Now there is a proper reminder and someone can know if a dose was missed. I think it reduces the pressure on the family without removing the family from it.

**Interviewer:** Anything else you want to add?

**Respondent:** No. I think the main thing is it should support what we already do. Family should still check and make decisions. The assistant should make that easier.

**Interviewer:** Thank you.

**Respondent:** Okay.
