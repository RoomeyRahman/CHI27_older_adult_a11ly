# Phase 2 Interview Transcript

**Participant:** Raju Roy  
**Age:** 31  
**Occupation:** Software engineer

## Phase 2: Post-Deployment Evaluation Interview

### Section 1: Affiliation Ledger & Negotiating Allegiance

**Interviewer:** Now that you have used the medicine assistant, how did you feel when it made it clear that it was helping both you and your mother?

**Respondent:** I think that was good. Because in our case I am also involved in her medicines. So it is not only for her. I am helping with setup and checking also. If the app knows that then it makes more sense.

**Interviewer:** Did you feel comfortable with it knowing about your mother's medicine routine?

**Respondent:** Yes, because she knows about it and I am already helping her. It did not feel like some outside person was watching. But I would still want only the necessary information to be shared.

**Interviewer:** What do you mean by necessary?

**Respondent:** Medicine time, whether she took it or not, maybe if there is a problem. That is enough. I don't need every detail about what she is doing.

**Interviewer:** Did the assistant ever feel intrusive?

**Respondent:** Not really. If it starts giving too many notifications then maybe. Because I am working also. If I get notifications for every small thing then I will stop paying attention.

**Interviewer:** When did it start feeling like part of the family routine rather than just an app?

**Respondent:** After my mother started responding to the voice reminder herself. Before that I was thinking I will have to manage everything. Then she started hearing it and taking the medicine. I only check when there is something to check.

**Interviewer:** Did that make you feel less responsible?

**Respondent:** Less pressure, not less responsibility. I still want to know if she misses it. But I don't have to remember the exact time myself.

**Interviewer:** Would you rather the assistant work independently or depend on family members?

**Respondent:** For normal reminders it should work independently. For medicine changes or anything serious, family should be involved and doctor should decide. The app should not become the decision maker.

---

### Section 2: Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** If the assistant suggested something you did not agree with, would you feel comfortable stopping it?

**Respondent:** Yes. Especially if it is about medicine. I should be able to stop a reminder or correct the information. If the prescription has changed, the old reminder should not continue.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever feel the need to override something?

**Respondent:** Sometimes I checked the information against what we already had. I would not just accept something because the app said it.

**Interviewer:** How did you feel when the assistant became more urgent after a missed dose?

**Respondent:** That is actually useful. My mother forgets quite often, so if she does not respond after the first reminder, another reminder is fine. If she still does not confirm, then I should know.

**Interviewer:** Would you want repeated alerts?

**Respondent:** Not too many. Maybe one reminder again after some time. Then tell me. If it keeps ringing again and again, she will ignore it and I will also start ignoring it.

**Interviewer:** What does silence mean to you? If your mother does not respond to the assistant, would you assume she took the medicine?

**Respondent:** No. Earlier maybe I would. Now I would think maybe she did not hear it or maybe she forgot. So I would check.

**Interviewer:** Would every unconfirmed reminder require you to call her?

**Respondent:** No, not every time. If it is just a missed confirmation and she is busy, it may not mean anything. But if it stays unconfirmed for longer, then yes, I would check.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant changed how you understand a missed response?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before there was no proper system, so I did not know. Now no confirmation itself tells me I may need to check.

**Interviewer:** Does the assistant feel like a normal part of the routine now?

**Respondent:** Yes, mostly. It is normal when it gives the reminder and she takes it. It becomes a warning system only when she does not respond.

---

### Section 3: Care Collectivism & Checking as Love

**Interviewer:** Before the assistant, how did your family check whether your mother had taken her medicines?

**Respondent:** Somebody would ask her. Sometimes me, sometimes my father, sometimes another family member. There was no fixed person.

**Interviewer:** Has that changed?

**Respondent:** Yes. Now the app does the first reminder. So we don't have to keep asking her at night, did you take it, did you take it. If she misses it, then we check.

**Interviewer:** Do you miss the old way of checking?

**Respondent:** No, not really. Asking is still normal. But it was a little stressful because I would think maybe I should ask her and then I might be busy with work. Now the app handles the timing.

**Interviewer:** Does checking still feel like care?

**Respondent:** Yes. If I get a notification that she missed it and I ask her, that is still caring. The app is only helping with the reminder part.

**Interviewer:** What happens when her prescription changes?

**Respondent:** Then I check the new prescription. I don't want the app to keep using the old information. We update it and then start following the new timing.

**Interviewer:** What if the app says one thing but your mother says the doctor told her something else?

**Respondent:** Then I would check the prescription or call the doctor or pharmacist. I would not say the app is correct just because it is the app.

**Interviewer:** So medicine decisions are still discussed within the family?

**Respondent:** Yes. Especially if something is unclear. We talk about it. The app can show what is stored, but if there is a change we discuss it.

**Interviewer:** Does the assistant replace family care?

**Respondent:** No. It reduces the remembering work. The family still does the checking and decisions.

---

### Section 4: Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** What did you think when the assistant showed a good score or a medicine streak for your mother?

**Respondent:** It was a nice extra thing. If she keeps taking it properly, seeing the streak gives some satisfaction. We might say good, you are keeping it properly.

**Interviewer:** Did you or other family members encourage her because of the score?

**Respondent:** Sometimes. Not in a big way. Just normal encouragement. If the streak is good then we notice it.

**Interviewer:** What if the streak breaks?

**Respondent:** First I would check why. Maybe she missed the medicine, maybe she forgot to confirm. If she actually missed it then the important thing is the medicine, not the streak.

**Interviewer:** Would a broken streak make you anxious?

**Respondent:** It could make me check. But I don't want the score to make us panic. It should be a small motivation, not another source of pressure.

**Interviewer:** Would you prefer the score to be private or shared?

**Respondent:** For our family, shared is okay. I think it can encourage her. But it should stay within the family. I don't think health scores should be public.

**Interviewer:** Would you want points for yourself too?

**Respondent:** No, not really. I am not the one taking the regular medicine. I would rather see my mother's progress.

---

### Section 5: Probationary Trust & Retraining

**Interviewer:** When you first started using the assistant, did you trust it immediately?

**Respondent:** No. I wanted to see if it was actually useful. I use software every day, so I know sometimes systems can have mistakes.

**Interviewer:** How did you test it?

**Respondent:** I checked the medicine names and times against what my mother was already taking. I also checked the prescription. For some days I still kept the old written information nearby.

**Interviewer:** Did your trust increase after using it?

**Respondent:** Yes. Once the reminders were coming at the correct time and my mother was actually responding to them, I started trusting the normal routine more.

**Interviewer:** Would you trust it for a new medicine?

**Respondent:** I would check first. If the prescription changes, I want to verify it before depending on the app.

**Interviewer:** Did it replace your memory of your mother's medicine schedule?

**Respondent:** Not completely. I still know she takes two medicines at night. But I don't keep thinking about the exact time all the time. The app takes care of that.

**Interviewer:** Has that reduced your pressure?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before, if I was working and the time was passing, I might suddenly think, did my mother take her medicine. Now I know there is a reminder. If there is no confirmation, then I can check.

**Interviewer:** Did it change your sense of the routine?

**Respondent:** Yes, a little. The medicine time feels more fixed now. My mother also seems to expect the reminder.

**Interviewer:** So did the assistant replace memory or retrain the routine?

**Respondent:** I would say it trained the routine more. It made the timing regular. I still remember the medicine, but I don't have to depend only on memory.

---

### Section 6: Closing & Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** Finally, who do you think the assistant ultimately works for?

**Respondent:** Both of us. It is mainly helping my mother take her medicines, but it also helps me because I know if she misses one.

**Interviewer:** If you had to say who should come first, you or your mother?

**Respondent:** For the medicine, my mother. It is her medicine. But for the family notification, I should be included when there is an actual problem.

**Interviewer:** When is it acceptable for a private medicine tool to become a family advocate?

**Respondent:** When the person using it agrees. In our case my mother is okay with me knowing if she misses a medicine because I already help her. But I don't think it should automatically tell the whole family everything.

**Interviewer:** What would make you stop trusting the assistant?

**Respondent:** Wrong medicine information. Or if it keeps giving unnecessary alerts. Or if it changes something without telling us. And if it becomes too much work to maintain, then we probably won't use it.

**Interviewer:** What is the most useful part for you?

**Respondent:** The shared reminder. My mother gets the voice reminder and I get a notification only if she does not confirm. That is the main thing.

**Interviewer:** What about prescription reading?

**Respondent:** Very useful. My mother cannot read prescriptions properly. If I can take a picture and the app tells me the medicine name, dose and time, it will save us from going to the pharmacy just to understand the writing. But I would still check if there is any doubt.

**Interviewer:** Would pictures of the medicines help?

**Respondent:** Yes. She already recognizes medicines by packet and tablet, so a picture would help her identify them.

**Interviewer:** What about large text and voice?

**Respondent:** Large text is good, but voice is more important for her. She may not look at the phone when she is doing household work. If it speaks, she can hear it.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the assistant to help when you travel?

**Respondent:** Yes. That is one of the times she forgets more. It can remind us to carry the medicine before leaving and remind her even when the routine is different.

**Interviewer:** What about family events and guests?

**Respondent:** Same thing. Those are the times when she gets busy and forgets. A voice reminder would help because somebody does not have to remember to remind her.

**Interviewer:** Would you want medicine refill reminders too?

**Respondent:** Yes. Sometimes we only notice when the strip is almost finished. A reminder before that would be useful.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about the assistant, what would you change?

**Respondent:** I would keep it simple. Set the medicine once, set the time, voice reminder, and then family alert if it is missed. Prescription reading also. I don't want too many things.

**Interviewer:** So overall, has the assistant changed how your family manages medicines?

**Respondent:** Yes. Earlier it was mostly my mother remembering and family asking her. Now the assistant does the first reminder. We still check when needed. So there is less pressure but family is still involved.

**Interviewer:** Anything else you want to add?

**Respondent:** No. Just that it should help us, not make another job for us. If it stays simple, I think it is useful.

**Interviewer:** Thank you.

**Respondent:** Okay.
