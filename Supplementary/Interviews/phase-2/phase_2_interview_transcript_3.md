# Phase 2 Interview Transcript

**Participant:** Not stated  
**Age:** 69  
**Gender:** Male

## Phase 2: Post-Deployment Evaluation Interview

### Section 1: Affiliation Ledger & Negotiating Allegiance

**Interviewer:** Now that you have used the medicine assistant, how do you feel about it helping you and also your wife and son with your medicines?

**Respondent:** It is useful. At home my wife already reminds me. My son knows the medicine names and keeps the prescription. So if the assistant helps with that also, it is okay.

**Interviewer:** The assistant can work for you and also for the family members who help you. Does that feel comfortable?

**Respondent:** Yes. They already know about my medicine. I don't feel it is something private from them. But it should not tell them every small thing. If I take the medicine, no need to send message to everyone.

**Interviewer:** What would be useful for your family to know?

**Respondent:** If I missed it. Or if I didn't confirm for a long time. Then my son can call. If medicine is finishing also, that is useful.

**Interviewer:** Did it ever feel like the assistant was interfering too much?

**Respondent:** No, not when it is simple. If there are too many sounds or messages, then I don't like it. Phone already has many things.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant become part of your family routine?

**Respondent:** Yes, a little. Before my wife would ask me, did you take the morning one. Now the reminder comes and I check. If I don't respond, then family can know.

**Interviewer:** Do you feel more independent because of it?

**Respondent:** Maybe for remembering. I can take the medicine myself. The problem is remembering sometimes, especially when I go outside. The assistant helps there.

**Interviewer:** Should the assistant make medicine decisions by itself?

**Respondent:** No. Doctor decides. If medicine changes, my son should check. The assistant should remind us what was decided.

---

### Section 2: Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** If the assistant tells you to take a medicine but you think something is wrong, what would you do?

**Respondent:** I would stop and ask my son. If he is not there, I would ask the pharmacy or doctor. I don't take another medicine just because the phone says it.

**Interviewer:** Would you feel comfortable overriding the assistant?

**Respondent:** Yes. It should be possible. If there is a mistake, I should be able to say no.

**Interviewer:** What if you miss the first reminder?

**Respondent:** It can remind again. Sometimes I am outside or talking and I don't hear. One more reminder is good.

**Interviewer:** Would you want a stronger alert if you still do not respond?

**Respondent:** Yes, but not too much. After one or two reminders, tell my son. Then he can call me.

**Interviewer:** If you do not confirm the reminder, does that always mean you missed the medicine?

**Respondent:** No. I may have taken it and forgotten to press. So don't assume immediately.

**Interviewer:** How should the assistant handle that?

**Respondent:** Give me some time. If still no response, then tell family to check.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the whole family to be alerted?

**Respondent:** No. Son is enough. Wife may also know because she is at home, but no need to alert everybody.

**Interviewer:** Does the assistant now feel like part of your normal routine or a warning system?

**Respondent:** Mostly normal routine. Warning is only when I miss something.

---

### Section 3: Care Collectivism & “Checking as Love”

**Interviewer:** Before the assistant, how did your family check your medicine?

**Respondent:** My wife asks. Sometimes my son asks. If I am going somewhere, my wife tells me to carry the medicine. If I come home and see it still there, then I know I forgot.

**Interviewer:** Has that changed after using the assistant?

**Respondent:** Yes. Now the phone does the first reminder. My wife does not have to remember every time.

**Interviewer:** Do you miss your wife reminding you?

**Respondent:** No, she still does sometimes. That is normal. But she has other work. If the phone reminds me, it helps her also.

**Interviewer:** Does your wife's checking still feel like care?

**Respondent:** Yes. Of course. She knows my daily routine. Phone does not know if I have eaten or if I am outside. So her reminder is different.

**Interviewer:** What about your son?

**Respondent:** He knows the medicine and doctor things. If there is a new medicine, he checks it. He remembers what doctor said better than me.

**Interviewer:** If the assistant has an old prescription but your son says the doctor changed it, what would you do?

**Respondent:** I would follow the new prescription after checking. The old information should be changed in the assistant.

**Interviewer:** Would you discuss a medicine change with your family?

**Respondent:** Yes. Because everybody needs to know if the routine changes. Otherwise my wife may remind me about the old one.

**Interviewer:** Does the assistant replace the family?

**Respondent:** No. It only helps with the remembering. Family still checks and decides.

---

### Section 4: Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** The assistant shows points or a streak when you take medicines regularly. What do you think about that?

**Respondent:** It is okay. If I see that I have taken it regularly, that is good. It gives some encouragement.

**Interviewer:** Would you show the streak to your wife or son?

**Respondent:** Yes, that is okay. They are already involved. My wife may say good, you are taking properly.

**Interviewer:** Would you feel bad if the streak broke?

**Respondent:** I would check why. Maybe I actually missed the medicine. Maybe I forgot to confirm. First check that.

**Interviewer:** Would the broken streak make you anxious?

**Respondent:** Not because of the points. If I missed the medicine, then I worry about that. The score is not the important thing.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the family to encourage you when the streak is good?

**Respondent:** Yes, small encouragement is fine. But don't make it like a competition.

**Interviewer:** Would you want your score shared with other people in your group?

**Respondent:** No need. Our medicine is our business. Family can see, other people don't need to.

---

### Section 5: Probationary Trust & Retraining

**Interviewer:** When you first started using the assistant, did you trust it immediately?

**Respondent:** No. My son checked it first. I also watched what it was saying. If it tells the right medicine at the right time, then slowly you trust it.

**Interviewer:** What did your son check?

**Respondent:** He checked the medicine names and timings with the prescription. He knows those things better.

**Interviewer:** Did your trust increase after using it?

**Respondent:** Yes. After some time, when it kept reminding me correctly, I became used to it.

**Interviewer:** Would you trust it immediately after a medicine change?

**Respondent:** No. New medicine means check again. I don't remember all the names. My son should update it.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant replaced your memory of your medicine routine?

**Respondent:** No. I still know some by looking. But exact time I don't always remember. The reminder helps.

**Interviewer:** Has it changed the way you think about your medicine times?

**Respondent:** Yes. It makes the timing more regular. At home I already use food time to remember, but when I go outside the phone is more useful.

**Interviewer:** Has that reduced your pressure?

**Respondent:** Yes, somewhat. Earlier my wife or I had to remember. Now the phone reminds first.

**Interviewer:** Has it made you more independent?

**Respondent:** For taking the medicine, yes. I already take it myself. For knowing the medicine and prescription, I still need my son.

---

### Section 6: Closing & Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** Finally, who do you think the assistant works for?

**Respondent:** For me and the family. It reminds me, but it also helps my wife and son know if I have forgotten.

**Interviewer:** Who should come first?

**Respondent:** Me for my medicine. But if there is a problem, family should know.

**Interviewer:** When is it acceptable for a private medicine tool to involve your family?

**Respondent:** If I miss a medicine or don't respond. Also if medicine is finishing. Those are important. No need to tell them every time I take it.

**Interviewer:** What would make you stop trusting the assistant?

**Respondent:** Wrong medicine, wrong timing, too many alerts. If it changes something without telling us, also bad.

**Interviewer:** What is the most useful part for you?

**Respondent:** Reminder. Especially when I am outside. At home my wife can remind me, but outside I may forget. The phone can remind me there.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the reminder to include the medicine picture?

**Respondent:** Yes. That would help because I don't remember all the names. If I see the strip picture, I know which one.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the medicine name too?

**Respondent:** Yes, name and picture. Then I can check.

**Interviewer:** What about voice?

**Respondent:** Yes. Voice is good. Just say medicine time and which one. Not too much talking.

**Interviewer:** What about the written information?

**Respondent:** Big writing is better. Small writing is difficult. I can read some with glasses but not everything.

**Interviewer:** Would prescription storage be useful?

**Respondent:** Yes. My son already keeps the prescription. If it is in the phone, then we can check the latest one.

**Interviewer:** What if the assistant could read a prescription from a photo?

**Respondent:** That would be useful. My son can take the photo and check what is written. But it has to be correct. If there is doubt, we ask the doctor or pharmacy.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the assistant to tell you why you take each medicine?

**Respondent:** Sometimes yes. But not every time. Just saying BP medicine or night medicine is enough. Too much information will confuse me.

**Interviewer:** Would you want a reminder when medicine is almost finished?

**Respondent:** Yes. I don't count tablets. If it tells my son before it finishes, then he can buy.

**Interviewer:** Would that help before you go somewhere?

**Respondent:** Yes. It can remind me to carry the medicine. That is useful because sometimes I say I will take it and then leave it at home.

**Interviewer:** What about family gatherings?

**Respondent:** That is exactly when I forget. Food time changes, people are talking, then medicine is forgotten. If the phone reminds me, good.

**Interviewer:** What about your morning group where people talk about medicines?

**Respondent:** That also affects me. If someone says they have to take their tablet, sometimes I remember mine. If many people say a medicine is good, I also become interested. But I still ask the doctor before changing anything.

**Interviewer:** So group opinion can make you consider something, but not decide it?

**Respondent:** Yes. People I know influence me. If many people say the same thing, I think maybe it is useful. But doctor has to say whether it is for me.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the assistant to recommend medicines based on what other people take?

**Respondent:** No. It can tell me about my own medicine. It should not tell me to take something because other people use it.

**Interviewer:** If the assistant and your family disagree about a medicine, who should decide?

**Respondent:** Doctor. Family can check and discuss. Assistant can show the information. But final medicine decision is doctor.

**Interviewer:** What kind of app would you continue using?

**Respondent:** Simple one. Big writing, medicine picture, voice reminder, one button to say taken. Family notification if I miss. That's enough.

**Interviewer:** Would you want many features?

**Respondent:** No. Too many features make it difficult. I don't need everything in the phone.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant changed how your family manages your medicines?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before it was mostly memory and family reminders. Now the phone does the first reminder. Family still checks when needed. So it helps without taking over.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about the assistant, what would you change?

**Respondent:** Make it simpler and better for when I am outside. Reminder should work wherever I am. And family should know if I miss it.

**Interviewer:** Anything else you want to add?

**Respondent:** No. The main thing is medicine should not be guessed. Reminder is good. Family help is good. Doctor decides the medicine.

**Interviewer:** Thank you.

**Respondent:** Okay.
