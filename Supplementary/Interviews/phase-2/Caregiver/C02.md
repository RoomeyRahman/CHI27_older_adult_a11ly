**Janntul Tajrin:** "That can be useful. If something is actually important, I want to know. But it should tell me what the problem is. Just making a big alarm without explaining is not enough."

**Interviewer:** "Did it ever repeat or escalate an alert after you had dealt with it?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "First remind them again after some time. Sometimes they are busy or they may not notice the phone. If they still don't take it, then maybe tell me."

**Interviewer:** "What happened when nobody answered a reminder?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "No, not every time. That will be too much. One reminder, then another one, and if it is still missed then tell the family. Something like that."

**Interviewer:** "When you saw that a reminder had not been answered, what did you assume?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "If someone is just a little late, that can happen. But if they completely miss it, keep missing it, or take the wrong medicine, then that is more serious. The system should make that difference clear."

## 7. Follow-up Reflection from the Caregiver's Perspective

**Interviewer:** "I just have a few more questions. From your side as the person who mostly manages the medicines, how is this smart reminder different from a normal phone alarm?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "A normal alarm only rings at a fixed time. This one can do more than that. It can tell the older person which medicine to take, and if they still do not respond after some time, then it can let me know. That is more useful, especially when I am working."

**Interviewer:** "So what should it do that a normal alarm cannot do?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "It should first remind them clearly, maybe with voice if that is easier. Then if there is still no response, it can remind again. Only after that should it involve me. It should not treat every small delay like an emergency."

**Interviewer:** "Why is that important from your side?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "Because sometimes they may be a little late, busy, or maybe they already took the medicine and forgot to confirm. If every small thing comes to me as an alert, I will start ignoring the notifications. I need to know which one actually needs attention."

**Interviewer:** "How can this system make things easier for you as a caregiver?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "The biggest thing is that I don't have to keep every medicine time in my head. My grandfather takes several medicines at different times, so that is a lot to remember. If the system handles the routine reminders, I only have to step in when something goes wrong."

**Interviewer:** "What kind of things would you want it to inform you about?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "Repeated missed medicine, a wrong medicine, no response for a long time, maybe medicine finishing, or a change in the schedule. Those are useful. I don't need a notification every time someone takes a tablet."

**Interviewer:** "Would that reduce some pressure on you?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "Yes, definitely. I have my own work also. Before, part of my attention was always on remembering who has to take what and when. If the routine part is handled properly, then I can focus on the situations that actually need me."

**Interviewer:** "Do you think this system can solve all the problems you face while managing their medicines?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "No, definitely not all. It can help with timing and remembering. But if there is a new medicine, a side effect, a confusing prescription, or something the doctor changed, then a person still has to check."

**Interviewer:** "What would you still need to do yourself?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "I would still compare the medicine with the prescription, update the schedule when something changes, talk to the doctor or pharmacist when there is confusion, and make sure the information in the system is correct."

**Interviewer:** "If the system says one thing but the prescription says something different, what would you trust?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "The prescription and whatever the doctor or pharmacist confirmed. I would stop and check before anyone takes anything. The system should never be the final authority."

**Interviewer:** "Is there anything the system should never decide by itself?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "Yes. It should not change a medicine, change a dose, stop a medicine, or recommend a new one by itself. Those are medical decisions."

**Interviewer:** "Then what should the actual role of the system be for the older adult?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "For them, it should be very simple. Tell them which medicine, when to take it, maybe show a picture or use voice, and let them confirm easily."

**Interviewer:** "And what should its role be for you as the caregiver?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "For me, it should reduce the checking work. I should be able to see the schedule, update it when needed, and get an alert only when there is something important that needs my attention."

**Interviewer:** "Would you want to monitor every small action they take?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "No. That is not necessary. They should still be able to manage normal medicine-taking themselves. I only need to be involved when there is a real issue."

**Interviewer:** "What should the family still do even if the system is working well?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "We still need to talk to them, notice if they are feeling unwell, bring the medicines, and help when something changes. The app cannot understand the whole family situation."

**Interviewer:** "And what should the doctor or pharmacist still do?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "They should make or confirm the medical decisions. The system should help us follow those decisions accurately."

**Interviewer:** "If you had to describe the system's main role in one sentence, what would you say?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "It should handle the routine reminders and show me clearly when something actually needs my attention."

**Interviewer:** "Anything else you would want to change?"

**Janntul Tajrin:** "Make the alerts meaningful and the older person's side very simple. If it gives too many unnecessary notifications or complicated screens, then both the caregiver and the older person will stop using it."

**Interviewer:** "Thank you for your time."

**Janntul Tajrin:** "Thank you."
