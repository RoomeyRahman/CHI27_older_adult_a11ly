# Phase 2 Follow-Up Interview Transcript: Medication Management

**Participant:** Sudarson Saha  
**Age:** 25

---

## 1. Affiliation Ledger & Negotiating Allegiance

**Interviewer:** "So, after using the medication support agent for some time, how did you feel when it made it clear who it was helping or who it was responsible for?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yeah, I noticed that. Since I am the one mainly handling my grandfather's medicines, I mostly felt like it was helping me to manage his medicines. It was clear enough."

**Interviewer:** "Did that feel transparent to you, or did it sometimes feel like the agent was getting too involved in family matters?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "No, I didn't feel it was getting too involved. Most of the time it was just reminding me about the medicine. That is what I wanted also. If it starts going into other family things, then maybe it would feel different, but for medicine it was okay."

**Interviewer:** "Was there a time when the agent changed from being something you used privately to something that became more shared with the family?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, I think when other family members started knowing about the reminders and what was happening. Since three people in the family take medicine, sometimes it is useful if someone else knows what is going on, especially if I am not around. But I would still want to be the main person handling it."

**Interviewer:** "Would you say you prefer the agent to work independently, or do you prefer it to work together with you and the family?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Together, I would say. It can do the reminder part by itself, but for decisions I would want a person to be involved. Especially with my grandfather, I don't think the system should decide everything on its own."

---

## 2. Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** "If the agent gave you an alert saying your grandfather's medicine needed attention, would you feel comfortable stopping or overriding what it suggested?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, if I knew there was a reason. I would check the medicine label or prescription information first. If something looks wrong, then yes, I would not just follow the reminder."

**Interviewer:** "What if the agent started giving stronger or more noticeable warnings because it thought the risk was increasing?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "I think that would be useful, actually. If it is something important, I would rather have it clearly tell me. But it should also tell me why it is giving the warning, not just make a loud alert without explaining."

**Interviewer:** "And if there was silence from you or your grandfather after an alert, how should the system handle that?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "If nobody responds, then maybe it should remind again. Because sometimes I can be busy and miss the first one. If there is still no response, then maybe another family member can be informed. But I would not want it to keep doing that for every small thing."

**Interviewer:** "How would you know whether the system is helping with the normal routine or whether something has actually gone wrong?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "The normal thing is just a reminder, I take the medicine and that is it. If there is a missed medicine, repeated missed reminders, or something different from the usual schedule, then I would consider that a problem. It should make that difference clear."

---

## 3. Care Collectivism & "Checking as Love"

**Interviewer:** "Has having the agent changed how your family checks on your grandfather's medication?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "A little, yes. Earlier I had to remember and remind him myself. Now the reminder can come automatically, so I don't have to keep thinking about the exact time all the time. But I still check with him."

**Interviewer:** "Do you miss those manual check-ins, or has the technology simply given you another reason to care?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "No, I don't think I miss them exactly. The checking is still there. The difference is I don't have to depend only on my memory. So I can spend less time worrying whether I forgot something."

**Interviewer:** "Suppose there was some confusion about a prescription or your grandfather suddenly needed a change in his medicine. Would you want the agent to handle that, or would you discuss it with the family first?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "I would discuss it. Definitely. The agent can tell me that there is a difference or remind me that something has changed, but if there is a new prescription or a sudden health decision, I would check the doctor's instruction and talk with the family. I wouldn't just change the medicine because the system says so."

**Interviewer:** "So the technology helps with remembering, but the family still stays involved in decisions?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, exactly. That would be better."

---

## 4. Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** "If the system showed a high score or a long streak for keeping up with your grandfather's medicines, how do you think your family would react?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "They would probably be happy. Maybe they would say, okay, good, everything is being managed properly. I don't think the score itself is the main thing. It is more like a sign that we are not missing the medicines."

**Interviewer:** "Would praise or encouragement from the system or from family members make you want to continue?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, probably. Even a simple message saying everything is on track would be nice. And if family members notice that also, it gives some encouragement."

**Interviewer:** "What if a streak was broken because a medicine was missed? Would that make you anxious or would you see it as a reason to try again?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "I would not want to become too worried about the streak. If one medicine was missed, the important thing is to understand why it happened and make sure it doesn't happen again. The score should not become more important than the medicine."

**Interviewer:** "Would you prefer the score to stay private or be visible to the family?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "For something like medication management, I think sharing basic progress with the family is okay. But I don't think every small detail needs to be shown. Just enough so they know things are being managed."

---

## 5. Probationary Trust & Retraining

**Interviewer:** "When you first started using the agent, did you feel like you had to test it before trusting it?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, definitely. I wouldn't immediately trust a new system with ten medicines. At first I would compare its reminders with the medicine label and the prescription information. If it keeps giving the correct reminders, then slowly I would trust it more."

**Interviewer:** "Did that early testing change how you used it?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes. In the beginning I would check everything myself. After some time, if I saw that the reminders were matching what I already knew, I could depend on them more. But I would still keep the medicine information available."

**Interviewer:** "Do you think the agent replaced your need to remember the schedule, or did it change your own sense of when medicines were due?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Mostly it reduced the need for me to remember every time. I still know the general routine, but now I don't have to keep checking the clock. If the phone or agent reminds me, I can act then. So yes, it takes some pressure away from my memory."

**Interviewer:** "Would you still want the original medicine labels or prescription information nearby?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, definitely. I would keep them. The reminder is helpful, but the prescription and medicine information are still the main reference."

---

## 6. Closing & Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** "After using it, who do you think the agent ultimately works for—just you, your grandfather, or the whole family?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "I would say both me and my grandfather, and in a way the family also. I am the one managing it, so it helps me. But the reason for doing all this is my grandfather's medicine. And if I am not around, other family members may need to help."

**Interviewer:** "Would you be comfortable with the agent becoming a family advocate in some situations, rather than staying only as your private tool?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "For important things, yes. If there is a missed medicine or something that really needs attention, then informing the family can be useful. But for normal reminders, I don't think everyone needs to be involved."

**Interviewer:** "So where would you draw the line between a private reminder and something that should involve the family?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "If it is just reminding me to give the medicine, that can stay with me. If there is a repeated problem, a missed dose, or some issue that I cannot handle myself, then it should involve someone else. That makes sense to me."

**Interviewer:** "Overall, after using the agent, do you feel it has made managing your grandfather's medicines easier?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "Yes, I think so. Especially because he takes around ten medicines. Having clear reminders means I don't have to remember every single timing myself. But I would still check the prescription and medicine information, and I would still be responsible for making sure he gets the right medicine."

**Interviewer:** "Anything else you would like to add?"

**Sudarson Saha:** "No, I think that's pretty much it. The main thing for me is that the reminders should be clear and reliable. If it does that properly, then it is helpful."
