# Phase 2 Interview Transcript

**Participant:** Not stated  
**Age:** 66  
**Gender:** Female

## Phase 2: Post-Deployment Evaluation Interview

### Section 1: Affiliation Ledger & Negotiating Allegiance

**Interviewer:** Now that you have used the medicine assistant, how do you feel about it helping you and also your family with your medicines?

**Respondent:** It is good. My husband and daughter already help me. So if this also helps, then it is okay.

**Interviewer:** The assistant can help you directly and also let your daughter or husband know when something is missed. How does that feel?

**Respondent:** That is useful. My daughter knows more about my medicines. My husband also reminds me. So if they know when I forget, then they can ask me.

**Interviewer:** Would it feel intrusive if your family got every medicine update?

**Respondent:** Every time, no need. If I take it, then finished. They don't need a message for everything. If I don't take it or there is some problem, then tell them.

**Interviewer:** So you are comfortable with some information being shared?

**Respondent:** Yes. They already know my medicines. I am okay with that. But too much information is not needed.

**Interviewer:** Did the assistant feel like a private tool at first?

**Respondent:** Yes, at first it was just something on the phone. Then it became part of the routine because my daughter was also checking it.

**Interviewer:** Would you want to manage the assistant yourself?

**Respondent:** My daughter should set it. I can use the reminder. I don't know how to put all the medicines and times.

**Interviewer:** Would you still want control over your medicines?

**Respondent:** Yes. I take the medicine myself. But if something changes, my daughter should check and tell me.

---

### Section 2: Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** If the assistant tells you to take a medicine and you think it is not the right one, what would you do?

**Respondent:** I would not take it directly. I would ask my daughter or husband. They can check the prescription.

**Interviewer:** Would you feel comfortable stopping a reminder if it was wrong?

**Respondent:** Yes. If it is wrong, stop it. I don't want to take something just because the phone says so.

**Interviewer:** What if you do not respond to the first reminder?

**Respondent:** It can remind me again. Sometimes I am busy or talking. I may not hear it.

**Interviewer:** How many reminders would be enough?

**Respondent:** One more is enough. If I still don't answer, tell my daughter.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the assistant to become more urgent when a medicine is missed?

**Respondent:** If it is important, yes. But not shouting or making too much sound. It should just tell my daughter that I have not confirmed.

**Interviewer:** If you do not press the confirmation button, does that always mean you missed the medicine?

**Respondent:** No. Sometimes I take it and forget to press. That can happen.

**Interviewer:** What should happen then?

**Respondent:** My daughter can ask me. The app should not immediately say I did not take it.

**Interviewer:** So silence should mean that someone needs to check, not that something definitely went wrong?

**Respondent:** Yes yes. Check first.

---

### Section 3: Care Collectivism & “Checking as Love”

**Interviewer:** Before using the assistant, how did your family remind you about medicines?

**Respondent:** My husband asks me. My daughter also tells me. If I am at home and they are there, somebody usually remembers.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant changed that?

**Respondent:** Yes, they don't have to remember every time now. The phone reminds me first. If I don't take it, then they can check.

**Interviewer:** Do you miss the family reminders?

**Respondent:** No. They still remind me sometimes. But they have their own work. It is better if the phone does the first reminder.

**Interviewer:** Does your family checking still feel like care?

**Respondent:** Yes. They ask because they care. My husband also takes medicines, so we remind each other sometimes.

**Interviewer:** What happens if your doctor changes one of your medicines?

**Respondent:** My daughter tells me. She knows the prescription. If one old medicine is stopped and a new one comes, she explains it.

**Interviewer:** Would you change the assistant yourself after a prescription change?

**Respondent:** No. My daughter should do it. I don't want to put the wrong thing.

**Interviewer:** What if the assistant says to take the old medicine but your daughter says the doctor changed it?

**Respondent:** Then I listen to my daughter after she checks with the doctor. The doctor decides the medicine.

**Interviewer:** Would you discuss medicine changes with your family?

**Respondent:** Yes. Because they keep the prescription and bring the medicines. They need to know.

**Interviewer:** So does the assistant replace family care?

**Respondent:** No. It only helps with remembering. Family still helps with the medicine.

---

### Section 4: Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** The assistant shows points or a streak when you take medicines regularly. What do you think about that?

**Respondent:** It is okay. I don't need many points. If it shows I am taking regularly, that is good.

**Interviewer:** Would you feel happy if you had a long streak?

**Respondent:** Yes, maybe. It means I am doing properly.

**Interviewer:** If your daughter saw that, would that be useful?

**Respondent:** Yes. She can see I am taking the medicines. She may say good, keep doing it.

**Interviewer:** Would you want your husband to see it too?

**Respondent:** He can see. We already remind each other.

**Interviewer:** What if your streak breaks?

**Respondent:** Then I would check why. Maybe I forgot the medicine. Maybe I took it but forgot to press. First check.

**Interviewer:** Would the broken streak make you worried?

**Respondent:** Not because of the number. If I missed the medicine, then I worry about that. The points are not important.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the score shared outside your family?

**Respondent:** No. Why should other people know. Family is enough.

**Interviewer:** Should the assistant make the streak very important?

**Respondent:** No. Medicine is important, not the score.

---

### Section 5: Probationary Trust & Retraining

**Interviewer:** When you first started using the assistant, did you trust it immediately?

**Respondent:** No. My daughter showed me. I was checking whether it was saying the right medicine.

**Interviewer:** What did you check?

**Respondent:** The medicine and the time. My daughter compared it with the prescription.

**Interviewer:** After using it for some time, did you become more comfortable?

**Respondent:** Yes. When it gives the right reminder, I know what to do. It became easier.

**Interviewer:** Do you still check when something changes?

**Respondent:** Yes. If the packet is different or the medicine is new, I ask. I don't take a new one without checking.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant replaced your memory?

**Respondent:** No. I still know some medicines by the strip and colour. But I forget sometimes, especially when people come or I go somewhere.

**Interviewer:** Has it changed your sense of timing?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before I remembered mostly from tea, food, dinner. Now the reminder also tells me. So it is easier.

**Interviewer:** Do you feel less pressure on your family?

**Respondent:** Yes. My daughter does not have to remember every time. The phone can tell me first. She only needs to check if there is a problem.

**Interviewer:** Does that mean the assistant has made you more independent?

**Respondent:** A little. I still take the medicine myself. I need them for checking and arranging, but I don't need someone standing there every time.

---

### Section 6: Closing & Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** Finally, who do you think the assistant works for?

**Respondent:** For me and my family. It helps me remember, and it helps them know if I forget.

**Interviewer:** Who should come first?

**Respondent:** Me for my medicine. But if there is a problem, family should know.

**Interviewer:** When is it okay for the assistant to involve the family?

**Respondent:** If I don't take the medicine, if I don't respond for a long time, or if medicine is finishing. Those are useful things to tell them.

**Interviewer:** Should the family get a message every time you take your medicine?

**Respondent:** No no. That is too much. Only if something is wrong.

**Interviewer:** What would make you stop trusting the assistant?

**Respondent:** If it tells me the wrong medicine. Or wrong time. Or if it keeps making too much noise. If it is difficult, I will not use it.

**Interviewer:** What is the most useful part for you?

**Respondent:** The reminder. Voice is good. If it says medicine time, I understand.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the voice to use simple language?

**Respondent:** Yes. Simple words. Not too much talking.

**Interviewer:** Would you want it to show the medicine picture?

**Respondent:** Yes. That would help. I know my medicines by the strip and colour. If I see the picture, I can compare.

**Interviewer:** What about large writing?

**Respondent:** Yes, big writing is better. Small letters are difficult, especially without glasses.

**Interviewer:** Would you want different pictures for morning and night?

**Respondent:** Yes. Morning and night can have different pictures. Then I can understand quickly.

**Interviewer:** Would you want your daughter to set everything up?

**Respondent:** Yes. She knows the phone. She can put all the medicine names and times. I don't want to type all that.

**Interviewer:** What should you have to do when the reminder comes?

**Respondent:** Just take the medicine and press one big button. Taken. Nothing more.

**Interviewer:** What if you forget to press the button?

**Respondent:** Then my daughter can ask me. It should not assume I missed the medicine.

**Interviewer:** Would prescription storage be useful?

**Respondent:** Yes. My daughter already keeps the prescription in her phone. If it is in the assistant also, then she can check it easily.

**Interviewer:** Would prescription reading from a photo be useful?

**Respondent:** Yes, maybe. If it can read the doctor's writing and show the medicine clearly, that would help my daughter. For me also if it shows in big writing.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the assistant to remind your daughter when medicine is almost finished?

**Respondent:** Yes. That would be very good. Sometimes we notice when only few tablets are left. If it tells her before, she can buy it.

**Interviewer:** Would that also help when you are going somewhere?

**Respondent:** Yes. It can remind us to take medicine before going. Sometimes when visiting someone, the routine changes and I forget.

**Interviewer:** What about family gatherings and guests?

**Respondent:** That is when I can forget. We are talking, tea comes, people are there. Then medicine time goes. Voice reminder would help.

**Interviewer:** If other people talk about medicines, does that affect you?

**Respondent:** People talk, yes. Somebody says this medicine is good. Somebody says that one helped them. I listen, but I don't take it because they said. I ask my daughter.

**Interviewer:** So would you want the assistant to recommend medicines based on what other people use?

**Respondent:** No. No, doctor should decide. The assistant can tell me about my medicine, not tell me to take somebody else's medicine.

**Interviewer:** Has the assistant changed how your family manages your medicines?

**Respondent:** Yes. Before my husband and daughter had to remember and tell me. Now the phone tells me first. They still help when I need them.

**Interviewer:** What would make the assistant easiest for you to use?

**Respondent:** Big writing. Simple voice. Medicine picture. One button. My daughter sets it up. That is enough.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing about it, what would you change?

**Respondent:** Make it simple. No many buttons. No difficult words. Just tell me medicine time and tell my daughter if there is a problem.

**Interviewer:** Anything else you want to add?

**Respondent:** No. If it helps me remember and helps my daughter know when I need help, then it is good. But medicine decisions should still be checked with the doctor.

**Interviewer:** Thank you.

**Respondent:** Okay.
