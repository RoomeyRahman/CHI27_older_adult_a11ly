# Phase 2 Follow-Up Interview Transcript

**Respondent: Female, 68 years, retired school teacher**

**Interviewer:** So we are meeting again after you have used the medicine reminder system. How was your experience with it?

**Respondent:** It was okay. At first I was a little confused because I am not used to apps. My daughter set it up for me. After that, the reminder part was easy. I could understand when it was telling me medicine time.

**Interviewer:** Do you remember what the system said about who it was helping?

**Respondent:** Yes, it was for me, but also my family could know if I had taken it or not. I understood that. Earlier also my husband or daughter would ask me. So this was like another way of checking.

**Interviewer:** Did that feel transparent to you, or did it feel like the family was interfering too much?

**Respondent:** No, it did not feel like too much because they were already doing this. If suddenly the app was sharing everything, then maybe I would not like it. But for medicine, if I miss something, my daughter knowing is okay. I don't need to hide that.

**Interviewer:** Was there a time when the system changed from being something for you privately to something the family also became involved in?

**Respondent:** Yes, when I did not press the confirmation after taking the tablet and my daughter asked me about it. I had actually taken it, I just forgot the phone part. Then we understood that the family side can be useful, but the phone does not know everything.

**Interviewer:** Did you feel the system was independent, or did you feel it needed to work together with your family?

**Respondent:** Together. For me definitely together. The app can remind me about time, but my daughter knows the prescription. My husband knows my daily routine. The app doesn't know those things unless somebody puts them there.

**Interviewer:** Did that make you trust it more or less?

**Respondent:** More, because my daughter was checking it. If only the app was deciding things, no. I would not be comfortable with that.

---

## Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** Suppose the system says you should be alert because you may have missed your medicine. Would you want the family to be notified?

**Respondent:** If I really missed it, yes. That is useful. But if I took the medicine and forgot to press the button, then they don't need to worry. So there should be some time before it tells them.

**Interviewer:** What if the system starts becoming more urgent because you have not responded?

**Respondent:** Then I would want it to become more noticeable, yes. Maybe one more reminder. If still nothing, then tell my daughter. But it should not keep making noise again and again.

**Interviewer:** If you disagreed with what the system was telling you, would you stop it or ignore it?

**Respondent:** Yes, I would not follow it blindly. If I know I already took the medicine, I will not take another one just because the app says so. I would tell my daughter also. If there is some medicine change, doctor has to confirm.

**Interviewer:** Did you ever remain silent instead of responding to the system?

**Respondent:** Yes, sometimes. Especially if I had already taken the medicine. I don't always remember that I have to press something. Sometimes I am doing something else and then I leave it.

**Interviewer:** How did the system handle that?

**Respondent:** It reminded again. That was okay. But I think it should understand that no response does not always mean medicine was not taken.

**Interviewer:** Did it change your normal medicine routine?

**Respondent:** Not really. The routine is still the same. I take the one tablet in the morning. It only gives another reminder. The main routine is still tea, breakfast, medicine.

**Interviewer:** What would count as a real failure for you?

**Respondent:** If it tells me the wrong time or wrong medicine, that would be serious. Or if it makes my daughter worried when there is no problem. Those things should not happen regularly.

---

## Care Collectivism and Checking as Love

**Interviewer:** Did the system change how your family checks on your medicine?

**Respondent:** A little. Earlier my husband would ask directly, did you take it. Now sometimes he asks whether the reminder came. My daughter can also see if something is not confirmed. So the checking has changed a little.

**Interviewer:** Do you miss the old manual reminders from your husband or daughter?

**Respondent:** No no, not really. They still remind me. I actually think both are okay. If my husband asks, that is family care. The phone is just helping with the timing.

**Interviewer:** So does checking still feel like care?

**Respondent:** Yes. Sometimes I get irritated when they ask again and again, but I know why they ask. It is because they are worried. The app doesn't replace that feeling. It just gives another reminder.

**Interviewer:** Suppose there is some confusion about your prescription or a sudden health decision. Would you discuss it with the family before doing anything?

**Respondent:** Definitely. I always did that before also. If something changes, I tell my daughter and husband. Then we ask the doctor. I would not let the app make that decision.

**Interviewer:** Did the system ever make you feel that you should discuss something with your family?

**Respondent:** Yes, when there was something unclear. If I am not sure, I ask my daughter. Earlier also I did that. The system made me think more about keeping everyone informed, but the actual decision is still family and doctor.

---

## Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** The system also had points or a streak for taking medicines regularly. Did you notice that?

**Respondent:** Yes, I saw it. I understood that it was counting the days. At first I thought, why is it counting, I am taking only one medicine. But then my daughter showed me.

**Interviewer:** How did your family react when you had a good streak?

**Respondent:** My daughter was happy. She said, see, you are doing it regularly. My husband also asked how many days it had been. It became a small thing we talked about.

**Interviewer:** Did they praise or encourage you because of the score?

**Respondent:** Yes, a little. Not too much. My daughter would say good, keep it going. That was nice. But I don't need a big celebration for taking one tablet.

**Interviewer:** What if you broke the streak because you forgot?

**Respondent:** I would feel a little bad, yes. But I would not panic. One day can happen. My daughter would probably say, okay, start again. That is better than making me worried.

**Interviewer:** Would you prefer the score to be private or shared with your family?

**Respondent:** Shared with my family is okay. I would not want everyone else to see it. My family can know. It is not something I need to show outside.

**Interviewer:** Did the score actually make you more regular?

**Respondent:** Maybe a little. Because when you see that you have been regular for many days, you don't want to lose it. But the main reason is still habit. I was already taking it regularly.

---

## Probationary Trust and Retraining

**Interviewer:** In the beginning, did you feel the system had to prove itself to you?

**Respondent:** Yes, definitely. I don't trust a new thing immediately. My daughter set it up and we watched it for some time. I wanted to see whether it gives the reminder at the right time.

**Interviewer:** What did you check during that early period?

**Respondent:** Mainly whether the medicine time was correct and whether the reminder was easy to understand. Also whether it would bother me too much. I was checking if it was actually helping or just making another problem.

**Interviewer:** Did it become easier to trust after using it for some time?

**Respondent:** Yes. Once I saw that it was giving the reminder properly, I became comfortable. But still, if the prescription changes, I want my daughter to check everything again.

**Interviewer:** Do you think the system replaced your memory?

**Respondent:** No, I don't think so. I still know when I take my medicine. It just gives me a second reminder. My habit is still there.

**Interviewer:** Did it change your sense of when the medicine was due?

**Respondent:** Not really. Maybe it made me more conscious about the exact time. Before I would say morning time and take it around that routine. Now the reminder gives a more fixed point. But I don't want to become dependent on the phone completely.

**Interviewer:** If the phone reminder stopped tomorrow, would you still take the medicine?

**Respondent:** Yes, I think so. I have been taking it for many years. My routine is strong. I might be late sometimes if the day changes, but I would not suddenly forget everything.

---

## Closing and Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** Finally, who do you feel this system works for, you, your family, or both?

**Respondent:** Both. It starts with me because I am the one taking the medicine. But my family is involved anyway. So it works better when both sides are included.

**Interviewer:** When would it be acceptable for the system to become an advocate for your family?

**Respondent:** If there is a real missed medicine or something that could be risky, then yes, it can tell my daughter. That is useful. But it should not interfere in every little thing. And it should not make decisions about changing medicines.

**Interviewer:** Would you want the system to contact your family before you in some situations?

**Respondent:** Only if I have not responded for some time and there is a genuine concern. First remind me. Give me a chance to respond. Then family can be told. That seems reasonable.

**Interviewer:** What is the most useful thing you found in the system?

**Respondent:** The simple reminder. For me that is enough. I don't need ten features. If it tells me medicine time and, if I really miss it, tells my daughter, that is useful.

**Interviewer:** And what is the least useful part?

**Respondent:** Maybe too many details. I don't need to see a lot of information every time. I have one regular medicine. Keep it simple.

**Interviewer:** If you could change one thing, what would you change?

**Respondent:** I would make the confirmation very simple and make it clear that not pressing it does not always mean I missed the medicine. Also the family notification should happen only after some checking.

**Interviewer:** Would you continue using it?

**Respondent:** Yes, if my daughter is managing the setup and it stays simple. I am okay with it. It is helpful as an extra reminder, but family and routine are still important.

## Follow-up Reflection on the Smart Assistant

**Interviewer:** Okay, I just have a few more questions. You said the reminder was useful, but you also already have a strong morning routine. So, compared with a normal phone alarm that only rings at one fixed time, what is better about this smart reminder for you?

**Respondent:** Hmm, I think the difference is that it can tell me what the reminder is for. A normal alarm only rings. This one can say it is medicine time, and if I do not respond, it can remind me again. That is more useful.

**Interviewer:** So it should do more than just make a sound?

**Respondent:** Yes. It should be clear. Just tell me, "It is time for your medicine." I don't need a lot of other information.

**Interviewer:** And if you have already taken the medicine but forgot to press the confirmation?

**Respondent:** Then that is where the system has to be careful. It should not immediately say I missed it. Maybe remind me once, and then give some time. If there is still no response, my daughter can ask me.

**Interviewer:** Would you want it to keep reminding you again and again?

**Respondent:** No, no. That would be annoying. One extra reminder is enough. Too many sounds will make me ignore it.

**Interviewer:** Now, how do you think this system can make things easier for both you and your family?

**Respondent:** For me, it gives another reminder if my routine changes. For my daughter and husband, they don't have to keep asking me all the time. They can know only if there is actually some problem.

**Interviewer:** So the phone should help first, and family should come in later?

**Respondent:** Yes, that is better. First remind me. I should get the chance to handle it myself. Then if I still don't respond, family can check.

**Interviewer:** Would that make things easier for your daughter?

**Respondent:** Yes. She already has many things to do. If the system works properly, she only needs to look when something is wrong.

**Interviewer:** Do you think this system can solve all your medicine-related problems?

**Respondent:** No, not all. For me, the main thing is remembering the time. It can help with that. But if the prescription changes, or there is some new medicine, then I still want my daughter and doctor involved.

**Interviewer:** So what problems would still remain?

**Respondent:** Medicine changes, understanding new instructions, and knowing whether something is correct. Those things should still be checked.

**Interviewer:** If the system says one thing and your daughter says something different, what would you do?

**Respondent:** I would not just follow the phone. We would check the prescription and ask the doctor if needed. The phone should not decide that.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything the system should not try to do?

**Respondent:** Yes. It should not change medicine, tell me to stop something, or tell me to take something new by itself. That is for the doctor.

**Interviewer:** In your daily life, what should the system actually do for you?

**Respondent:** Keep it simple. Remind me at the right time, maybe let me press one simple button, and if I really don't respond, then tell my daughter. That is enough.

**Interviewer:** And what should your daughter still do?

**Respondent:** She should set it up, check the prescription, and update it if anything changes. I don't want to enter all those details myself.

**Interviewer:** And your husband?

**Respondent:** He can still remind me sometimes. That is normal for us. The phone does not replace that.

**Interviewer:** So the system should support your family, not replace them?

**Respondent:** Yes, exactly. Family is still important. The system is just another support.

**Interviewer:** You also said you don't want to become fully dependent on the phone. What do you mean by that?

**Respondent:** I already have my own routine. Tea, breakfast, medicine. I don't want to forget that habit and only wait for the phone every day. The phone should help when I need it, not make me stop thinking for myself.

**Interviewer:** So if the reminder stopped one day, you would still want to manage from your routine?

**Respondent:** Yes, yes. That is important. I should still know my own medicine routine.

**Interviewer:** If you had to describe the main role of this system in one simple sentence, what would you say?

**Respondent:** It should quietly help me remember and tell my family only when there is a real reason to worry.

**Interviewer:** Anything else you would change?

**Respondent:** Keep the confirmation simple. Don't send family alerts too quickly. And don't put too many things on the screen. Simple is better.

**Interviewer:** Okay, thank you very much.

**Respondent:** Yes, okay. Thank you.
