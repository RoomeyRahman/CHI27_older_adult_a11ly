# Phase 2 Interview Transcript

**Participant:** P11  
**Age:** 65 years old  
**Gender:** Male  
**Context:** Urban household, Dhaka, Bangladesh

## Phase 2: Post-Deployment Evaluation Interview

### Section 1: The Affiliation Ledger and Negotiating Allegiance

**Interviewer:** Hello, P11. Now that you have been using the agent for some time, how did it feel when the agent openly told you who it was working for?

**P11:** It was okay. I actually liked that it told me directly. I think that is better than something happening in the background and I don't know about it. It was a little unusual in the beginning, because I am not used to having something talk about who it is working for. But after using it, it made sense.

**Interviewer:** Did that make the technology feel more transparent, or did it feel intrusive in your household?

**P11:** More transparent for me. I don't really have a problem with technology telling me what it is doing. Intrusive, it can be if it keeps telling other people things which I think I can manage myself. But if it is about a missed medicine and there is a proper reason for informing the family, then I don't mind.

**Interviewer:** Can you walk me through the last time the agent changed from working privately with you to alerting your family?

**P11:** Yes. I think there was one evening when I came home late from the theatre. I was tired and I had not taken the evening medicines. The agent reminded me and I did not respond immediately. Then it asked about involving my family. I allowed it because I knew I was late and there was a possibility I would forget. My son then checked with me. It was fairly straightforward.

**Interviewer:** Did handing over the agent's loyalty to your family feel like you were giving up your independence?

**P11:** No, not really. I don't depend on my family for taking my medicines. I have been doing it for eighteen years. So I don't see one alert to my son as giving up independence. It is just another layer of checking when something is going wrong.

**Interviewer:** So it felt more like trust?

**P11:** Yes, yes. Trust. I trust my son, and I also expect the system to first give me the chance to handle it myself.

### Section 2: Veto Power, Risk, and Silence

**Interviewer:** When the agent wanted to alert your family about a missed dose, you had the power to veto or stop it. Did you ever use that veto?

**P11:** Yes, once or twice. Sometimes I had already taken the medicine but I had not responded to the agent. In that case, I stopped it because there was no reason to call my son and make him think I had missed something.

**Interviewer:** The system is designed so that when the health risk grows, your ability to veto weakens audibly. How did you react when the agent pushed back on your veto?

**P11:** Initially I found it a little annoying. I thought, I have already said no, why are you asking again. But if it is actually becoming a health risk, then I can understand it. I don't think the system should just keep accepting my veto if I am repeatedly not taking the medicine.

**Interviewer:** Did that feel like the agent was taking control away from you?

**P11:** Not exactly. I would have a problem if it did that immediately. But if it reminds me, gives me some time, asks again, and then says this is becoming a risk, then okay. There has to be some judgment in the system.

**Interviewer:** Sometimes people do not respond at all. When you were silent, how did the agent handle it?

**P11:** Usually it reminded me again. That was fine. There are times when I am working in the theatre or travelling and I don't look at the phone. Silence does not necessarily mean I have forgotten. Sometimes I am simply busy.

**Interviewer:** Did you feel it understood your silence as a normal part of your routine?

**P11:** Mostly. It did not immediately treat it as a failure. That is important for someone like me because I don't have a completely fixed routine. When I am in Dhaka, things are fairly regular. When I am outside for theatre work, it changes.

### Section 3: Care Collectivism and Checking as Love

**Interviewer:** Before this agent, how did checking your medicine by family members work in your home, and how has that changed?

**P11:** My family did not have to check very much because I usually manage it myself. They know I take several medicines, and sometimes they ask if I have taken them, especially when I come home late. With the agent, they don't have to ask as much. The system does some of that checking.

**Interviewer:** Do you miss the manual check-ins from your family?

**P11:** No, I wouldn't say I miss them. Not because I don't appreciate it. I just don't need someone checking every time. If my son asks occasionally, that's fine. But I don't want my medicine routine to become a family project.

**Interviewer:** Does the agent's notification give your family a new reason to talk to you or show care?

**P11:** Yes, sometimes. If there is a missed dose, my son can ask me what happened. That is useful. But I don't think every reminder needs to become a conversation. Sometimes it is just a reminder.

**Interviewer:** Think about a time when a prescription was unclear or you had to make a sudden health decision. Did the agent help in the family discussion or get in the way?

**P11:** There was a situation where one of my medicines had been adjusted by the doctor. The agent was still reminding me based on the previous schedule until the change was updated. That was a little confusing. So I checked with my son and then with the doctor. In that kind of situation, the agent should help keep everyone aligned, but it should not decide the medicine itself.

**Interviewer:** So would you use the agent as part of that kitchen table discussion?

**P11:** Yes, as information. Not as the final decision maker. If the prescription changes, doctor first, then family can discuss it, and the agent can follow the updated instruction.

### Section 4: Gamification as a Relational Trigger

**Interviewer:** When you reached a high score or a long streak, how did your family react?

**P11:** They noticed it once or twice. My son was more interested than I was. He asked how long I had kept the streak. I told him. It was mildly encouraging, but I don't take the score very seriously.

**Interviewer:** Did your son ever use the score to praise or encourage you?

**P11:** Yes. He said something like, good, you are keeping it properly. That was nice. But I don't need a score to take my medicines. I have been doing this for eighteen years.

**Interviewer:** Think back to a time when a streak was broken. Did losing it cause anxiety, or did it lead to encouragement from your family?

**P11:** It did not cause anxiety. I was slightly irritated because it was a long streak and then I missed one when I was travelling. My son said, okay, start again. So that was basically it. I don't think anyone in my family sees the score as a serious thing.

**Interviewer:** Would you prefer keeping the score private, or does sharing it with your family make the experience better?

**P11:** I don't mind sharing it with my family. I just don't think it should become something everyone monitors. If they see it and encourage me, fine. If it becomes another performance measure, then no.

### Section 5: Probationary Trust and Retraining

**Interviewer:** When the agent was first introduced, did you put it on a probationary period?

**P11:** Yes, definitely. I did not immediately trust it with something related to medicine. I wanted to see whether it actually understood my routine.

**Interviewer:** How did you test it before relying on it?

**P11:** I compared the reminders with what I already knew. Since I have been taking these medicines for so long, I know the timing. I also checked whether the reminders were coming at the right time when I was at home. Then I tested it when I was outside, because that is where my routine usually becomes less regular.

**Interviewer:** Did your existing eighteen years of experience make it easier to judge whether the agent was correct?

**P11:** Yes, yes. I know my own routine quite well. If the agent says something different from what my doctor has prescribed, I am not going to blindly follow it. I will check.

**Interviewer:** Over the past few weeks, do you feel relying on the agent has replaced your own memory, or has it retrained your internal sense of when a dose is due?

**P11:** I don't think it has replaced my memory. Maybe it has reinforced the timing. I still know when I am supposed to take the medicines. The reminder just makes it harder to overlook it when I am busy.

**Interviewer:** If the agent stopped working tomorrow, would you be able to continue your medicine routine?

**P11:** Yes, absolutely. I managed for eighteen years without it. The difficult part is when I am travelling or coming home late. The agent helps more in those situations, but I would still manage.

### Section 6: Closing and the Plural Principal

**Interviewer:** If you had to summarize, who do you feel this AI agent ultimately works for, you, your family, or both?

**P11:** Both, but primarily me. I am the person taking the medicines, so the first responsibility is with me. But if I miss something and there is a real risk, then my family should know. So it has to work for both.

**Interviewer:** Under what specific conditions is it acceptable for the agent to stop acting as your private tool and start acting as your family's advocate?

**P11:** If I miss a dose and don't respond after a reasonable amount of time, then yes, it can tell my family. Also if there is some repeated problem, not just one small delay. But I should get the first opportunity to correct it myself.

**Interviewer:** What would make that balance feel right for you?

**P11:** Give me the reminder first. Then maybe another reminder. If I still don't respond or I keep missing the medicine, then involve my son. That seems reasonable. I don't want the system to be too aggressive, but I also don't want it to stay silent if there is an actual health risk.

**Interviewer:** Any final thoughts about having this kind of agent in your household?

**P11:** It is useful, especially for people who are older or who have memory problems. For me, I don't really need it every day because my routine is already a habit. But when I am travelling for theatre or coming home late, it can be useful. And if it can also help find medicines at a better price, that would be even more useful. I think that is all.
