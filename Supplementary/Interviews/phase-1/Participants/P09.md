Participant: P09 (68-year-old female)  
Location: Rural Household, Bogra District, Bangladesh  


Phase 1: Environment & Artifact Observation (Pre-Interview Notes)
The participant, P09, is seated on a low wooden bench (piti) in the shaded corner of the household veranda. Beside her sits a small plastic container where several foil medicine strips are kept loosely along with a folded doctor's prescription slip, slightly worn at the edges. A basic Nokia button phone rests on top of the small table nearby. Lighting is daylight-based. The household is quiet but shows signs of morning household chores (cooking preparations, sweeping).


Phase 2: Background & Caregiver Context

Interviewer: Assalamu Alaikum, Chachi (Auntie). Thank you for sitting down with me today. To start off, can you tell me a bit about yourself and how you manage your medications every day?

P09: Walaikum Assalam, baba. My name is P09. I am about sixty-eight years old.I stay here at home. 

Interviewer: Do you need to take medicine regularly every day, Chachi?

P09: Oh yes, baba. I have to take them every single day. My body aches, my pressure goes up, and my stomach bothers me if I don't take them.

Interviewer: Walk me through a typical morning and afternoon. How many times a day do you take your medicines, and how do you take them?

P09: I take them three times a day. I take one in the morning after eating a little puffed rice or flatbread, then two pills at noon after my main meal, and then one pill at night before sleeping.



 Phase 3: Situated Walkthrough & Current Practices

Interviewer: Can you show me where you keep these medicines and how you know which pill to take at what time?

P09:(Reaches into the small plastic tub and holds up a couple of foil blister strips)  
Here, see? I keep them all in this small plastic bowl so they don't get lost. My son brought this bowl for me. 

Interviewer: How do you recognize which pill is for the morning, which is for noon, and which is for night?

P09: Ah, baba, I don't know the long English names written on the paper! My elder son buys them from the market pharmacy in the town. When he brings a new box, he opens it and explains it to me ,he points and says, Ma, this blue strip is for your stomach, take it at noon with the round white one. The red foil strip is for your pressure, take it at night. That is how I know. My children bring the medicine, tell me how to take it, and I just keep it in my head.

Interviewer: So you rely mostly on what your family members bring and explain to you?

P09: Yes, exactly. I can't read the small letters on the packets. Whatever my son or daughter-in-law buys and tells me, that's what I follow. Once they tell me two or three times, it becomes a habit. I just remember: morning this strip, noon those two, night that red one.



 Phase 4: Scenario Probing: Challenges & Barriers

Interviewer: Does it ever become difficult to take your medicines regularly on time? Have you ever missed a dose?

P09: Yes, sometimes it happens. When there is a lot of work at once—like during paddy drying season, or when guests come over suddenly and I have to cook big meals—my mind gets occupied. I might forget the morning dose or miss the noon one until late in the afternoon. 

Interviewer: When you miss a dose or feel confused, what do you do?

P09: If my daughter-in-law notices, she reminds me: Amma, did you take your noon pill? If I forget completely, I just take it whenever I remember, or I skip it until the next scheduled time.



Phase 5 & 6: Technology Elicitation, Accessibility, & Literacy

Interviewer: Do you use any digital technology or phone to help you remember your medicine?

P09: Phone? (Points to the basic Nokia button phone on the table) I only have this button phone. 

Interviewer: Have you ever tried setting an alarm or using any tool on that button phone?

P09: Oh no, baba! I don't know how to do all that. If technology or fancy phone things were used to remind me, it would be very difficult for me. I can't read well, my eyesight is dim, and I have trouble understanding how to press all those small buttons. I only know how to press the green button when it rings to talk, and the red button to cut the call.

Interviewer: If someone built a special tool or phone app to remind people to take medicine, what feature would be most useful for you?

P09: If the phone could make a loud sound—like a loud alarm ringing at noon and morning—that would be good. Just a simple loud sound so I know, *"Oh, the clock is ringing, it's time for medicine."* Beyond a simple alarm, I cannot operate complex things.



 Phase 7: Care Network & Social Context Mapping

Interviewer: Who else in your family plays a role in managing your medicine?

P09: My elder son is the main one. He works near the town center, so he brings all the medicines from the big pharmacy. He pays for them and brings them home. My daughter-in-law checks the bowl every few days to see if any strip is running empty. If it is empty, she tells my son, *"Bring another leaf of Amma's pressure pill."* So you see, the whole thing stems from them—they bring the medicine, explain the timing, and keep track of stock.

Interviewer: How do you communicate with healthcare providers or doctors when you feel unwell or run out of prescriptions?

P09: I don't talk to doctors directly usually. If I feel bad, I tell my son. Sometimes he calls the pharmacy shop keeper on his phone to ask, or he takes me to the local village doctor (pallichikitsak). If we need to talk, it's just by calling on the button phone.

Phase 8: Closing & Participatory Reflection

Interviewer: Do you have any suggestions or final thoughts on how people like you in rural areas can be supported better with taking medicine?

P09: No, baba, what suggestions can an uneducated village woman give? As long as my children bring the proper medicines on time and remind me when I forget, Allah keeps me going fine. A simple ringing alarm on the phone is all that could help, nothing more complicated than that.

Interviewer: Thank you so much, P09, for sharing your time and experience with us today.

P09: You are welcome, baba. May Allah bless you.
