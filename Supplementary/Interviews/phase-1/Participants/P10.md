 
Participant: Abul Hasin (78 years old, Male)  
Location: Urban Neighborhood (Dhaka, Bangladesh)  
 

 Phase 1: Environment & Artifact Observation (Pre-Interview)

Observation Note: The session takes place in the living room of a modest urban residence. On the side table next to Abul Hasin's easy chair sits a small plastic tray holding two medicine strips: one for diabetes and one inhaler/oral medicine for breathing. Next to the tray is a basic feature phone (button phone) and a slightly aged smartphone. A jug of water and a glass are kept readily accessible. No written pill calendars, sticky notes, or automated pillboxes are present in the room.



Phase 2: Background & Caregiver Context

Interviewer: Assalamu Alaikum, uncle. Thank you for sitting down with me today. To begin, could you tell me a little bit about yourself and how you manage your daily health routines here at home?

Abul Hasin: Walaikum Assalam, baba. Come, sit. Well, what is there to say? I am Abul Hasin, seventy-eight years old now. I have been living here in the city for many years. Naturally, at this age, the body carries a few burdens—I have diabetes and this breathing difficulty (*shwas-kashthe*). Every morning, I like to go out to the local park or court-area to meet my friends. We have a small community group; we walk together, talk about old times, and keep each other active. But when it comes to my body, these medicines are a constant companion.

Interviewer: How do you usually take your medicines each day? Could you walk me through your typical routine from morning till night?

Abul Hasin: Yes, yes. In the morning, after I finish my prayers and come back from my morning walk with the community, I take my morning dose. My family—my daughter-in-law or son—always makes sure the water is there and reminds me if I look busy. But I take the medicine myself. I take my diabetes medicine twice a day—once in the morning and once in the evening after dinner. For my breathing problem, I take that medicine once a day. 

Interviewer: How many types of medicines are you currently taking regularly?

Abul Hasin: Right now, two main types. One for diabetes, taken twice daily, and the breathing medicine, taken once a day. So three doses in total across the day.


Phase 3: Situated Walkthrough & Current Practices

Interviewer: Uncle, could you show me where you keep your medicines and how you handle them when it is time for a dose?

Abul Hasin: (*Gestures to the small plastic tray on the side table near his chair*)  
Look here, they are kept right here on this side table. I don't use any fancy boxes or paper notes. When the time comes, I just reach out, take the strip out of the packet, pop out the pill, and swallow it with water. 

Interviewer: You mentioned you don't use written notes, paper calendars, or alarms. How do you keep track of whether it’s time?

Abul Hasin: I don't use any tool or written method, to be honest. It’s mostly habit and memory. I sit right here after meals, so I see the tray and remember. And as I said, if I forget, someone in the family—or even my friends at the morning walk when we talk about our daily routines—will ask, *"Uncle, did you take your tablet today?"* So between my own habit and the people around me, we manage.

Interviewer: What part of this routine feels smooth, and what feels a bit tricky or inconvenient?

Abul Hasin: Taking them is simple enough when everything is right in front of me. But keeping the timing exact every single day without fail—that is where the mind slips sometimes.



 Phase 4: Scenario Probing: Challenges & Barriers

Interviewer: Have you ever forgotten to take a dose, uncle?

Abul Hasin: Yes. truth be told, I forget sometimes. 

Interviewer: Do you remember what causes you to forget or when it becomes particularly difficult to manage?

Abul Hasin: I don’t know the exact reason, baba. Old age, perhaps. Sometimes the mind is occupied with something else—maybe I am talking with relatives, or watching the evening news, or feeling tired from my breathing trouble. When the daily routine gets disturbed or when life gets busy around the house, that is when managing the medicine regularly becomes very difficult. You lose track of whether you took it an hour ago or not.



 Phase 5: Technology Elicitation & Device Demonstration

Interviewer: Let's talk a bit about mobile phones. What kind of phone do you currently use?

Abul Hasin: (Points to both devices on the table)  
I actually use both! I have this button phone it is sturdy, easy to press, and I carry it everywhere. And I also have this touch phone (smartphone) that my family set up for me. 

Interviewer: How do you communicate with your doctors when you need a consultation or prescription check?

Abul Hasin: Through the mobile phone. My family helps me dial or connect, and I speak to the doctor directly over the phone.



Phase 6: Accessibility, Literacy, & Interaction Needs

Interviewer: If someone were to design a special medicine management app or digital tool specifically for you, what feature would be the most important?

Abul Hasin: A message reminder would be good. Just a clear message or prompt that tells me, "It is time for your diabetes tablet" or "Take your breathing medicine." At my age, I don't need complicated graphics or complex buttons. Just a simple message reminder on the phone that alerts me so I don't have to rely purely on memory.

Interviewer: Do you have any other suggestions or ideas on how technology can be made more effective for elders like you in our community?

Abul Hasin: No, nothing specific. Just keep it simple and easy for old eyes and ears to notice.


Phase 7 & 8: Care Network & Closing Reflection

Interviewer: It is wonderful how your family and community walking group keep an eye out for you. 

Abul Hasin: Yes, in our culture and society, we don't live in isolation. My family ensures my medicine stock is full, and my community friends keep my spirits high. Technology should complement that—not make things complicated.

Interviewer: Thank you so much, Uncle Hasin, for your valuable time and for sharing your experience with us.

Abul Hasin: You are very welcome, baba. May Allah bless your work.
