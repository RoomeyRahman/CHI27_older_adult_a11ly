# CHI-Calibrated Prose Style

Academic prose at CHI level must be denser than general writing, more precise about claims, and more explicit about where claims came from — without becoming skimmable sludge. Density and engagement pull against each other; these habits hold both. A paper that reads easily but claims loosely gets rejected on rigour; a paper that claims carefully but reads like a specification gets rejected because reviewers never found the contribution.

Read this before drafting; check against the reference table afterward. Checking mid-draft produces sentences written to satisfy a metric.

## Rhythm

- Pair a long sentence carrying qualification with a short one landing the point. The long holds conditions, scope, caveats; the short says what to take from it.
- Target ~19 words mean sentence length, but the spread matters more: SD between 6 and 9. Ten sentences of nineteen words each read as machine output.
- Per ten sentences: one or two under 12 words, six or seven in the middle, two past 25.
- Spend short sentences on the claims you most want remembered. A flat six-word sentence after a thirty-word qualification reads as confidence.
- Past 35 words is almost always two sentences unseparated; split, except genuine parallel lists.
- Long words fine, long clauses not. Put density in vocabulary (a precise term saves a clause), not syntax. CHI reviewers read internationally; embedded clauses tax non-native readers.

## Holding the thread

- Link sentences by pointing back at the previous idea ("this suggests", "because of that", "the same pattern held for", "which is why") rather than repeating the noun. Roughly half of adjacent sentence pairs should share no content word.
- Given-before-new: open each sentence with something already established, close with the addition. Choppiness usually means new information arriving in subject position.
- Fix 3–5 anchor terms for the paper. Define each once, use exactly that word every time, vary everything around them. Synonym variation on a defined construct is a defect: the reader cannot tell whether the second term names the same thing.
- Watch nominalization. "The utilisation of colour for the facilitation of expression" says less than "participants used colour to express tone". Keep actor and action in the sentence; nominalize only when the process itself is the topic.

## Defining by contrast

- Frame claims against what they are not: "rather than X, Y". This is the signature move, and at CHI it does positioning and definition at once, because the contrasted thing is usually prior work. Example: "Rather than treating emotional expression as something to be inferred, we ask how it can remain something people shape."
- Six to eight per thousand words, spread across sections. One for the thesis, one per construct that matters, one per related-work gap, one at the close. All in the first page reads as defensive.
- Keep them honest: name the alternative accurately enough that its advocates would recognise it. A straw-man contrast gets caught immediately.

## Paragraphs

- A paragraph makes a claim, supports it, and says what the support established.
- Open with an arguable claim, not an announcement. "Participants used colour differently at work" is a claim; "This section describes how participants used colour" wastes the position readers attend to most.
- Support = mechanism, citation, number, quotation, or worked case — two to four sentences. Restatement in different words is padding.
- Close about half of substantive paragraphs with a synthesis sentence ("together, these themes show", "the upshot is"). All of them, and the template becomes visible.
- Vary paragraph length as you vary sentence length: 3–6 sentences working range, occasional two-sentence paragraph for emphasis.

## Describing what already exists

- Shape: what the established line achieved → a sentence or two on how it plays out → the turn ("however", "yet", or nothing) → what it does not handle, why that matters here, what you do about it.
- One gap per paragraph. Stacking complaints reads as axe-grinding and invites the defended reviewer.
- Be generous before critical. A gap after a fair account reads as scholarship; the same gap after a dismissive summary reads as not having read carefully.

## Who acts

- Put the actual actor in subject position. Participants decide, adjust, abandon, work around; systems suggest, render, return, fail. "Participants could disable highlighting", not "the system allowed participants to disable highlighting". Agency in the grammar shapes what a reader believes was in participants' control.
- First person for research acts: we built, we recruited, we analysed, we argue — around 6 mentions per thousand words. Passive is fine when the actor is irrelevant or obvious, but a fully passive method section hides the decisions transparency requires.

## Calibrating certainty

- Flat about procedure and observation; hedge inference. The reader must be able to tell what you watched from what you concluded.
- Verb ladder: *shows/demonstrates* needs a controlled comparison; *indicates/points toward* needs a strong consistent pattern; *suggests/is consistent with* needs an observed regularity; *appears/may* marks genuine uncertainty. Pick the rung your evidence reaches; over-hedging a measured result is as miscalibrated as over-claiming.
- One hedge per claim. "This may possibly suggest that colour might support expression" hedges four times and says nothing.
- Causal language only for controlled designs. Deployments, interviews, observation yield association and description.
- Quantify qualitative patterns coarsely and consistently: most, roughly half, several, a few, one participant. No percentages on a sample of twenty-four; no retreat into "many".
- State exact figures once and leave them.
- Say plainly what the work does not cover.

## Citations as prose

- Citations attach to specific claims about the literature, positioned at clause end. A bracket carrying four references must point at something those four papers share.
- ~11–14 citation brackets per thousand words, most carrying 2–4 references. Organise by idea, not one-paper-per-sentence summaries.
- Never cite your own findings — the reader loses track of what is new.
- **When drafting for a user, never invent citations.** Use placeholders: `[CITE: deployment studies of X]`.

## Being interesting without hype

- Engagement comes from stakes and tension: what would be lost if the question went unanswered, what the reader would not have predicted.
- Surface tension rather than smoothing it. The participant who abandoned the system buys more credibility than three more supporting quotes. The strongest sentence in many CHI papers starts "but this held only when".
- Let participants' words carry qualitative evidence; do not paraphrase a quote right after it.
- Intensifiers do the opposite of what they promise. Replace the adjective with the evidence.

## Tuning by section

Introduction and discussion: contrast frames densest, sentences longest — both sections argue. Method: flat short declaratives, exact figures, minimal hedging, explicit agency. Findings: participants in subject position, quotations as evidence, coarse quantifiers. Related work: gap structure and citation integration. Limitations: the flattest prose in the paper — hedged limitations read as excuses.

## Banned

Hype: leverage, robust, novel, seamless, state-of-the-art, cutting-edge, comprehensive, powerful, crucial, pivotal, delve, landscape, realm, underscore, unlock, harness.

Stacked connectors: moreover, furthermore, additionally, "it is important to note that". If the connection is real, the content carries it.

Also out: intensifiers that measure nothing, "ever-evolving landscape" openers, rhetorical questions as section transitions, em-dashes for parenthetical asides (use a comma, semicolon, or new sentence).

End on the last real point or its consequence, never a summary.

## Pre-send checklist

- No banned word survives anywhere.
- 6–8 contrast frames per 1,000 words, spread not bunched, each naming an alternative its advocates would recognise.
- No three consecutive sentences share a length; at least one short flat sentence in every long paragraph.
- Sentences link by reference to the previous idea; given before new.
- 3–5 anchor terms used consistently; no synonym for a defined construct.
- Every paragraph opens with an arguable claim, not an announcement.
- Every paragraph survives "what is the evidence here".
- Synthesis closers in about half the paragraphs.
- Participants and researchers, not systems, in subject position for findings.
- Observation flat, inference hedged once, the two distinguishable.
- Every verb on the rung its evidence reaches; causal language only with controlled comparison.
- Coarse quantifiers consistent; no percentages on small samples.
- Citations attach to claims about the literature, never to your own findings.
- At least one finding complicates the framing.
- What the work does not cover is stated plainly.
- The ending is a consequence, not a summary.
