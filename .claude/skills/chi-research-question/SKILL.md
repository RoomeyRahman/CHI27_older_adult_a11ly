# General Rules for Developing a Research Question

Distilled from Scribbr ("Writing Strong Research Questions"), Litmaps ("How to write a research question that drives your study"), and Wordvice ("How to Write a Good Research Question"). Every generated question must pass this checklist.

## The core criteria (all three sources converge on these)

1. **Focused** — addresses a single problem or issue; multiple questions must all tie back to one central aim.
2. **Researchable** — answerable with primary/secondary data or scholarly sources; something observable or measurable. Purely opinion-based or philosophical questions fail this test.
3. **Feasible** — answerable within the real timeframe, budget, data-access, and skill constraints. If the required data cannot be accessed, narrow the question.
4. **Specific** — all terms have clear, well-defined meanings; no vague language ("affect", "impact" without an operationalized outcome), no undefined jargon.
5. **Complex** — cannot be answered "yes/no" or by a single Google search; requires synthesis, original data, and interpretation; deep enough to sustain a full paper.
6. **Relevant & original** — addresses a gap in the literature or a timely debate; contributes knowledge others can build on. Originality can come from a new population, context, angle, or method — not necessarily a never-asked question.
7. **Not value-laden** — avoid subjective words like _good, bad, better, worse_; replace with measurable criteria ("How effective is X at reducing Y?").
8. **Not solution-demanding** — research informs rather than instructs. "What should the government do about X?" fails; "What communication strategies are most effective for X among population P?" passes. (If a ready-made solution is truly required, that's action research.)
9. **Literature-anchored** — extends, examines, or verifies existing work; fits within academic consensus and is discoverable by other researchers. Fringe/conspiracy premises fail.
10. **Arguable/testable** — the answer is open to scrutiny, counterargument, and (where quantitative) falsification.

## The 6-step development process (Litmaps)

1. Identify the broad topic → 2. Narrow the focus (population, variable, context, location, time period) → 3. Define objectives (what relationship/phenomenon/gap?) → 4. Convert into a question (stems: "How does…", "What factors influence…", "To what extent does…", "Why does…") → 5. Verify researchability → 6. Revise for clarity and precision.

## The Goldilocks calibration (all sources)

- **Too broad:** "How does social media affect people?" — many variables, undefined constructs.
- **Too narrow:** "How does Instagram affect self-esteem in 15-year-olds at XYZ High School?" — findings can't generalize; sample trivially small.
- **Balanced:** "How does daily Instagram use influence teenage self-esteem?" — specified platform, dosage, population, and measurable outcome, with generalizable scope.

## Framing by research objective (Scribbr)

| Objective        | Question stems                                                                                 |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Describe/explore | What are the characteristics of X? How has X changed over time? How do users of X deal with Y? |
| Explain/test     | What is the relationship between X and Y? How does X influence Y? What is the role of X in Y?  |
| Evaluate/act     | How effective is X (at Z, measured by M)? How can X be improved for population P?              |

## Question types by paradigm (Litmaps, Wordvice)

- **Qualitative** — open-ended "how/what/why"; explores experiences, perceptions, meaning; categories: contextual, descriptive, evaluative, explanatory, exploratory. Don't over-narrow; leave room for discovery. No forced IV/DV.
- **Quantitative** — measurable variables; states population, IV, DV, and implies design; categories: descriptive ("what?"), comparative (group differences), relationship/correlational (associations, trends).
- **Statistical** — subset of quantitative; hypothesis tests, group comparisons, prediction; wording includes "significant difference", "correlation", "predict"; needs adequate sample size and a matching analysis plan.

## Sub-questions (Scribbr)

Optional. Use only when the main question can't be answered in one step. Keep them: simpler than the main question, single-method each, logically ordered, max 4–5. Needing more than 5 signals the main question is too broad.

## Common failure modes to check before finalizing

- Yes/no phrasing → reword with "how", "to what extent", "what factors"
- Undefined construct ("affects", "impacts", "engagement") → name the operationalized measure
- Unrealistic scope (global databases, multi-year longitudinal work for a semester project) → cut population, context, or timeframe
- Superficial (answerable from one textbook) → add mechanism, comparison, or moderating context
- Already answered → shift population, context, technology generation, or method
