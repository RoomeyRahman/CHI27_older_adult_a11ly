# A Writing Style, Calibrated to CHI

Academic prose at this level has to be denser than general writing, more precise about what it claims, and more explicit about where its claims came from, all without becoming the kind of writing people skim. Density and engagement pull against each other; the habits below exist to hold both.

Read this before drafting, and check against the reference table afterward. Checking mid-draft produces sentences written to satisfy a metric, which is worse than either failure mode.

## Rhythm

Write a long sentence that carries the qualification, then a short one that lands the point. The long sentence holds the conditions, the scope, and the caveats; the short one tells the reader what to take from it. This pairing does more for readability than any other single habit, and it matters most in academic prose, because academic sentences are long by necessity and a reader needs somewhere to rest.

Published CHI prose averages around nineteen words per sentence. That is the target, but the spread matters more than the mean: aim for a standard deviation between six and nine. Ten sentences of nineteen words each will read as machine output regardless of how good the words are. Per ten sentences, expect one or two under twelve words, six or seven in the middle, and two that run past twenty-five.

The short sentence is rarer in academic writing than in general writing, which is exactly why it works. Spend them on the claims you most want a reviewer to remember. A flat six-word sentence after a thirty-word qualification reads as confidence.

Length past thirty-five words is almost always two sentences that have not been separated. The exception is a genuine list, where parallel structure carries the reader through. Everything else should be split.

Long words are fine and long clauses are not. Density belongs in the vocabulary, where a precise term saves a clause, rather than in the syntax, where subordination makes the reader hold more in working memory. This is also a fairness point: CHI reviewers read internationally, and a sentence with three embedded clauses costs a non-native reader far more than it costs you to split it.

## Holding the thread

Link sentences by pointing back at the previous idea rather than repeating the noun that carried it: "this suggests", "because of that", "the same pattern held for", "which is why". Repeating a noun across three consecutive sentences reads as a stall; referring to the idea keeps the argument moving. In practice roughly half your adjacent sentence pairs will share no content word, and that is correct — the connective work is done by demonstratives and by abstract nouns standing in for whole propositions.

Order information given-before-new. Open each sentence with something the previous sentence established and close it with what you are adding. This is the single most reliable way to make dense material feel like it flows. When a paragraph feels choppy despite short sentences, the cause is usually new information arriving in subject position.

Fix three to five anchor terms for the whole paper. Define each once, use exactly that word every time you mean that thing, and vary everything around them. Synonym variation is a virtue in general prose and a defect in academic prose, because a reader cannot tell whether your second term names the same construct or a different one.

Watch nominalization. "The utilisation of colour for the facilitation of expression" says less than "participants used colour to express tone" and takes longer to say it. Keep the actor and the action in the sentence. Nominalize only when the process itself is the thing under discussion.

## Defining by contrast

Frame claims against what they are not. This is the signature move of the style and, at CHI, it is also how you position yourself in the literature. The gap in your related work, the framing sentence in your introduction, and the contribution claim in your abstract are all the same construction: rather than X, Y.

The construction works because readers understand new things by their boundaries, and stating the boundary explicitly saves them the inference. In a paper it works twice as hard, because the thing contrasted against is usually prior work, so the frame does positioning and definition in one sentence.

Six to eight per thousand words, spread across sections rather than clustered in the introduction. Use one for the thesis, one for each construct that matters, one for the gap in each related-work subsection, and one at the close. If they all appear in the first page, the paper reads as defensive.

Keep them honest. A contrast frame set against a position nobody holds is a straw man, and reviewers who work in the area being contrasted against will notice immediately. Name the alternative accurately enough that its advocates would recognise it.

## Paragraphs

A paragraph makes a claim, supports it, and says what the support established.

Open with the claim, and make the claim arguable. "Participants used colour differently at work" is a claim. "This section describes how participants used colour in different settings" is an announcement, and an announcement wastes the position in the paragraph a reader attends to most. Background follows the claim; it does not precede it.

Then two to four sentences of support: a mechanism, a citation, a number, a quotation, or a worked case. Restatement in different words is not support, and a reviewer reads it as padding. Every paragraph should be answerable to "what is the evidence here".

Close some paragraphs with a sentence naming what has been established: "together, these themes show", "the upshot is", "what this leaves is". Use it in perhaps half your substantive paragraphs; used in all of them, the pattern becomes visible, and a visible pattern reads as a template.

Paragraph length should vary the way sentence length does. Three to six sentences is the working range, with the occasional two-sentence paragraph for emphasis.

## Describing what already exists

Any passage covering prior work, competing approaches, or the obvious solution follows one shape. Say what the established line of work achieved. Give a sentence or two on how it plays out. Then the turn, with "however" or "yet" or nothing at all if the contrast is already clear. Say what it does not handle, why that matters here, and what you do about it.

One gap per paragraph. Stacking three complaints against the same body of work reads as an axe being ground rather than an argument being made, and it invites a reviewer who works in that area to defend it.

Be generous before you are critical. A gap identified after a fair account of what prior work accomplished reads as scholarship; the same gap after a dismissive summary reads as a failure to have read carefully.

## Who acts

Put the thing that actually acts in the subject position. Participants decide, adjust, abandon, and work around. Systems suggest, render, return, and fail. Write "participants could disable highlighting" rather than "the system allowed participants to disable highlighting". Agency in the grammar shapes what a reader believes was in the participants' control, which is frequently the substance of the finding.

Use first person for research acts you performed: we built, we recruited, we analysed, we argue. Around six mentions per thousand words is enough. Passive voice is not banned and is sometimes correct, particularly when the actor is irrelevant or obvious, but a method section written entirely in the passive hides exactly the decisions transparency requires exposing.

## Calibrating certainty

Be flat about procedure and observation. Hedge inference. Keeping these distinguishable is the core discipline of academic prose, because a reader who cannot tell what you watched from what you concluded cannot evaluate either.

There is a ladder of verbs, and each rung commits to a different standard of evidence. *Shows* and *demonstrates* need a controlled comparison. *Indicates* and *points toward* need a strong, consistent pattern. *Suggests* and *is consistent with* need an observed regularity. *Appears* and *may* mark genuine uncertainty. Pick the rung the evidence reaches and stay there; over-hedging a measured result is as much a miscalibration as over-claiming an unmeasured one.

One hedge per claim. Stacked hedging reads as either evasion or an author who does not know what their data supports.

Reserve causal language for controlled designs. Deployment studies, interviews, and observational work yield association and description, and reviewers are unforgiving about this specific slip.

Quantify qualitative patterns coarsely and consistently: most, roughly half, several, a few, one participant. Do not switch to percentages on a small sample, and do not retreat into "many", which hides whether you counted. State exact figures once, where they belong, and leave them.

Say plainly what the work does not cover. A short passage naming untested conditions, unreached populations, and claims not being made does more for credibility than any careful phrasing elsewhere.

## Citations as prose

Citations are part of the sentence, not decoration attached to it. Attach them to specific claims about the literature, position them at clause end rather than mid-clause, and make sure a bracket carrying four references points at something those four papers actually share.

Around twelve to thirteen citation brackets per thousand words is typical for CHI, most carrying two to four references. A long reference list does not demonstrate engagement, and a paragraph that summarises one paper per sentence demonstrates the opposite. Organise by idea, cite several papers per claim, and let the citation do argumentative work.

Never cite your own findings. Citations support claims about what is already known; attaching one to your own result makes the reader lose track of what is new.

## Being interesting without hype

Engagement comes from stakes and tension, not adjectives. A paper is interesting when the reader can see what would be lost if the question went unanswered, and when the findings contain something the reader would not have predicted.

Surface the tension rather than smoothing it over. A finding that complicates your own framing is more engaging than one that confirms it. The strongest sentence in many CHI papers is the one that starts "but this held only when".

Let participants' language do the work in qualitative sections; integrate quotations and leave them unglossed. Paraphrasing a quotation immediately after it appears tells the reader you did not trust it.

Intensifiers do the opposite of what they promise. "Highly novel", "significantly improves", "a powerful framework": each asks the reader to accept an evaluation instead of showing the thing evaluated. Replace the adjective with the evidence and the sentence gets shorter and more persuasive.

## Tuning by section

In the introduction and discussion, contrast frames run densest and sentences run longest, because both sections argue. In the method, the prose flattens: short declaratives, exact figures, minimal hedging, agency fully explicit. Findings sit in between, with participants in subject position, quotations carrying the evidence, and coarse quantifiers doing the generalising. Related work leans hardest on the gap structure and on citation integration. Limitations should be the flattest prose in the paper, because hedged limitations read as excuses.

## What to avoid

Hype carries no information and costs trust: leverage, robust, novel, seamless, state-of-the-art, cutting-edge, comprehensive, powerful, crucial, pivotal, delve, landscape, realm, underscore, unlock, harness.

Stacked connectors announce a relationship the sentence should be demonstrating: moreover, furthermore, additionally, it is important to note that. Cut all of them.

Also out: intensifiers that measure nothing, openers about the ever-evolving landscape of anything, rhetorical questions as section transitions, and em-dashes for parenthetical asides. A comma, a semicolon, or a new sentence does the same work without the theatrical pause.

End on the last real point or its consequence. A closing paragraph that restates the paper tells the reader their time was wasted.

## Before you send it

- No banned word survives anywhere.
- Six to eight contrast frames per thousand words, spread rather than bunched, each naming an alternative its advocates would recognise.
- No three consecutive sentences share a length; at least one short flat sentence in every long paragraph.
- Sentences link by reference to the previous idea, and each opens with given information before new.
- Three to five anchor terms used consistently; no synonym stands in for a defined construct.
- Every paragraph opens with an arguable claim, not an announcement.
- Every paragraph survives "what is the evidence here".
- Synthesis closers in about half the paragraphs, not all.
- Participants and researchers, not systems, occupy subject position in findings.
- Observation is flat, inference is hedged once, and the two are distinguishable.
- Every verb sits on the rung of the evidence ladder its evidence reaches.
- Causal language appears only where a controlled comparison supports it.
- Coarse quantifiers are consistent; no percentages on small samples.
- Citations attach to claims about the literature, never to your own findings.
- At least one finding complicates the framing rather than confirming it.
- What the work does not cover is stated plainly somewhere.
- The ending is a consequence, not a summary.
