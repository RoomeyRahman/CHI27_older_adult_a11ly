**Compiled** 2026-09-02 by `chi-literature-scout`; revised the same day across three reductions of the bibliography, to 150 entries, then 146, then 122.
**Bibliography** `references/reference.bib`, 122 entries, keys `1`–`122`.
**Unverified material** `analysis/literature/unverified-leads.md`. Nothing in section A of that file may be cited.

## How this corpus was built

Search ran against two authoritative metadata services rather than free-text web results: the Crossref REST API (publisher-deposited records, which is what ACM, Springer, Elsevier, and the journals themselves register) and the OpenAlex API (abstracts, citation counts, and records for books that carry no DOI). Seventy-one topical queries were run across seven research streams with the ACM DOI prefix as a filter, producing 2,662 unique candidate records; 900 were enriched with abstracts and citation counts; four further rounds of targeted title-level queries chased named canonical works. Selection then ran by hand on titles, venues, years, and abstracts.

**218 works were verified in that pass**, 210 against a Crossref publisher record and 8, which carry no publisher DOI, against an OpenAlex record. Together with the 99 entries already in the file, and after removing one pre-existing duplicate key, the bibliography reached 313 entries.

**It was then cut, in three passes, to 122.** The first kept an entry only if it carried analytic load, meaning a full key-paper row here, a theory-ledger row, or a named place in the ranked gaps; that removed 67 adjacent papers listed only with a one-line note, 95 pre-existing entries the literature pass never cited, and 7 whose work a kept neighbour already carried, leaving 150. Six pre-existing entries were promoted in and given full rows, four of them the project's only citable South Asian ageing sources. The second pass tightened the rule to the row alone and cut four more. The third replaced that rule, because a row proved nothing: one had been written for every selected paper. The surviving test is **used in at least one sentence of this file's prose, a theory-ledger row, or a ranked gap**. Thirty-four entries failed it. Ten were kept anyway and given real prose work, since the failure was this file's rather than the paper's; the other 24 went. The bibliography holds no duplicate DOI and no duplicate title. `analysis/literature/unverified-leads.md` section D lists every removal by reason, and `reference.bib.bak-146`, `.bak-150`, and `.bak-313` hold the supersets, so any paper can be restored.

**Venue distribution** skews as instructed: CHI, PACM HCI (CSCW), DIS, TOCHI, ASSETS, FAccT, UIST, IMWUT, CUI, IUI, COMPASS, ICTD, PDC, HRI, HAI, and GROUP first; health and social-science journals (JMIR, BMC, *The Gerontologist*, *Ageing and Society*, *Patient Education and Counseling*, *Minds and Machines*, *Philosophical Studies*, *Human Factors*) second, and only where they carry load no HCI paper carries. Per stream: S1 11, S2 14, S3 20, S4 20, S5 18, S6 12, S7 27.

**Citation keys.** Entries are cited below by their integer key in `references/reference.bib`. Keys run 1 to 122 in the alphabetical order ACM-Reference-Format prints, so a draft that writes a bracketed 27 compiles to `\cite{27}` and prints as 27. A full number-to-paper index sits at the end of this file.

**Reading conventions.** Every row's *Finding* is paraphrased from the abstract or landing page that was fetched; no row restates abstract text. Where the abstract was empty in both services, the row says `title-level relevance only` and no finding is claimed. `c` values are OpenAlex citation counts as of 2026-09-02 and move over time.

## Stream 1 — Medication adherence and chronic-illness self-management technology

**What this body of work has established.** Adherence technology in HCI has moved from reminder design toward routine and habit: reminders work while they are scaffolding and fail when they substitute for a routine, and self-management for people with several conditions is a scheduling and interpretation problem before it is a memory problem. The stream's most recent turn puts the family, not the patient, at the center of chronic-disease work, and that turn is where our RQ1 attaches.

| Key | Authors, year | Objective | Method | Sample / data | Main finding | Limitation | Relevance here |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| [92] | Richards et al., 2025, TOCHI | Build a systematic design basis for family-based chronic disease management | 2-year field study, routines theory | 38 families managing type 2 diabetes and/or HIV/AIDS | Chronic-disease work is organized as interrelated *collective* family routines, with distinct roles for family, patient, and artifacts | Routines theory imported wholesale; no agentic system in play | The closest prior work to RQ1's unit of analysis; our care network claim builds on this and adds an agent that decides |
| [9] | Andersen, 2013, CSCW | Reconceptualize the ethnography–design relation through medication management | Ethnography, ontological framing | Clinical medication management setting | Medication management is produced in practice rather than described; "use" is not a stable object designers can survey | Theoretically dense, thin empirical reporting | Licenses treating medication work as practice rather than compliance |
| [107] | Stawarz et al., 2014, CHI | Assess whether medication reminder apps support daily routine | Functionality review plus thematic analysis of reviews | 229 apps, 1,012 user reviews | Apps optimize for alerting and neglect routine integration; forgetting is a third of unintentional non-adherence but poorly addressed | App-store data, no deployment | The canonical statement of the reminder-design gap our system section argues past |

| [27] | Doyle et al., 2019, CHI | Derive design requirements for multimorbidity self-management | Requirements study | Older adults with multiple chronic conditions | Multimorbidity means coordinating conflicting advice across providers, not tracking one regimen | Requirements-level, no deployment | Grounds the complex-regimen participant in Study 2 |
| [54] | Keys et al., 2025, CHI | Understand consumer technology in atrial-fibrillation self-care | Interviews | 29 adults living with AF | People outsourced bodily awareness to a "digitised heart" and used it to negotiate in shared decision-making | Single condition, self-report | Nearest published account of a device *strengthening* a patient's hand in negotiation with others |
| [35] | Grindrod et al., 2014, JMIR mHealth | Assess usability of medication apps with older adults | Usability sessions | 35 participants aged 50+ | Existing medication apps were largely unusable for this group | Lab usability, dated app corpus | The setup-friction findings in our Study 2 have a prior |
| [102] | Shin et al., 2022, CHI | Reframe sleep as family collaborative work | Interviews | 20 parents of young children | A "personal" health behavior is entangled with other members' routines and self-care | Parents of children, not eldercare | Precedent for the move from individual health behavior to household practice |
| [119] | Zakreuskaya, 2025, CHI | Support collaborative medication reconciliation across clinical teams | Doctoral-consortium research statement | Hospital care transitions | Current systems do not support the collaborative work that reconciliation actually is | Position piece, no results | Clinical-side parallel to household medication coordination |
| [74] | Mathur et al., 2022, ASSETS | Support medication management for older adults with mild cognitive impairment | Design of MATCHA, a conversational check-in system, built from exploratory focus groups and design sessions | Older adults with MCI and their care partners | A conversational check-in fits gradual cognitive decline better than habit-formation interventions, because it compensates as ability changes rather than demanding new learning | Prototype-stage evaluation | The nearest system to ours in the medication domain: it checks in with the older adult, and never decides whom else to tell |

**Agreements.** Reminders alone do not produce sustained adherence ([107], [108], [35]). Chronic-illness work is distributed across household members ([92], [120], [102]). The interpretive work of reconciling advice, not remembering, is the hard part ([27], [119]). Where habit formation is the wrong target, a conversational check-in that compensates as ability changes is the published alternative ([74]).

**Conflicts.** [108] finds reminders *impede* habit formation by preventing cue learning; our Study 2 participants describe the opposite ("even before the reminder notification comes I do get a sign"). The difference may be dosage and duration: a 4-week study with healthy participants versus weeks-to-months of real regimen use. The conflict must be reported, not resolved by preference. [54] reports a device strengthening the patient's position in shared decisions, while [24] (Stream 2) reports monitoring systems disempowering the care recipient; sample (self-selected trackers with a single condition versus onboarded monitoring recipients) plausibly explains the split.

**Theories in play.** Routines theory ([92]), habit and cue learning ([108], and [31] in Stream 6), practice theory ([9], [61]).

**Recurring limitations the literature admits.** Short deployments; app-store proxies for use; single-condition samples; almost no work outside high-income settings; near-total absence of the caregiver's own account of the same episode.

**Gap.** *Known:* reminder design, habit dynamics, and that family members do medication work. *Unknown:* how a system that itself decides when to speak, stay silent, or escalate is absorbed into a family's existing division of that work. *Why it matters:* every design recommendation in this stream presumes a system that only informs. *Possible RQ:* how does an agent with initiative change the distribution of medication work inside a household, and who bears the residual labor?

## Stream 2 — Multi-stakeholder care, caregiver-mediated health technology, and monitoring

**What this body of work has established.** CSCW has documented caregiver coordination, information sharing, and monitoring in detail, and it has repeatedly found the same asymmetry: systems are designed for and adopted by the caregiver, while the older adult experiences them as onboarding done to them. Privacy in this stream is interpersonal and negotiated rather than individual and configured. What the stream has not studied is a system that must itself choose whom to inform.

**Agreements.** Care data changes relationships when it becomes visible ([115], [111], [23]). The care recipient's perspective is systematically underrepresented and, when collected, diverges from the caregiver's ([24], [65], [76]). Consent as a one-time configuration misfits domestic care ([50], [122], and [71] in Stream 7).

**Conflicts.** Monitoring reads as care in some accounts ([81], [118]) and as disempowerment in others ([24], [73]). The split tracks who was interviewed: studies centering caregivers and staff report coordination gains, studies centering recipients report loss of standing. This is exactly why our Study 3 narrates the same episode from both sides, and it is the empirical basis for treating checking-as-care as an open question rather than an assumption. Clinical research resolves the same split differently: it maps caregiver involvement across the stages of a treatment decision and treats that involvement as legitimate rather than intrusive ([63]), which is the position HCI's monitoring literature rarely grants.

**Theories in play.** Articulation and invisible work ([87], [47], [106]); Altman's privacy regulation ([23]); relation work ([65]); health equity and community health ([36]); triadic decision-making ([63]).

**Recurring limitations the literature admits.** Caregiver-only or recipient-only samples; short deployments; institutional rather than domestic settings; Western households; systems that report rather than act, so no study can observe an allegiance decision being made.

**Gap.** *Known:* that visibility of care data reorganizes family relations, and that recipients and caregivers read the same system differently. *Unknown:* what happens when the system itself holds the decision about whom to tell, and how a family grants, contests, or withdraws that standing. *Why it matters:* the field's design guidance (more transparency, more control) presumes a human is making the disclosure choice. *Possible RQ:* through what practices does a family assign and revoke a system's disclosure authority, and what makes a shift legitimate?

## Stream 3 — Agent autonomy, delegation, contestability, and AI alignment with plural principals

**What this body of work has established.** Alignment research is explicitly built on a single principal: a human whose preferences the agent infers and serves, with obedience traded off against inferred benefit. A recent and still small literature names this as the field's structural limit. In parallel, HCI has studied delegation, proactivity, oversight, and contestability, almost always with one user facing one system. The intersection with a plural, related principal, a family, is nearly empty, and the two 2026 FAccT papers below are the first records this search found that name it directly.

**Agreements.** Alignment's single-principal assumption is a modeling convenience with normative consequences ([38], [32], [121], [62]). Obedience and benefit trade off ([79], [39]). Proactivity changes what explanation must do ([52], [42], [70]). Contestability must be designed in, not appended ([6], [7], [28]).

**Conflicts.** The formal literature concludes that a *less* obedient agent serves the human better ([79]); the contestability and oversight literature concludes that human intervention must be preserved at every step ([6], [25]). Both are defensible and they pull our veto design in opposite directions. Our graduated veto is a position in that argument and should be presented as such. Independently of that, the decision log is evidence about the agent, not proof of accountability, and the Method should state its limits rather than lean on it.

**Theories in play.** Principal–agent theory ([62], [79]); value alignment taxonomies ([32], [105]); mixed-initiative interaction ([45]); trust calibration ([67]); contestable AI and seamful design ([6], [28]); responsibility gaps ([99]).

**Recurring limitations the literature admits.** Formal work assumes one rational human; empirical work uses vignettes, speculative prototypes, or task settings rather than deployments; where deployments exist they are workplace or public, not domestic; almost no study observes an autonomous decision *and* the affected parties' separate accounts of it.

**Gap.** *Known:* that plural principals break the alignment formalism, and that people calibrate trust and want to contest decisions. *Unknown:* what a plural principal actually does when an agent serving it must choose sides in daily life, and what makes the choice acceptable. *Why it matters:* the conceptual literature has named the structural problem and has no empirical case. *Possible RQ:* which properties of a role change (direction, visibility, revocability, ceremony) determine whether a household treats the agent's new allegiance as legitimate?

## Stream 4 — Global South HCI, Bangladesh, intermediated and proxy technology use

**What this body of work has established.** Two decades of ICTD and HCI4D work has documented that technology use in South Asia is routinely collective: devices are shared, operated by proxy, and mediated by literate or skilled relatives, and privacy is performed inside those relations rather than defended against them. Bangladesh specifically has a substantial HCI literature on shared phones, repair, patriarchal constraint, and religious practice. In all of it the object is a mediated *tool*. None of it studies an agent whose loyalty the household negotiates.

**Conflicts.** [110] shows technology reinforcing subordinate roles while also being used tactically for agency; [33] reads relinquishing control as competent strategy, whereas much aging literature reads it as lost autonomy. Both readings are available for our delegated-dependence tension, and Study 3 has to adjudicate case by case rather than in general. The one deployment in this stream of a system that decides rather than informs went badly for a different reason: rural clinicians adopted an AI decision-support system and then worked around it, so adoption never became use ([114]).

**Theories in play.** Intermediated use ([98], [33]); postcolonial and decolonial computing ([49], [26]); structural analysis of care work ([53]).

**Recurring limitations the literature admits.** Interview and ethnographic snapshots rather than deployments; urban bias; very little eldercare (the Bangladesh HCI corpus is dominated by women, workers, youth, and repair); no agentic systems.

**Gap.** *Known:* how proxy and shared use work in Bangladesh, and that privacy is relationally negotiated there. *Unknown:* how these same relational assets absorb a system that acts on its own initiative and must pick a side. *Why it matters:* the stream's object has always been a passive tool, so its findings do not settle what happens when the tool acquires standing. *Possible RQ:* which existing relational assets (proxy operation, collective decision-making, checking-as-care) does a household recruit when it assigns an agent's allegiance?

## Stream 5 — Aging, dignity, autonomy, interdependence, and assistive technology

**What this body of work has established.** The critical turn in aging HCI has retired the deficit frame: older adults are documented as circumspect, competent, reasoning adopters whose refusals are informative. Accessibility research has replaced independence with interdependence as the design frame. Both moves are directly load-bearing for us, because they make "ceding control to family" describable as agency rather than loss.

## Stream 6 — Gamification, streaks, and motivation as relational rather than behavioral mechanisms

**What this body of work has established.** HCI's gamification literature is large, conceptually loose about what a "game element" is, and increasingly critical of the behaviorist reading of points and streaks. Self-determination theory dominates as the explanatory frame, and the most useful recent work treats shared metrics as social objects that trigger interaction rather than as levers that move behavior. This is a small stream by design: gamification is demoted to one mechanic in our paper.

## Ranked research gaps across streams

1. **No empirical account of a plural principal negotiating an agent's allegiance in daily life.** The conceptual literature has now named the problem precisely ([62], [121], [105]) and the formal literature has established the obedience–benefit trade-off ([79], [39]), but every empirical study found here has either one user ([42], [55]) or a passive multi-stakeholder system ([111], [81]). This is the gap our C1 and C2 occupy, and it is the strongest of the seven because two independent literatures have converged on it from opposite directions.
2. **Multi-stakeholder care research systematically hears one side of each episode.** Caregiver-centered studies report coordination gains and recipient-centered studies report lost standing ([24], [65], [115], [76]), and no study found here narrates the same episode from both sides and checks it against the system's own record. Our Study 3 design is the methodological answer to a documented gap, not merely a thorough protocol, and [56] is the nearest published template for the multi-stakeholder triangulation it requires.
3. **Consent mechanisms for distributed decisions exist in energy, social networks, and games but not in care households.** [71], [109], [83], [104], and [122] supply the model; [50] shows one-time consent fails for older adults specifically. The Affiliation Ledger is the missing care-domain instance.
4. **South Asian aging HCI is thin, and Bangladeshi eldercare HCI is close to absent.** The Bangladesh corpus is strong on shared phones, repair, gendered labor, and religion ([3], [110], [2], [96]) and nearly silent on older adults; the South Asian aging work found here is thin, and what exists sits largely outside HCI ([5], [51], [90]) or studies disability rather than ageing ([86]). Our formative study fills a sampling gap the field's own reviews document ([41]).
5. **Whether delegation can be agency remains contested rather than settled.** [13], [30], [33], and [101] support it; the autonomy-preservation tradition that [66] documents predicts the opposite. Our delegated-dependence tension is a live question in the literature, which is precisely why it needs a named counter-case before it anchors a claim.
6. **Streak loss is documented as relational grief only among adolescents on social media.** [46] and [19] are the evidence base; [68] and [97] show family metrics trigger interaction. Nothing found here studies a shared household score attached to one member's medical regimen, which is what our participant asked for.
7. **Proactivity has outrun explanation.** [52] shows explanation must change form when agents act unprompted, [42] shows disclosure sequencing changes trust, and [28] argues for showing the seams; none of this has been tested where the unprompted act is telling one family member about another.
