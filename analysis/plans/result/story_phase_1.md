# Phase 1 story: from distributed medication work to accountable handoffs

Phase 1 answers RQ1 and provides the formative basis for RQ3 through 24 audited transcripts, comprising 16 older adults and 8 caregivers. No Phase 1 transcript for P14 or household pairing map is filed, so we report convergence across participant roles rather than agreement within any household.

## Theory alignment

Articulation work and invisible work provide the primary frame because they locate medication management in coordination across people, artifacts, and routines [106]. Within that coordination, older adults, kin, pharmacists, doctors, papers, medicine strips, and phones carry differentiated parts of a workable regimen. The logic of care helps us interpret checking and delegated setup as situated care rather than failed individual autonomy [80]. Interdependence and intermediated use also support that interpretation when older adults participate through devices operated with family members [13, 30, 98, 33, 1].

Individual autonomy nevertheless remains the strongest rival because several participants refused help, protected privacy, or asserted unaided competence. This account, however, cannot explain why other participants treated delegated knowledge and family checking as legitimate action within a care network. We therefore use theory to locate work and authority, while leaving each episode's moral meaning open. Theory organizes the question without predetermining whether any episode constitutes care or control, dependence or agency, and we use that discipline to separate observation from interpretation and design opportunity.

## The story in one paragraph

Medication work in these Bangladeshi households belongs neither to an isolated older adult nor to a single caregiver. Older adults often retain taking through familiar strips, places, meals, and embodied repetition, while kin carry interpretation, supply, setup, and checking. Pharmacists translate difficult prescriptions and doctors retain authority over changes, producing an arrangement that works when these differentiated contributions remain aligned. Travel, guests, work, stock depletion, changed packaging, and distance can then separate one task from the person or artifact that usually carries it. Existing aids sometimes provide isolated support, but they rarely support the resulting handoff and may create more coordination work. Participants therefore evaluated these aids and proposed technologies as legitimate when they keep the older adult involved, alert caregivers only about meaningful exceptions, make checking visible, and leave clinical decisions with people. The opportunity is accountable coordination, not a stronger reminder.

## 1. Medication work is distributed through differentiated roles

The first theme concerns differentiated competence rather than individual deficit because P08 separated taking from the knowledge that organizes it: "Taking is easy. Knowing which medicine is little difficult" (P08, Phase 1, L101). Across several older adult accounts, participants recognized tablets through packaging, colour, location, or relation to a meal instead of recalling biomedical names. The same separation appeared when P03 could identify familiar strips but said, "the name I cannot say" (P03, Phase 1, L71), and these locally meaningful cues sustained direct participation.

Other members of the care network hold different knowledge, and P04 explained that division: "My son. He keeps the prescription also. He knows what doctor said" (P04, Phase 1, L51). The same information distribution appeared when C04 knew medicine names that an older adult recognized through packaging. Pharmacists also interpreted handwritten prescriptions, but participants reserved medication changes for doctors. P06 stated that boundary plainly, "Change means doctor has to say" (P06, Phase 1, L103), confirming that medication work is distributed but authority remains differentiated.

This distribution gives the collectivist angle its empirical content. It does not mean that each family member shares every decision or that an older adult relinquishes authority. It means that participation is relationally achieved through differentiated contributions. P10 articulated that orientation directly: "we don't live in isolation. My family ensures my medicine stock is full, and my community friends keep my spirits high. Technology should complement that" (P10, Phase 1, L84). The account is grounded in these Bangladeshi households, with independent management and refusal retained as countercases rather than treated as departures from a cultural rule.

Reminding follows household presence because whoever is nearby can notice and act. P02 assigned this reminder task to "Whoever is around" (P02, Phase 1, L377), and spouses or peers sometimes cued a dose by taking their own medication. This circulation cannot be rewritten as one caregiver remembering for everyone. C01 described a role that "just happened" and became routine (C01, Phase 1, L357), but no second transcript directly supports role acquisition through accretion, so it is retained as a single instance.

Against that caregiver role, independent management defines the theme's boundary rather than undermining the wider pattern. P11, P15, P16, and P17 described using memory, notes, or prescriptions without family intervention, and several rejected a present need for medication technology. Among these independent accounts, P17's statement, "my memory is very sharp" (P17, Phase 1, L27), functions as both a practice report and a competence claim. A design that assumes family intervention is always welcome would violate this evidence because a care network must represent refusal as well as delegation.

Together, these design boundaries and accounts suggest that meaningful participation does not require one person to perform every task. Within these accounts, older adults may retain taking and distribute information work, or they may decide not to distribute it at all. We therefore frame this differentiated information work as a household arrangement rather than a scale from independence to dependence, and we use that arrangement to keep participation and authority analytically distinct.

## 2. Routine transitions expose weak handoffs and uncertain verification

Ordinary medication routines work because spatial, temporal, and social cues remain aligned. P01 described this contrast between stable home cues and disrupted travel: "At home, everything is fixed. I know where the medicine is, I know the time. When travelling, everything changes" (P01, Phase 1, L95). That disruption mechanism also appears when farm work follows variable hours or when guests and delayed meals interrupt an established sequence. Across these accounts, a transition displaces the cue that ordinarily carries the dose.

Transition failure is more precise than memory decline because the corpus contains several mechanisms that remembering alone cannot repair. These mechanisms appeared when older adults left medicine at home, lost access to a familiar package, or exhausted their supply. P08 located one such failure in supply, "We forget to buy before it finishes. Then there is no medicine" (P08, Phase 1, L149), while changed brands disrupted recognition without changing timing knowledge; both transition failures concern availability and interpretation.

Shared care becomes vulnerable when the availability of family members changes at the same time. P13 recalled a departure when family members were present but occupied: "Everybody was busy. Nobody remembered either" (P13, Phase 1, L53). The same family availability problem appears from the caregiver perspective when physical distance produces uncertainty instead of independent evidence about a dose. When calling was impossible, C01 said, "I just have to trust that she will take it. There is no proper way to know" (C01, Phase 1, L109), which leaves an unobserved dose uncertain rather than necessarily missed.

That uncertainty gives checking its moral tension because one person's reassurance can become another person's pressure. C03 located the caregiver's pressure in an absent check: "If nobody checks then I worry" (C03, Phase 1, L49). By contrast, P05 described the older adult side of this pressure as irritation when a daughter asked repeatedly, then interpreted the same questions as help. We cannot classify checking in advance as surveillance or benign care because its meaning depends on who asks, how often they ask, and what consequences follow.

Those possible consequences help explain why caregivers sometimes attached high stakes to these handoffs, and two associated a missed dose with later illness, including one hospital visit (C02, Phase 1, L81 to L89; C03, Phase 1, L31 to L41). We report those accounts as explanations for caregiver concern, not as clinical evidence that a missed dose caused the symptoms. This clinical distinction keeps the perceived stakes visible without making a causal health claim.

The resulting design problem sits at transitions among home and travel, routine and interruption, older adult and caregiver, action and record. More salience cannot repair each transition because several failures occur after a reminder or beyond its reach, which is why the care network needs accountable handoffs.

## 3. Existing aids fit tasks but often miss household handoffs

Existing aids vary in fit because each aid solves one task and can redistribute another. One household abandoned a pillbox when refilling it became an additional task: "We had to put the medicine inside every time and sometimes we forgot to put it there" (C01, Phase 1, L61). A second aid, the generic alarm, produced a different gap by signaling time without identifying the required action, as C04 explained: "It just rings. It does not say what to take" (C04, Phase 1, L93); in both cases, the household still performs the missing articulation work.

Countercases nevertheless matter because low technology practices do not fail uniformly: P12 found a daily alarm helpful, even when taking was deferred after the sound. P16 used notes prepared with a spouse, and C06 reported that alarms and a paper prescription met the household's current needs. These current practices and familiar artifacts support bounded tasks, although their value still depends on the task and handoff at issue.

The fit of these artifacts is further conditioned by device ecologies because some older adults used smartphones directly, some relied on family setup, and others used button phones mainly for calls. Within those phone practices, P09 knew the call controls but not alarm configuration, and P06 stopped when an unintended action opened an unfamiliar screen: "Then I leave it" (P06, Phase 1, L167). We read this withdrawal as patterned participation rather than failed adoption because the person sets an interaction boundary.

Similar interaction boundaries appear around documents because they can provide useful support and still require additional interpretation. Such interpretation affects notes, labels, photographs, paper prescriptions, and familiar packages, which help families organize medication but often move small print or handwriting to kin and pharmacists. A changed package can break visual expertise, but a stable written schedule may sustain independent management. Technology designed for this management should therefore preserve useful artifacts and repair their handoffs instead of replacing the whole ecology.

## 4. Elicited expectations define bounded roles for technology

The Phase 1 guide introduced voice and pictures, prescription reading and reminders, family notifications, and confirmation buttons as proposed features. Participants evaluated these proposed features, so their responses support design expectations rather than spontaneous requirements or evidence of use because no agent had yet joined the care network. We therefore mark every design facing code as elicited, and we retain this evidentiary boundary throughout the story.

Within these elicited expectations, participants often preferred cues that preserved direct older adult participation. One participant, P02, valued voice because it avoided sustained screen attention: "I don't have to look at the phone. I can just hear it" (P02, Phase 1, L273). Other participants evaluated medicine names, pictures, large controls, and simple timing as connections between a prompt and a familiar strip. Yet P02 alone raised a privacy condition for spoken reminders around other people, so voice first design still needs a quieter alternative.

Beyond reminder privacy, participants evaluated escalation as a bounded sequence rather than a default broadcast. Their preferred sequence often gave the older adult a reminder and another opportunity before notifying one caregiver about a meaningful exception. That sequence provides time and information to the older adult, and it gives the caregiver both a reason and a boundary for checking, so each person can interpret who acts and why. P01 limited family knowledge to an exception, saying "if I really don't take it," but "not for every small thing" (P01, Phase 1, L211). That exception boundary also appeared when C02 expected frequent notifications to be ignored and C01 also rejected secret checking. These judgments anticipate direction and visibility, although Phase 1 cannot establish revocation or ceremony through use.

This direction and visibility boundary also separates a system record from the action that it represents. P05 distinguished the medication action from its digital confirmation: "Taking medicine is the main thing. Pressing button should be secondary" (P05, Phase 1, L211). A missing confirmation in the system record must therefore remain uncertain instead of becoming a factual claim about nonadherence. Because caregiver escalation acts on that record, this distinction protects both dignity and evidentiary accuracy.

Setup and decision authority remain distributed because several older adults accepted trusted kin entering schedules, while caregivers preferred one time configuration over daily data entry. That configuration delegation did not authorize technology to change medication, and C04 limited the proposed system to prescribed information rather than recommendations. Trust in that prescribed information depended on human verification for P04, making the design opportunity visible and revocable configuration under continuing clinical authority.

## 5. Design opportunities traced to Phase 1 evidence

We traced each opportunity through current practice, the resulting tension, and an elicited expectation. The table does not claim that any feature was implemented or used.

| Current practice or tension | Elicited expectation | Design opportunity | Boundary carried forward |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meals, places, and familiar strips cue doses | Voice, pictures, large controls, and simple timing | Reinforce embodied cues instead of replacing them with abstract schedules | Support direct older adult use and a private mode in company. |
| Travel, guests, and work displace routines | Reminders that persist across place and allow delay | Design for transitions and interruption recovery | A reminder may be noticed without producing immediate action. |
| Generic alarms do not identify the task | Speak the medicine, dose, and food relation | Make prompts actionable rather than merely salient | Voice preference was often elicited and is not universal. |
| Changed packaging breaks recognition | Show current medicine and mark changes | Link visual recognition to a verified current schedule | Images cannot stand alone because brands and packaging change. |
| Distance creates uncertainty | Retry with the older adult before notifying a caregiver | Escalate exceptions rather than broadcast every event | Nonconfirmation is not proof of a missed dose. |
| Checking can mean care, irritation, or pressure | Visible family access and selective alerts | Make direction and visibility explicit | Older adult consent cannot be inferred from caregiver approval. |
| Kin already configure devices and retain papers | Role specific setup and everyday use | Let trusted kin configure while older adults retain confirmation | Delegated setup must remain inspectable and revocable. |
| Prescriptions are difficult to interpret | Capture, explain, or compare prescribed information | Support document interpretation and human verification | Prescription capture was specified but not implemented. |
| Stock monitoring is distributed | Notify the person who buys before supply ends | Route supply information to the responsible role | Inventory support requires a reliable stock signal. |
| Doctors authorize medicine changes | Information and warnings, not recommendations | Keep clinical authority outside the agent | No autonomous medicine change is legitimate in this evidence. |

## 6. A Bangladesh grounded care network framework

We organize the Phase 1 caregiver technology framework around three differentiated analytic layers because coordination and interpretation are distributed, and this structure keeps participation, accountability, and authority analytically visible. Although each layer addresses a distinct question, the layers connect people and artifacts, participation and authority, so design moves from isolated features toward accountable participation in an existing care network and makes coordination, interpretation, participation, accountability, and authority available for systematic comparison.

| Analytic layer | Question | What Phase 1 contributes |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution of work | Who takes, recognizes, interprets, buys, reminds, verifies, configures, and authorizes? | Roles are differentiated and may move with presence, competence, and context. |
| Conditions of handoff | What changes when time, place, supply, regimen, or availability shifts? | Breakdowns concentrate at transitions between people, artifacts, and routines. |
| Accountability boundary | Who may see, change, escalate, and decide, and how is that authority known? | Checking needs limits, records remain uncertain, and clinicians retain medication authority. |

This framework contributes to Global South HCI because it begins with local care assets and intermediated use instead of presuming an isolated user. Because its empirical claim remains bounded to Bangladesh, only the analytic contrast between single principal systems and distributed care networks may travel. Research elsewhere must test that contrast rather than inherit it as fact.

## 7. Gamification is a Phase 2 question, bounded by Phase 1

Phase 1 did not ask older adults to evaluate streaks, points, scores, badges, rewards, or game-like medication features. We therefore cannot claim a Phase 1 view of gamification. Adding such a preference would turn an absence in the formative corpus into invented evidence.

Phase 1 nevertheless establishes the conditions under which any later gamification mechanic must be interpreted. Older adults place medicine taking above digital confirmation, some participants experience repeated checking as pressure, family involvement is acceptable under limits, and technology is expected to complement relational care. These findings rule out treating a streak as a neutral behavioral lever. Phase 2 must determine from lived accounts whether the deployed streak became encouragement, pressure, competition, loss, or an occasion for connection.

## 8. Bridge to Phase 2

Phase 1 establishes the human affiliation baseline by locating existing assignments, fragile handoffs, and boundaries around hypothetical technology. It does not show families negotiating the agent's allegiance because the agent had not yet been deployed. Phase 2 must therefore examine whether lived interaction preserves or changes those arrangements.

Those tests concern whom the agent addresses first, when caregivers receive exceptions, and whether confirmation remains uncertain rather than accusatory. They also concern whether families can inspect, contest, and revoke delegated setup without turning caregivers into hidden monitors. Phase 1 supplies the analytic terms, and Phase 2 must supply the negotiation.
