# Phase 2 story: the agent that was given its place rather than taking one

Source: the Phase 2 final codebook and themes (`output/codes/A1/final-codebook/phase-2/`), built from 17 older-adult and 8 caregiver post-deployment transcripts after a two-week household deployment. Every quote is verbatim and attributed by id, phase, and line, and every Phase 2 quote is introduced with its evidence grade: lived (done or seen during the deployment), elicited (a position under an offered frame), hypothetical (an answer to a conditional), or believed-capability (a behaviour attributed to the agent that the build record excludes; it grounds no use claim). No household pairing is filed; agreements between roles are across the corpus, not within one family. This narrative answers RQ2 and RQ3 and continues `story_phase_1.md`.

## 1. What Phase 1 predicted and what the deployment did

Phase 1 ended with a specification: an agent that speaks to the older adult in Bangla, reports to one family member only on a real miss after a wait, holds its record below the person's word, is set up by kin, and never decides. The deployed build did most of this: reminders naming the medicine, a family notification on an unconfirmed dose, a spoken announcement of whom the agent served, and a request-and-grant prompt the older adult could decline. Four things it did not do: no risk-graded pushback against a veto, no interpretation of silence, no probationary mode, no shared family score. Phase 2 therefore tests the specification, and the first result is that the households did not negotiate the agent's allegiance so much as inherit it.

## 2. Inherited allegiance: the agent enters family practice

The announcement worked as legibility. Lived: "When it said it is working for me, I understood it better. I knew, okay, this reminder is for me. And when it said it is also working with my family, I understood that also." (P10, Phase 2, L13). Lived, from an older adult who reads it as explaining the family: "if it is working with my family, then I also know why they are getting something." (P13, Phase 2, L12). Its cost was named once and sized small. Lived: "if it says something in front of everyone, then I feel a little, what do you call it, exposed. Not very bad." (P10, Phase 2, L17).

The request-and-grant prompt was lived by six older adults as an episode with the same shape. Lived: "The agent reminded me first. I did not respond because I thought I would take it after finishing the news. Then later it asked for permission to involve the family, something like that. I agreed. After that my son came and asked me if I had taken it. So that was the shift." (P10, Phase 2, L21). The grant is accounted for exactly as Phase 1 predicted, as trust and not lost independence. Elicited: "I still take the medicine myself. My son is not putting the tablet in my mouth. He is just checking. So it felt more like trust." (P10, Phase 2, L25). One grant was a self-assessment of risk. Lived: "I allowed it because I knew I was late and there was a possibility I would forget. My son then checked with me." (P11, Phase 2, L22).

From the caregiver's side the sharing happened through use, not configuration. Lived: "Earlier I was thinking I will set it up for them and then leave it. But after using it, my mother started listening for the reminder and my father also knew that if he does not confirm then I may get a notification. So it became more of a family thing." (C03, Phase 2, L29). An older adult dates it the same way. Lived: "it became part of the routine because my daughter was also checking it." (P03, Phase 2, L29). And in two homes the agent's first user was the caregiver who administers. Lived: "Mostly it reminded me, because I am the one managing their medicines. If I am busy doing household work, the reminder helps me remember that it is time." (C05, Phase 2, L17).

Direction and proportion restated Phase 1's rules. Hypothetical: "Son is enough. Wife may also know because she is at home, but no need to alert everybody." (P04, Phase 2, L69). Direction acquired a reason no Phase 1 account gave. Hypothetical: "One family member is enough. If everyone gets it, then everybody may start asking me. One person can ask me and then it is done." (P13, Phase 2, L130). And three participants named consent where the rest named risk. Hypothetical: "When the person using it agrees. In our case my mother is okay with me knowing if she misses a medicine because I already help her. But I don't think it should automatically tell the whole family everything." (C04, Phase 2, L189).

The tension here is with alignment research rather than with the family: the principal is plural, ordered "for me first, then both", and its first member varies by household. The agent did not decide whom to serve. The household's existing division decided for it.

## 3. Relocated reminding, retained care

What changed is who reminds first. Lived: "Before, someone at home might ask, did you take your medicine. Now the agent does that part first." (P13, Phase 2, L52). From the caregiver's side the night-time loop ended. Lived: "Now the app does the first reminder. So we don't have to keep asking her at night, did you take it, did you take it. If she misses it, then we check." (C04, Phase 2, L85). The husband's question changed its object. Lived: "Earlier my husband would ask directly, did you take it. Now sometimes he asks whether the reminder came." (P05, Phase 2, L67).

What did not change is that asking is care. Elicited: "Sometimes I get irritated when they ask again and again, but I know why they ask. It is because they are worried. The app doesn't replace that feeling." (P05, Phase 2, L75). Elicited: "If my son comes and says, baba, did you take it, that is different from a phone message. It feels more caring." (P10, Phase 2, L57). Lived: "It has reduced the small checking, but it has not reduced the care. My family still looks after me. They buy the medicine, they make sure I have water, they ask about my breathing. The agent is doing one small part." (P10, Phase 2, L61). The caregivers' formula is the same distinction from their seat. Elicited: "Less pressure, not less responsibility. I still want to know if she misses it. But I don't have to remember the exact time myself." (C04, Phase 2, L33). Lived: "I don't keep watching the clock for their medicine. I can focus on my work. If something needs my attention, then I get the notification." (C03, Phase 2, L165). Lived: "it reduces the pressure on the family without removing the family from it." (C03, Phase 2, L205).

The counter-case is the self-manager. Elicited: "I just don't need someone checking every time. If my son asks occasionally, that's fine. But I don't want my medicine routine to become a family project." (P11, Phase 2, L62). Where no kin does medication work, there is no allegiance to negotiate, and the plural principal collapses to the older adult and the doctor.

## 4. Person over record, doctor over agent

The households applied their Phase 1 rule to the agent's record. Lived: "Sometimes I take the medicine and then forget to press the button. So no response does not always mean I did not take it." (P06, Phase 2, L51). One shift was triggered by the record's error. Lived: "I had actually taken it, I just forgot the phone part. Then we understood that the family side can be useful, but the phone does not know everything." (P05, Phase 2, L19). Caregivers turned silence from an assumption into a signal. Lived: "Earlier we might just think he forgot to answer. Now I would treat no response as something to check. Maybe he is busy, maybe he did not hear it, maybe something else happened." (C03, Phase 2, L61). The decline was used on the same ground. Lived: "Sometimes I knew I had taken the medicine but I had not responded to the agent. Then I stopped the family alert because there was no need to worry them." (P10, Phase 2, L31). And the failure participants fear is a worried kin, not a missed alert. Hypothetical: "if it tells my son I missed medicine when I actually took it every day, then he will worry for no reason." (P06, Phase 2, L63).

Eight participants answered probes that presupposed a weakening veto. Believed-capability: "I thought, I already told you, why are you asking again. But then I understood why it was doing that. If I keep saying no and actually I have not taken the medicine, then it becomes dangerous." (P10, Phase 2, L35). The mechanism was not built. This is reasoning about a described design and it may appear in the paper only as such.

Authority did not move. Hypothetical: "The prescription is the authority for me. The system should follow that, not make its own decision." (P15, Phase 2, L43). Hypothetical: "The assistant is for reminding, not for forcing me." (P14, Phase 2, L45). Hypothetical: "The agent can tell me that something changed, but a person should confirm it." (C02, Phase 2, L64). Where the agent's information was wrong it was checked, not obeyed. Lived: "there were times when something did not match what we knew from the prescription. Then I would check it instead of just accepting it." (C03, Phase 2, L49).

The deployment added one tension Phase 1 had only foreseen: the agent's knowledge is only as current as its human updater. Lived: "The agent was still reminding me based on the previous schedule until the change was updated. That was a little confusing. So I checked with my son and then with the doctor." (P11, Phase 2, L70). Lived: "The agent reminded me about the old timing, but then my son checked with the doctor. So in that case it helped us remember what we were doing before, but it did not decide for us." (P10, Phase 2, L65). The consequence both roles draw is a ceremony of change. Elicited: "Because everybody needs to know if the routine changes. Otherwise my wife may remind me about the old one." (P04, Phase 2, L105). Elicited: "If something changes, he should know. Otherwise he may bring the old one." (P08, Phase 2, L113). Caregivers add that a warning must carry its reason. Hypothetical: "it should explain what the problem is. Just giving a big warning without telling me why would not be very helpful." (C01, Phase 2, L36).

## 5. Relational outcomes and borrowed trust

The streak worked as a relational trigger and not as a behavioural lever. Lived: "My daughter was happy. She said, see, you are doing it regularly. My husband also asked how many days it had been. It became a small thing we talked about." (P05, Phase 2, L95). Lived: "My son would say, oh, baba is doing better than all of us." (P10, Phase 2, L71). Its sting was absorbed by the household. Lived: "I was a little upset because I had kept it for many days. But my son said, okay, it happened, take the next one properly. So after that I did not worry too much. If I was alone, maybe I would have felt more annoyed. With family, they made it into a small thing." (P10, Phase 2, L79). More participants read a changed count as something to check than as a loss. Hypothetical: "First I would see if I really missed medicine. Sometimes I may take it but not press something. So don't worry just because the number changed." (P08, Phase 2, L137). Elicited: "Medicine is not a game. If it gives some encouragement, okay." (P08, Phase 2, L149). Sharing is bounded and personal beyond the family. Elicited: "I would not want the score to become another thing they keep checking all the time though." (P10, Phase 2, L83). Elicited: "My medicine is my matter, even if my family knows." (P07, Phase 2, L115). For one caregiver the streak was her own. Lived: "It was satisfying. It showed that I had been keeping up with everything." (C06, Phase 2, L103).

Trust was earned by testing and borrowed from kin. Lived: "I also checked whether the reminders were coming at the right time when I was at home. Then I tested it when I was outside, because that is where my routine usually becomes less regular." (P11, Phase 2, L102). Lived: "For some days I still kept the old written information nearby." (C04, Phase 2, L149). Lived: "If my son puts the medicine and time, then I know it is from my prescription. If the phone itself starts deciding things, no, I would not trust that." (P06, Phase 2, L27). Lived: "Once the reminders were coming at the correct time and my mother was actually responding to them, I started trusting the normal routine more." (C04, Phase 2, L153). Probationary mode was not built; the testing was participant-initiated, which is why this can be claimed as lived.

Memory was reinforced rather than replaced, against Phase 1's competence claims. Elicited: "I still remember the medicine. I have been taking it for many years. It is more like a second memory." (P06, Phase 2, L147). The honest centre admits both. Elicited: "Now I still know the usual time, but I also wait for the reminder sometimes. So maybe I am depending on it more. But at the same time, the repeated timing has made me remember the dose time better. Sometimes I know it is almost time before the phone says anything." (P10, Phase 2, L97). The counter-case stays. Elicited: "But I still forget sometimes. So it has not completely replaced my memory." (P13, Phase 2, L108).

## 6. From findings to the next prototype

| Finding | What it asks of the next build | Source codes |
|---|---|---|
| Allegiance is inherited through kin's checking, and the first principal varies by household | Configure the announcement and the notification path per household, including the caregiver as first user where she administers | It became the family's when my child started checking it; In some homes the agent reminded the caregiver |
| Consent named as the condition for involving family | Make the older adult's agreement an explicit, revisitable setting, not a one-time grant | Only if I agree |
| A missed press is not a missed dose; a false alarm is the failure | Delay and repeat before any family alert; phrase the alert as unconfirmed, not missed; let a person ask | Do not assume a miss; a person should check first; A false alarm worries family for nothing |
| The agent was stale after a prescription change | Treat a change as a household event: mark old as old, announce what changed and why to everyone who reminds or buys | After a change, the agent is wrong until someone updates it; Everyone must be told what changed and why |
| Warnings without reasons are ignored by caregivers | Every escalation carries its reason | A warning must say why |
| The streak's meaning was supplied by the family | Keep the streak private to the family, small, and framed as a record to check; no competition or public sharing | A broken streak means check why; Encouragement yes, pressure no; Family may see the score; nobody outside |
| Trust was earned by testing against the household's own records | Show the source of each reminder (who entered it, from which prescription) so the test is easy to run | I tested it against my routine or my papers; My child set it and checked it, so I trust it |
| Self-managers want no family involvement | Allow a single-principal configuration with the doctor as the only other party | My medicine is not a family project |
| The caregiver's attention is finite | Notify on exception only, through a channel that reaches them at work; never make maintenance another job | The caregiver no longer watches the clock; Wrong information, noise, or extra work ends use |

## 7. Why this is a CHI finding

Phase 2 shows what happens when an agent designed for one principal enters a household whose medication work is already collective. The agent's allegiance was inherited, not decided: it took the one empty seat, the first reminder where nobody was present, became shared through the kin who checked it, and was granted or declined by older adults as acts toward their family. Every bound placed on it restated a rule the family already applied to one another: told of a miss but not watched, the record below the act, the doctor above everyone, a change told to all. The outcomes were relational and small, and the household supplied the meaning that the mechanics lacked. The tool role was granted without condition; the coach role was accepted as small encouragement and refused as pressure; the advocate role was accepted under three triggers, after a first chance, toward one kin, and, for three participants, only with the older adult's consent.

Two findings the design did not anticipate belong in the Discussion: the caregiver as the agent's first principal where she administers, and consent made explicit only when a system had to be configured. The largest caution also belongs there: eight participants narrated as lived a pushback the build did not have, because the guide asked as if it did. The claims are bounded to Bangladesh and to these 25 participants; what travels is the contrast between a single-principal design and a household that renegotiates it into a plural principal along its own division of work.
